\begin{abstract}
The EKG sequence starts $a(1)=1$, $a(2)=2$, and thereafter takes the
least unused positive integer not coprime to its predecessor.
In 2002, Lagarias, Rains and Sloane conjectured
$a(n)=n+n/(3\log n)+o(n/\log n)$ for almost all $n$.
Replacing values $p,3p$ by $2p$ for odd primes $p$ defines $b(n)$;
we prove, at every index $n$,
$b(n)=n+n/(3\log n)+o(n/\log n)$ as $n\to\infty$ ($\log$ natural).
Writing $E(t)$ for the least positive even integer outside
$\{a(1),\ldots,a(t)\}$, Haver reduced its asymptotic to counting used odd values above $E(t)$
other than $3p$ ($p$ odd prime), and proved the sharp upper bound
$E(t)-t\leq(1/3+o(1))t/\log t$; we prove the lower half and passage to all indices.
For $x=E(t)$ as $t\to\infty$, every $a(i)$ ($1\leq i\leq t$) other than
$3p$ ($p$ odd prime) is at most $x+o(x/\log x)$, and every unused composite
$h<x$ satisfies $x-h=o(x/\log x)$ uniformly in $h$.
The proofs inject selected neighbors of even terms into prime cofactors
whose positive density in a common interval contradicts an elementary sieve bound.
Haver's counting identity and the prime number theorem yield $1/3$.
The complete Lean formalization imports Haver's estimates and the prime
number theorem as proved theorems, and also checks the following
consequences. We give, to our knowledge, the first proofs that
$a(n)>n$ for all large $n$ with $a(n)$ not prime and that
$\#\{k\leq N:a(k)>k\}/N\to1$ (Ordowski's 2009 conjectures in OEIS
A064413), and that each of $a(n)=n$, $2a(n)=n$ and $2a(n)=3n$ has only
finitely many solutions (Jasinski's 2008 conjectures in A152458 and
A152519). Also $\#\{k\leq N:a(k)<k\}\sim N/(2\log N)$, and the
permutation has only finitely many cycles of each bounded length.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The EKG sequence, first studied by Lagarias, Rains, and Sloane
\cite{lrs}, starts with $1,2$ and then chooses the least unused positive
integer having a common factor with the preceding term. It is
\href{https://oeis.org/A064413}{A064413} in the On-Line Encyclopedia
of Integer Sequences \cite{oeis}. Write $\U_t=\{a(1),\ldots,a(t)\}$.
The rule is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
 a(1)=1,\quad a(2)=2,\quad
 a(n)=\min\{m\geq 1:m\notin\U_{n-1},\
                         \gcd(m,a(n-1))>1\}\quad(n\geq3).
\end{equation}
Its first terms are $1,2,4,6,3,9,12,8,10,5,15,18,14,7,21$.
We use LRS numbering from the arXiv text; in the journal version their
Conjectures 1--3 are Conjectures 4.1--4.3.
Lagarias, Rains, and Sloane prove the permutation property, increasing
order of the primes (their Lemma 2), and linear upper and lower bounds.
Their numerical experiments suggested three branches: ordinary values
near $n$, primes near $n/2$, and three times primes near $3n/2$.
Their PDF abstract says, exactly, ``We conjecture that almost all
$a(n)$ satisfy the asymptotic formula'' and displays
\[
 a(n)=n(1+1/(3\log n))+o(n/\log n)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.
\]
Their Conjecture 2 defines $\sim$ as a ratio tending to $1$ and gives
these exact mathematical expressions, with the indicated conditions:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
 a(n)\sim n[1+1/(3\log n)],&a(n)\ne p,3p\ (p\text{ prime}),\\
 a(n)\sim \tfrac12 n[1+1/(3\log n)],&a(n)=p\ (p\text{ prime}),\\
 a(n)\sim \tfrac32 n[1+1/(3\log n)],&a(n)=3p\ (p\text{ prime}).
\end{array}
\]
Thus the displayed Conjecture 2, read literally, asks only for three
ratio limits. We interpret its intended second-order content in the
stronger additive sense stated in their PDF abstract. The arXiv web
abstract has different parentheses; the PDF is the source here.

Hofman and Pilipczuk \cite[Theorem 4.3]{hp} prove that every odd prime
$p$ is preceded by $2p$; the following $3p$ is the consequence already
noted in LRS Conjecture 1. Their proof uses the LRS computation for
$p\leq25000$; Haver's proof reduces this finite range \cite{haver}.
Hofman and Pilipczuk also prove the three ratio limits in LRS Conjecture 2:
their Theorem 4.17 gives $a'_n=n+O(n/\log\log\log n)$.
Their $a'_n$ also replaces the prime $2$, so it agrees with our $b(n)$
only up to finitely many indices and yields the same asymptotic bound.
Introducing Conjecture 5.1, they call the LRS formula the ``stronger
conjecture'', supporting the additive second-order reading above.
Haver \cite{haver} proves the
stronger $b(n)=n+O(n/\log n)$ and quantitative estimates for early even
values, used values above the least unused even integer, and missing
composites below it. Haver's Corollary 2(e), in version 3 dated October 4,
2026, reduces the second-order assertion at these even integers to a
negligible count of odd used values above them. Haver's Theorem 1(d)
(also stated in his abstract) and Corollary 12(d) already give
$E(t)-t\leq(1/3+o(1))t/\log t$ and
$N_p\geq2p-(2/3+o(1))p/\log p$, with sharp constants.
Our contribution is the lower half of the clock,
$E(t)-t\geq(1/3-o(1))t/\log t$, and the all-index transfer.
We prove the remaining hypothesis in Haver's Corollary 2(e), and a
uniform bound for missing composites needed for that transfer.
The reduction and the estimates it uses are Haver's.

For comparison with \eqref{eq:rule}, the Lean construction in
\path{EKG/Canonical.lean} is transliterated below (\texttt{Nat} denotes the
natural numbers). It is zero-based internally; \texttt{chooseNext}

    (actualA (n-1))

\end{verbatim}
The one-based interface in \path{EKG/EndpointDefinitions.lean} uses\\

We use the smoothing convention of LRS Figure 5 \cite{lrs}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smooth}
 b(n)=s(a(n)),\qquad
 s(m)=\begin{cases}
  2p,&m=p\text{ or }m=3p\text{ for an odd prime }p,\\
  m,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In particular, $s(2)=2$ and $s(6)=6$. OEIS A256417 includes the prime
$2$ in both replacements \cite{oeis}; it differs from $b$ only at
indices $2,4$, where our values are $2,6$ and its values are $4,4$.
Here is the corresponding Lean definition, transliterated from
\path{EKG/EndpointDefinitions.lean}, using ASCII type names:
\begin{verbatim}
def smoothValue (m : Nat) : Nat :=
  if And m.Prime (Not (m = 2)) then 2 * m
  else if And (m / 3).Prime
      (And (Not (m / 3 = 2)) (m = 3 * (m / 3))) then
    2 * (m / 3)
  else m
noncomputable def b (n : Nat) : Nat := smoothValue (a n)
\end{verbatim}
Here \texttt{And} and \texttt{Not} are Lean's logical constructors;
the distributed source uses the usual symbolic notation. All logarithms are natural.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For the sequence defined by \eqref{eq:rule} and \eqref{eq:smooth},
\[
 b(n)=n+\frac{n}{3\log n}+o\!\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)
 \qquad(n\longrightarrow\infty).
\]
Precisely, for every real $\eps>0$ there is an $N\in\N$, $N\geq3$,
such that for every $n\in\N$ with $n\geq N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:epsilon}
 \left|b(n)-n-\frac{n}{3\log n}\right|
       \leq \eps\frac{n}{\log n}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The Lean type, transliterated using \texttt{abs}, \texttt{And} and \texttt{Exists}
for the usual logical notation and opening \texttt{EKG.CanonicalEndpoint},
is
\begin{verbatim}
theorem ekg_sharp_all_index :
  forall eps : Real, 0 < eps ->
    Exists (fun N : Nat => And (3 <= N)
      (forall n : Nat, N <= n ->
        abs ((b n : Real) - (n : Real) -
          (n : Real) / (3 * Real.log (n : Real))) <=
        eps * ((n : Real) / Real.log (n : Real))))
\end{verbatim}
The theorem has no sequence-estimate hypotheses. The separate statement
in \path{Statement.lean} and its type check in \path{Check.lean} fix
exactly these quantifiers and casts.

\begin{corollary}[The three branches]\label{cor:branches}
Along the indices of each indicated kind, as $n\to\infty$,
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
 a(n)=n+\dfrac{n}{3\log n}+o(n/\log n),&
       a(n)\ne p,3p\quad(p\text{ odd prime}),\\[4pt]
 p=\dfrac n2+\dfrac{n}{6\log n}+o(n/\log n),&a(n)=p,\\[4pt]
 3p=\dfrac{3n}{2}+\dfrac{n}{2\log n}+o(n/\log n),&a(n)=3p.
\end{array}
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In the three cases $a(n)=b(n)$, $a(n)=b(n)/2$, and
$a(n)=3b(n)/2$, respectively. Multiply Theorem~\ref{thm:main}
by the fixed factor. The finitely many cases involving $2$ do not
matter for any limit.
\end{proof}
This proves the additive second-order interpretation, and in particular
implies the literal Conjecture 2, whose ratio limits were already proved
by Hofman and Pilipczuk. Among the three numbered LRS conjectures, Conjecture 3 remains open:
$13n/28\leq a(n)\leq12n/7$ globally, with equality only at $n=28$
and $n=7$, respectively. It is not settled here; an eventual asymptotic
does not settle the required finite range. Nor do we address the
unproved distributional suggestions for higher-order fluctuations in
LRS Section 4 or their unnumbered cycle questions, beyond the bounded
cycle lengths of Section~\ref{sec:consequences}.

Section~\ref{sec:consequences} derives three consequences. For all
large $n$, $a(n)>n$ exactly when $a(n)$ is composite, and
$\#\{k\leq N:a(k)<k\}\sim N/(2\log N)$; this proves two 2009
conjectures of Thomas Ordowski recorded in OEIS A064413. The equations
$a(k)=k$, $2a(k)=k$ and $2a(k)=3k$ have finitely many solutions; this
proves Artur Jasinski's 2008 finiteness conjectures on A152458 and
A152519. And the permutation has finitely many cycles of each bounded
length.

The coefficient has the counting heuristic of LRS Section 4 \cite{lrs}:
\[
 n\approx x-\frac{x}{2\log x}+\frac{x}{6\log x}
       =x-\frac{x}{3\log x}.
\]
Our new step upgrades two $O(x/\log x)$ displacement bounds to
little-$o$. For the upper bound, the old greedy inequality is
$D\leq2rk(t)$, with $r\leq\sqrt{e+D}$ and
$k(t)\leq K_0\sqrt e/\log e$. Multiplying these estimates leaves a
fixed coefficient of $e/\log e$; it does not force that coefficient
to vanish. For the lower bound,
$D\leq2\spf(h)F(e)$ and $\spf(h)\leq\sqrt e$ have the same loss.
Haver already identifies these rough-predecessor and rough-missing-composite
obstructions in the remark after Corollary 12 \cite{haver}.
The additional information is sparsity among the forced prime cofactors. Given a putative displacement $D\geq\delta x/\log x$, we
instead select multiples $2rMj$ and force their distinct neighbors
to be products $rq_j$ with $q_j$ prime. There are at least
$D/(4rM)-O(1)$ such cofactors in an interval of length
$O(x/(r\log x))$. For each fixed $\delta>0$ their density is bounded
below by a positive constant independent of $x$. It need not be
independent of $\delta$: $M$ also depends on $\delta$. The sieve bound
permits an arbitrarily small fixed upper density, chosen after $\delta$
and $M$, which is exactly what the little-$o$ conclusion needs.

The two estimates are an upper bound for the largest ordinary used
value above the least unused even integer, and a lower bound on every
unused composite below it. Sections~\ref{sec:upper} and
\ref{sec:lower} prove them, then exact counting and the prime number
theorem give the result. We extract no explicit remainder rate.
The nonquantitative step is the choice of a fixed sieve modulus for
each $\delta$, followed by unspecified eventual cutoffs in localization
and the qualitative prime number theorem. This is not a claim of an
intrinsically ineffective obstruction: the sieve constants are
computable, but this proof does not supply a function $N(\eps)$.
An explicit rate looks within reach using Brun--Titchmarsh in place
of the fixed-modulus sieve; this is left for future work.

\section{Frontiers and established estimates}\label{sec:inputs}

Write $L(x)=x/\log x$ for $x>1$, and let
$\U_t=\{a(1),\ldots,a(t)\}$.
Let $E(t)$ be the least positive even integer outside $\U_t$.
Call a value $3p$, for an odd prime $p$, a \emph{tick}, and put
\[
 T(t)=\max\{a(i):1\leq i\leq t,\ a(i)\text{ is not a tick}\},
 \qquad K(t)=\#\{m\in\U_t:2\mid m,\ m>E(t)\}.
\]
The frontier $E(t)$ is nondecreasing and tends to infinity, by the
permutation property. Here and below, $\pi(x)$ counts primes at most
the real number $x$, and $\spf(h)$ is the least prime factor of $h$.
We use the following previously established estimates.

\begin{proposition}[Established sequence estimates]\label{prop:inputs}
There are fixed constants $C\geq1$ and $C_1>0$ such that, eventually,
writing $e=E(t)$ and $k(t)=K(t)+1$,
\begin{align}
 |b(n)-n|&\leq C L(n),\label{eq:oldrate}\\
 k(t)&\leq K_0\frac{\sqrt e}{\log e},\qquad K_0=793,\label{eq:early}\\
 T(t)-e&\leq C_1 L(e).\label{eq:oldspan}
\end{align}
If $t\geq9$ and a composite $15\leq h<e$ is outside $\U_t$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oldhole}
 e-h\leq2\spf(h)\bigl(13\pi(\sqrt h)+724\bigr).
\end{equation}
For all sufficiently large $e$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F}
 F(e):=13\pi(\sqrt e)+724\leq B\frac{\sqrt e}{\log e},
 \qquad B=802.
\end{equation}
Every composite below $15$ is in $\U_{13}$.
For each odd prime $p$, its first multiple occurs at an index $N_p$,
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
 a(N_p)=2p=E(N_p-1),\quad a(N_p+1)=p,\quad a(N_p+2)=3p.
\end{equation}
Distinct introduction indices $N_p$ are at least four apart.
\end{proposition}

These are results about the actual greedy sequence, not hypotheses
added to Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The prime pattern goes back to
Hofman and Pilipczuk \cite{hp}; we use its introduction formulation
as proved in Haver's archive \cite{haver}.
The early-even bound follows from Haver's bound
$K(t)\leq12\pi(\sqrt e)+720$ and elementary prime estimates.
One may take $C_1=4\cdot793^2+2\cdot793$ in \eqref{eq:oldspan}.
The printed sources of \eqref{eq:oldhole} and \eqref{eq:oldrate}
are Haver's Theorem 11 and Corollary 12(c), respectively.
The constants in \eqref{eq:early}--\eqref{eq:F} are convenient
weakenings proved in the accompanying sources, rather than optimized
constants: $793=12\cdot6+721$ and $802=13\cdot6+724$ follow from the
eventual bound $\pi(\sqrt e)\leq6\sqrt e/\log e$ and
$\sqrt e/\log e\geq1$, absorbing the additive constants.
The prime estimate in \eqref{eq:F} does not require the
prime number theorem.

\section{Greedy bounds and interval prime capacity}\label{sec:elementary}

If $q$ is any prime divisor of $a(j-1)$, every positive multiple of $q$
smaller than $a(j)$ was used before index $j$; otherwise the greedy
choice would be smaller. Consequently, at any observation time $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:greedy}
 a(j)\leq E(t)+2qk(t)\qquad(3\leq j\leq t+1).
\end{equation}
Indeed, all multiples of $2q$ strictly between $E(t)$ and $a(j)$
are used even values above $E(t)$, and there are at least
$(a(j)-E(t))/(2q)-1$ of them. Their number is at most $K(t)$.
Notice that \eqref{eq:greedy} holds for every prime divisor $q$,
at the same observation time, even if $j$ is much earlier than $t$.

The following elementary fixed-modulus sieve estimate is included with
its proof; compare the sieve of Eratosthenes in \cite{sievebook}.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:capacity}
For every $\eta>0$ there is a constant $C_\eta$ such that every
closed real interval of length $z\geq0$ contains at most
$\eta z+C_\eta$ primes.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a positive integer $Z\geq2$ sufficiently large, and put
$W=\prod_{p\leq Z}p$. Unique factorization and geometric series give
\[
 \frac{W}{\varphi(W)}
 =\prod_{p\leq Z}(1-1/p)^{-1}
 \geq\sum_{m=1}^Z\frac1m\geq\log(Z+1).
\]
We may therefore choose $Z$ with $\varphi(W)/W<\eta$.
A residue class modulo $W$ has at most $z/W+1$ members in such
an interval. Primes not dividing $W$ lie in its $\varphi(W)$ reduced
residue classes, and at most $\omega(W)$ primes divide $W$.
Thus $C_\eta=\varphi(W)+\omega(W)$ works. Both constants are fixed
before the interval varies.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Forced common factor]\label{lem:factor}
Let $x,\beta>0$, let $r$ be prime, and let $M\geq1$ be an integer.
Suppose every prime divisor of $2M$ is smaller than $\beta\sqrt x$,
and $(\beta\sqrt x)^3>2x$. For selected positive integers
$j<\beta\sqrt x$, put $y_j=2rMj$. Suppose the distinct integers
$d_j$ are composite, satisfy $d_j<2x$, have every prime divisor at
least $\beta\sqrt x$, and satisfy $\gcd(d_j,y_j)>1$.
Then $d_j=rq_j$ for distinct primes $q_j$. If all $d_j$ belong to
an interval $I$, the $q_j$ belong to the interval $I/r$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose a prime $\ell$ dividing both $d_j$ and $2rMj$.
Every prime divisor of $j$ is at most $j<\beta\sqrt x$; every prime
divisor of $2M$ is smaller than that bound by assumption.
Since all prime divisors of $d_j$ are at least $\beta\sqrt x$,
$\ell$ divides neither $j$ nor $2M$. Euclid's lemma forces
$\ell\mid r$, so $\ell=r$. A composite $d_j$ has at least two
prime factors counted with multiplicity. It cannot have three,
since their product would be at least $(\beta\sqrt x)^3>2x>d_j$.
It therefore has exactly two, one of which is $r$; the other is a
prime $q_j$, with $q_j=r$ allowed. Distinct $d_j$ give distinct
$q_j$ by cancellation of the fixed positive integer $r$.
Division by $r$ gives the interval assertion.
\end{proof}

We also record how the old rate localizes adjacent ordinary values.
If $y=a(j)$ is unchanged by smoothing and $e/2<y<2e$, then, uniformly
as $e\to\infty$, the occurrence index $j$ tends to infinity.
To see this, choose any fixed index cutoff and exceed the maximum
of the finitely many preceding values. Equation~\eqref{eq:oldrate}
then gives $j/2\leq y\leq3j/2$ and hence $e/3<j<4e$.
If an adjacent value $d=a(j-1)$ or $a(j+1)$ is also unchanged by
smoothing, the old rate at both indices gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjacent}
 |d-y|\leq C L(j)+C L(j\pm1)+1=O(L(e)).
\end{equation}
The constant is uniform. This uses only the old big-$O$ rate,
not the sharp clock we will prove.

\section{The upper excursion}\label{sec:upper}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:upper}
As $t\to\infty$,
$\pos{T(t)-E(t)}=o(L(E(t)))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $\delta>0$. All constants in this proof are chosen before the
final threshold on $t$. Write $e=E(t)$, $v=T(t)$, and suppose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upperbad}
 D=v-e\geq\delta L(e).
\end{equation}
The old span implies $v<2e$ for sufficiently large $e$.
Choose $i\leq t$ with $a(i)=v$, and let $c=a(i-1)$.
The predecessor is not even: the unused even $e$ would then be an
eligible choice smaller than $v$. It is not $1$, which appears
only at the first index, and it is not an odd prime, since $v$
would then be a tick. If it were a tick, \eqref{eq:greedy} with
$q=3$ would give $D\leq6k(t)=o(L(e))$, a contradiction.
Thus $c$ is an odd composite non-tick, so $c\leq v$.
For $r=\spf(c)$, \eqref{eq:greedy} and \eqref{eq:early} imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r}
 \alpha\sqrt e\leq r\leq\sqrt v<\sqrt{2e},
 \qquad \alpha=\frac{\delta}{2B}.
\end{equation}
Here $B\geq K_0$, so the displayed lower bound is a weakening
of $r\geq\delta\sqrt e/(2K_0)$.

Set $\beta=\delta/(4K_0)$, and fix an integer
$M\geq2$ with $M>1/(\alpha\beta)$.
Consider
\[
 Y=\{2rMj:j\in\N,\ j\geq1,\ e+D/2<2rMj<v\}.
\]
Every member of $Y$ is a multiple of $r$ smaller than $v$, so it
occurred before $i$. Counting a lattice in an open interval gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Yupper}
 |Y|\geq\frac{D}{4rM}-2.
\end{equation}
For $y=a(i_y)\in Y$, put $d_y=a(i_y-1)$.
For every prime $q\mid d_y$, the common observation-time
inequality \eqref{eq:greedy} yields
\[
 q>\frac{D}{4k(t)}\geq\beta\sqrt e.
\]
For large $e$ this excludes $2$ and $3$. The predecessor is not
$1$, a prime, or a tick: in the prime case its successor would
be odd, whereas $y$ is even. Therefore $d_y$ is an odd composite
non-tick, and $d_y\leq v<2e$.
For large $e$, $(\beta\sqrt e)^3>2e$.

Write $y=r(2Mj)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:r} gives
\[
 j<\frac{\sqrt e}{\alpha M}<\beta\sqrt e.
\]
For large $e$, every prime factor of the fixed integer $2M$ is
also smaller than $\beta\sqrt e$. Consecutive values $d_y,y$
share a prime, and distinct selected values have distinct predecessor
indices and hence distinct predecessors. Lemma~\ref{lem:factor}
therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:du}
 d_y=rq_y\quad\text{for distinct primes }q_y.
\end{equation}

Both $y$ and $d_y$ are unchanged by smoothing.
The finite-prefix argument preceding \eqref{eq:adjacent} applies
uniformly to their indices. Since
$e<y<v\leq e+C_1L(e)$, it gives, for a fixed $A>0$,
\[
 e-AL(e)\leq d_y\leq e+AL(e).
\]
For example, $A=C_1+8C+1$ suffices.
Distinct $y$ have distinct occurrence indices, distinct predecessor
indices, and hence distinct $d_y$. Thus \eqref{eq:du} injects $Y$
into the primes in
$[(e-AL(e))/r,(e+AL(e))/r]$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:capacity},
\[
 \frac{D}{4rM}-2\leq\eta\frac{2AL(e)}r+C_\eta.
\]
Choose $\eta=\delta/(32AM)$ after fixing $M$. It follows that
\[
 D\leq\frac\delta4 L(e)+4\sqrt2 M(C_\eta+2)\sqrt e.
\]
Since $\sqrt e=o(L(e))$, the right side is less than
$\delta L(e)$ for sufficiently large $e$, contradicting
\eqref{eq:upperbad}. The frontier tends to infinity, so one threshold
on $t$ works for every later prefix. This proves the proposition.
\end{proof}

\section{Missing composites}\label{sec:lower}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lower}
For every $\delta>0$ there is $t_0$ such that, for all $t\geq t_0$
and every composite $h<E(t)$ outside $\U_t$,
$E(t)-h<\delta L(E(t))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $\delta>0$, put $e=E(t)$, and suppose such a missing $h$ has
$D=e-h\geq\delta L(e)$.
Take $t\geq13$. Then $h\geq15$, and $h$ is odd because every
positive even below $e$ is used. Put $s=\spf(h)$.
Equations~\eqref{eq:oldhole} and \eqref{eq:F} imply
\begin{equation}\label{eq:roughh}
 D\leq2sF(e)\leq2B L(e),\qquad
 \alpha\sqrt e\leq s<\sqrt e,\quad \alpha=\frac\delta{2B}.
\end{equation}
Set $\beta=\delta/(6B)$ and fix $M\geq2$ with
$M>1/(2\alpha\beta)$.
Select the even multiples $y=2sMj$ in $(e-D/2,e)$.
They all belong to $\U_t$. Discard $a(t)$ if it belongs to this
set, losing at most one value in total, and call the resulting set $Y$.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ylower}
 |Y|\geq\frac{D}{4sM}-3.
\end{equation}
Each $y=a(i_y)$ has a successor $u_y=a(i_y+1)$ with $i_y+1\leq t$.
The value $h$ is still unused and eligible after $y$, so
$u_y\leq h$ by the greedy rule. Equality is impossible since $h$
is missing at time $t$. Thus $u_y<h$.

By \eqref{eq:roughh}, $y>e-BL(e)>e/2$ for large $e$.
The old rate and a finite-prefix maximum force all $i_y$ beyond
any fixed cutoff, uniformly in $y$; in particular they exceed the
cutoff for Proposition~\ref{prop:upper} with parameter $\delta/6$.
For $f_y=E(i_y)\leq e$, that proposition gives
\[
 f_y\geq y-\frac\delta6 L(f_y)
       \geq y-\frac\delta6 L(e).
\]
We used that $L$ is increasing for arguments at least $3$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gapfy}
 f_y-u_y>\frac D2-\frac\delta6L(e)
                  \geq\frac\delta3L(e).
\end{equation}
These are post-occurrence frontiers. Their divergence follows from
the uniform divergence of the indices $i_y$.

The successor $u=u_y$ is unused at time $i_y$ and below $f_y$,
so it is not even. It is not an odd prime $p$: its predecessor
would be $2p$, whereas $y/2=sMj$ is composite. It is not a tick,
whose predecessor is odd, and it is not $1$.
It is therefore an ordinary odd composite.
Both $y$ and $u$ are unchanged by smoothing. Their occurrence
indices satisfy $e/4<i_y<2e$ for large $e$, by the old rate.
Applying that rate at $i_y,i_y+1$ gives
\[
 |u-y|\leq(6C+1)L(e),\qquad
 e-A_B L(e)<u<e,\quad A_B=B+6C+1.
\]
In particular $u\geq15$ for sufficiently large $e$, before we
apply the missing-composite bound to it.
Combining \eqref{eq:oldhole}, now at time $i_y$, with
\eqref{eq:gapfy} yields
\[
 \frac\delta3 L(e)<f_y-u\leq2\spf(u)F(e),
 \qquad \spf(u)>\beta\sqrt e.
\]

Every prime factor of $u$ is large, while
\[
 j<\frac{\sqrt e}{2\alpha M}<\beta\sqrt e,
\]
and every prime factor of the fixed integer $2M$ is smaller than
$\beta\sqrt e$ eventually. Since $u<e<(\beta\sqrt e)^3$ eventually, Lemma~\ref{lem:factor}
gives $u=sq_y$ with $q_y$ prime. This does not assert that
$s=\spf(u)$.

Distinct selected $y$ have distinct successor indices and values,
so the $q_y$ are distinct. They lie in
$((e-A_BL(e))/s,e/s)$, an interval of length $A_BL(e)/s$.
By \eqref{eq:Ylower} and Lemma~\ref{lem:capacity},
\[
 \frac{D}{4sM}-3\leq\eta\frac{A_BL(e)}s+C_\eta.
\]
Fix $\eta=\delta/(16MA_B)$. Since $s<\sqrt e$,
\[
 D\leq\frac\delta4L(e)+4M(C_\eta+3)\sqrt e
                           <\delta L(e)
\]
for sufficiently large $e$, a contradiction.
The choices have the order $\delta$, the upper-excursion cutoff,
$M$, $\eta$, $C_\eta$, and finally the frontier and index cutoffs.
All are uniform in the missing composite $h$ and the selected $y$.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact counting and the frontier clock}\label{sec:clock}

We give the boundary terms explicitly, since the main coefficient
arises from a difference of prime counts. Fix $t\geq9$ with
$e=E(t)\geq14$, and put
\begin{align*}
 H&=\#\{h<e:h\text{ composite},\ h\notin\U_t\},\\
 O&=\#\{w\in\U_t:w>e,\ w\text{ odd and not a tick}\},\\
 P_1&=\#\{p\text{ prime}:e/2\leq p<e\},\\
 P_2&=\#\{p\text{ prime}:e/3<p<e/2\},\\
 \theta_1&=\ind{\exists\text{ odd prime }p<e/2\text{ with }N_p=t},\\
 \theta_2&=\#\{p\text{ prime}:e/3<p<e/2,\ N_p\in\{t-1,t\}\}.
\end{align*}
Let $\theta=\theta_1+\theta_2$; the spacing of introduction indices
gives $0\leq\theta\leq2$.

For $p<e/2$, $2p<e$ is already used, so \eqref{eq:block}
shows that $p$ is missing precisely when $N_p=t$.
For $e/2\leq p<e$, $2p$ has not been used, so $p$ is missing.
For the first assertion the prime $2$ is already used and is excluded.
For the second, a used $2p\geq e$ would have entered at a frontier
$2p=E(N_p-1)\leq e$, forcing the impossible used value $e$.
There are therefore $H+P_1+\theta_1$ missing positive integers
below $e$. The used ticks above $e$ are exactly those indexed by
$P_2$, except for the $\theta_2$ unfinished blocks.
No used prime lies above $e$. Counting the $t$ used values below
and above the unused value $e$ gives
\[
 t=(e-1)-H-P_1-\theta_1+K+O+P_2-\theta_2.
\]
Write $I=\ind{e/2\text{ prime}}$. Since $e$ is even and at least $14$,
\[
 P_1=\pi(e)-\pi(e/2)+I,\qquad
 P_2=\pi(e/2)-I-\pi(e/3).
\]
We obtain the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clockexact}
 \begin{split}
 e-t={}&\pi(e)-2\pi(e/2)+\pi(e/3)\\
       &+H-K-O+1+\theta+2I.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Haver proves this counting identity in Corollary 12(b).
Haver's Lean archive already proves \path{clock_balance},
\path{clock_identity}, \path{clock_identity_with_ticks}, and
\path{clock_identity_from_interval_counts}; the last takes the
endpoint errors as hypotheses. We discharge those hypotheses by proving
that the missing-prime count is $P_1+\theta_1$ and the tick count is
$P_2-\theta_2$, and by evaluating the interval endpoints through $I$
and $\theta$ as above.

The old missing-composite estimate suffices to count holes.
For $t\geq13$, classify a missing composite by $s=\spf(h)$.
By \eqref{eq:oldhole}, it is an odd multiple of the odd prime $s$
in $[e-2sF(e),e)$. There are at most $F(e)+1$ such multiples,
because their spacing is $2s$. Hence
\[
 H\leq\pi(\sqrt e)(13\pi(\sqrt e)+725)
             =O(e/\log^2 e)=o(L(e)).
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:early} gives $K=o(L(e))$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:upper} gives
$O\leq\pos{T(t)-e}/2+1=o(L(e))$.
The correction $1+\theta+2I$ is between $1$ and $5$.

The prime number theorem, in the form $\pi(x)\sim x/\log x$
\cite{hadamard,poussin}, implies for each fixed $c>0$ that
$\pi(ce)/L(e)\to c$. Thus
\[
 \frac{\pi(e)-2\pi(e/2)+\pi(e/3)}{L(e)}
            \longrightarrow 1-2\cdot\frac12+\frac13=\frac13.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:clockexact} yields
$e-t=L(e)/3+o(L(e))$.
Since $L(e)=o(e)$ and $e\to\infty$, this implies $e\sim t$,
and consequently $L(e)\sim L(t)$. We have proved
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clock}
 E(t)=t+\frac13 L(t)+o(L(t)).
\end{equation}
The bound $O=o(L(e))$ proves the remaining hypothesis of the equivalence
stated in Haver's Corollary 2(e) and proved in Corollary 12(d);
the counting criterion itself is not new here.

\section{Passage to every index}\label{sec:transfer}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
First suppose $x=a(n)$ is ordinary, at a sufficiently large index,
and use the pre-occurrence frontier $f=E(n-1)$.
If $x<f$, then $x$ is odd, unused at time $n-1$, and neither $1$
nor a prime, so Proposition~\ref{prop:lower} gives
$f-x=o(L(f))$, uniformly over these occurrences.
If $x=f$, the discrepancy is zero.
If $x>f$, the unused even $f$ remains unused at time $n$, whence
$E(n)=f$. Now $x\leq T(n)$ and
Proposition~\ref{prop:upper} gives $x-f=o(L(f))$.
All three cases therefore give
\[
 a(n)=E(n-1)+o(L(E(n-1))).
\]
By \eqref{eq:clock}, $E(n-1)\sim n$, $L(E(n-1))\sim L(n)$,
and $E(n-1)=n+L(n)/3+o(L(n))$. This proves the desired formula
at all ordinary positions. The use of the pre-occurrence frontier
also covers a step at which the frontier jumps.

For a prime block with introduction index $m=N_p$, all three
smoothed values equal $2p=E(m-1)$.
For $n=m,m+1,m+2$, the index $m-1$ is one of $n-1,n-2,n-3$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:clock} is unchanged on the $L(n)$ scale by any
of these bounded shifts, since $L(n-j)/L(n)\to1$ and
$j=o(L(n))$. The formula follows at the prime and tick positions too.

For completeness, the uniform little-$o$ conclusions here give exactly
the quantifiers in \eqref{eq:epsilon}. Given $\eps>0$, apply the two
excursion propositions with $\delta=\eps/8$. Eventually
$L(E(n-1))\leq2L(n)$, so their contribution is at most
$\eps L(n)/4$. Take a common cutoff for the clock estimates shifted
by $1,2,3$, each with error at most $\eps L(n)/2$, and for the
finite seed exclusions. Their sum is at most $\eps L(n)$ at every
ordinary index; the shifted clock alone covers the prime blocks.
Increasing the common cutoff to at least $3$ proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\section{Consequences: the diagonal, three lines and short cycles}\label{sec:consequences}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has the following consequences for questions
recorded in the OEIS \cite{oeis}. On A064413, Thomas Ordowski
conjectured on January 23, 2009, ``If $a(n)\neq p$, then almost
everywhere $a(n)>n$'' and ``$\lim\#(a_n>n)/n=1$''. Artur Jasinski, in
December 2008, called $a(k)=k$, $2a(k)=3k$ and $2a(k)=k$ the three
critical lines of the sequence. His entry A152458, the indices with
$a(k)=k$, records the conjecture that it is finite, and on A152519 (the
indices with $2a(k)=3k$) he conjectured that the number of points on the
three lines is finite, asking also whether the listed points are
complete. We prove the
finiteness, not the completeness. Below, $p$ denotes a prime.

Only one feature of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is new for these purposes:
$b(n)>n$ for all large $n$, the sign of the second-order term. The ratio
$b(n)/n\to1$ was already known \cite{hp,haver}; it does not decide on
which side of the diagonal $a(n)$ lies.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:window}
For every $\eta>0$ there is $N_0\geq3$ such that $n<b(n)\leq(1+\eta)n$
for all $n\geq N_0$. For every $n$, exactly one of the following holds:
$a(n)$ is an odd prime and $b(n)=2a(n)$; $a(n)=3p$ for an odd prime $p$
and $2a(n)=3b(n)$; or $b(n)=a(n)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:epsilon} with $\eps=1/6$: for $n\geq N$,
$b(n)\geq n+n/(6\log n)>n$ and $b(n)\leq n+n/(2\log n)$, which is at
most $(1+\eta)n$ once $\log n\geq1/(2\eta)$. The second statement is
the definition \eqref{eq:smooth}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The diagonal]\label{cor:sign}
For all sufficiently large $n$, $a(n)<n$ if $a(n)$ is prime and
$a(n)>n$ if $a(n)$ is not prime. Thus, apart from finitely many $n$,
$a(n)>n$ exactly when $a(n)$ is composite, and $a(n)\neq n$. Moreover,
as $N\to\infty$,
\[
 \#\{k\leq N:a(k)<k\}\sim\frac{N}{2\log N},\qquad
 \#\{k\leq N:a(k)>k\}=N-\Bigl(\frac12+o(1)\Bigr)\frac{N}{\log N}.
\]
In particular $\#\{k\leq N:a(k)>k\}/N\to1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $N_0$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:window} with $\eta=1/3$, and let
$n\geq N_0$. If $a(n)$ is prime, it is not $2$, since $b(n)>n\geq3$;
so $a(n)=b(n)/2\leq2n/3<n$. If $a(n)$ is not prime, it is not $1$
(else $b(n)=1<n$), and either $a(n)=b(n)>n$ or $a(n)=3b(n)/2>n$.

For the counts, fix $\eta\in(0,1/3]$ and its $N_0$. For $N\geq N_0$,
the sets $\{k\leq N:a(k)<k\}$ and $\{k\leq N:a(k)\text{ prime}\}$ agree
on $[N_0,N]$, and $a(k)=k$ only for $k<N_0$. Let $P(N)$ be the number
of $k\in[N_0,N]$ with $a(k)$ prime. Each such $k$ has
$a(k)=b(k)/2\leq(1+\eta)N/2$, and $a$ is injective, so
$P(N)\leq\pi((1+\eta)N/2)$. Conversely, let
$M=\max\{a(k):k<N_0\}$. Since $a$ is a permutation, each prime $p$
with $M<p\leq N/2$ is $a(k)$ for one $k$, and $k\geq N_0$ because
$p>M$. Then $k<b(k)=2p\leq N$, so $P(N)\geq\pi(N/2)-M$. By the prime
number theorem $\pi(cN)\sim cN/\log N$ for each fixed $c>0$, hence
\[
 1-o(1)\leq\frac{P(N)}{N/(2\log N)}\leq1+\eta+o(1),
\]
and $\eta$ is arbitrary. The second count is $N$ minus the first and
minus the finitely many $k$ with $a(k)=k$.
\end{proof}

The first statement proves Ordowski's first conjecture in its strongest
reading, for all but finitely many $n$; it implies the density-one
reading of ``almost everywhere''. The last statement is his second
conjecture. The count of prime values alone,
$\#\{k\leq N:a(k)\text{ prime}\}\sim N/(2\log N)$, already follows from
the ratio form; the new content is that these are, with finitely many
exceptions, exactly the indices below the diagonal.

Two related OEIS entries are also finite sets: Ordowski's ``exceptional
numbers'' A155549, the $n$ with $a(n)$ not prime and $a(n)\leq n-1$,
and A064420, the $k$ with $a(k+1)=k$. Both are listed as ``probably
complete''. The first is finite by Corollary~\ref{cor:sign}. For the
second, $a(n)=n-1$ fails for large $n$: a prime value has
$a(n)\leq2n/3$ and any other value has $a(n)>n$. De Vlieger
\cite{devlieger} remarked that it is ``not surprising'' that A064420
is finite. As below, completeness is not shown.

\begin{corollary}[Three lines]\label{cor:lines}
Each of the sets $\{k:2a(k)=k\}$, $\{k:a(k)=k\}$ and $\{k:2a(k)=3k\}$
is finite.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $N_0$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:window} with $\eta=1/3$, and let
$n\geq N_0$, so $n<b(n)\leq4n/3$. If $a(n)$ is an odd prime, then
$2a(n)=b(n)$, so $n<2a(n)\leq4n/3$. If $a(n)=3p$ for an odd prime $p$,
then $2a(n)=3b(n)>3n$. Otherwise $a(n)=b(n)$ and $2n<2a(n)\leq8n/3$.
In no case is $2a(n)$ equal to $n$, $2n$ or $3n$, so all three sets
lie in $[1,N_0)$.
\end{proof}

In fact the ratios $a(n)/n$ eventually lie in
$(1/2,2/3]\cup(1,4/3]\cup(3/2,2]$, avoiding $1/2$, $1$ and $3/2$.
No explicit $N_0$ is extracted, so this does not show that the terms
listed in A152458 and A152519 are complete; Amiram Eldar recorded in
2024 that neither sequence has a further term below $10^5$ \cite{oeis}.

Lagarias, Rains and Sloane \cite[Section~7]{lrs} note that the
permutation appears to have infinitely many finite cycles and
infinitely many infinite cycles, and expect both to be hard to prove.
The following does not decide either question, but it shows that
infinitely many finite cycles, if they exist, have unbounded lengths.

\begin{corollary}[Short cycles]\label{cor:cycles}
For each $L\geq1$, the permutation $n\mapsto a(n)$ of the positive
integers has only finitely many cycles of length at most $L$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:window}, for each $n$,
\[
 2^{\sigma(n)}a(n)=3^{\tau(n)}b(n),\qquad
 (\sigma(n),\tau(n))\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\}.
\]
Choose $\eta>0$ with
$(1+\eta)^L<2^L/(2^L-1)$, and $N_0$ for this $\eta$. Suppose a cycle
$x_1,\ldots,x_\ell$, with $a(x_i)=x_{i+1}$ for $i<\ell$,
$a(x_\ell)=x_1$ and $\ell\leq L$, has every $x_i\geq N_0$. Since $a$ permutes the $x_i$,
$\prod a(x_i)=\prod x_i$; with $u=\sum\sigma(x_i)$ and
$v=\sum\tau(x_i)$, so $v\leq u\leq\ell$,
\[
 2^u\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}x_i=3^v\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b(x_i).
\]
As $b(x_i)>x_i$, this forces $3^v<2^u$, so $u\geq1$ and
$3^v\leq2^u-1$. As $b(x_i)\leq(1+\eta)x_i$, the right side is then at
most $(2^u-1)(1+\eta)^{\ell}\prod x_i$, which is less than
$2^u\prod x_i$ because
$(1+\eta)^\ell<2^L/(2^L-1)\leq2^u/(2^u-1)$. This contradiction shows
that every cycle of length at most $L$ contains an integer below
$N_0$. Distinct cycles are disjoint, so there are fewer than $N_0$ of them.
\end{proof}

indices through $5\cdot10^8$, with integer digests in
\path{data/ekg-table-summary.json}.

The ratios were certified by bounding $\log n$ with exact
rational sums for $2\operatorname{atanh}z$. After reducing the argument
to $[1,2]$, one uses $60$ positive terms with $0\leq z\leq1/3$ and tail
at most $2z^{121}/(121(1-z^2))$. This certifies an absolute error
at most $10^{-17}$ in the recorded ratios and correct rounding to
nine decimal places in Table~\ref{tab:ratio}.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

The argument completes the second-order program of Haver \cite{haver},
using the structural work of Lagarias, Rains, and Sloane \cite{lrs} and
Hofman and Pilipczuk \cite{hp}. Haver's Theorem 1(d) and Corollary 12(d)
already prove the upper half of the frontier clock with sharp constant
$1/3$. The new contribution is the lower half plus the all-index transfer.
Haver also proves the exact counting identity and the equivalence stated
in Corollary 2(e), and identifies the rough-predecessor obstruction in
the remark after Corollary 12. We discharge the remaining hypothesis of
that equivalence and prove the uniform missing-composite estimate needed
for the transfer, extending the Lean proof to the full second-order theorem.
The prime-capacity lemma is an elementary fixed-modulus sieve bound
\cite{sievebook}, proved here; it does not require a short-interval
prime number theorem. Haver's cited manuscript is unrefereed.
