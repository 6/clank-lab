\begin{abstract}
Williamson introduced the construction of Hadamard matrices from four
symmetric circulant $\{1,-1\}$ matrices. Holzmann, Kharaghani and
Tayfeh-Rezaie classified the odd orders through $59$, using row and pair spectral bounds and Williamson's product identity.
\DJ okovi\'c and Kotsireas developed compression of complementary
sequences, which Bright, Kotsireas and Ganesh incorporated into their
Williamson enumeration. Bright's research statement records the order-$65$
existence question. Building on these methods, we prove by exhaustive
computation that Williamson matrices of order $65$ do not exist: no four
symmetric circulant $\{1,-1\}$ matrices of that order have squares summing
to $260$ times the identity. Compression to length $13$ and common
multipliers reduce the enumeration to finitely many classes of quadruples.
Conservative power spectral density bounds and the product identity then
reduce the matching of pairs of sequences, followed by exact integer
autocorrelation tests. The difficult step is exhausting the full sequences
above every admissible compressed quadruple. The smaller-order enumerations
reproduce known results; separately written implementations
check the compressed enumeration and provide corroborating computations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A \emph{sign matrix} has entries in $\{1,-1\}$. A matrix is circulant when
each row is the cyclic shift of its predecessor. Four symmetric circulant
sign matrices $A,B,C,D$ of order $n$ are \emph{Williamson matrices} if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix}
 A^2+B^2+C^2+D^2=4nI_n,
\end{equation}
where $I_n$ is the identity matrix. We establish the following
computer-assisted result.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There are no four symmetric circulant sign matrices $A,B,C,D$ of order
$65$ satisfying $A^2+B^2+C^2+D^2=260I_{65}$.
\end{theorem}

The proof uses the written reductions below and the exhaustive computation
described in Section~\ref{sec:computation}. This settles the symmetric circulant
case at order $65$. Order $67$ remains open~\cite[Section 2.3]{KMTZ}.
Bright records the order-$65$ question in Section 4.2 of his research
statement~\cite{BrightStatement}.

Williamson's construction~\cite{Williamson} is described historically by
Koukouvinos and Kounias~\cite[Section 1, p.~162]{KK1990}. Turyn gave an
infinite family~\cite[abstract]{Turyn}.
Koukouvinos and Kounias~\cite[Section 1, p.~162]{KK1990} credit
Baumert, Golomb and Hall with order $23$ and Baumert and Hall with the
classification at odd orders through $23$~\cite{BGH,BH}.
\DJ okovi\'c's note~\cite[Sections 1 and 5, Table 1]{Djokovic1995}
lists the searches of Sawade, Koukouvinos and Kounias, and
\DJ okovi\'c~\cite{Sawade,KK1988,KK1990,Djokovic1992,Djokovic},
with the corrected count at $25$, discrepancies reported at $33$ and $39$,
and nonexistence at $35$. Holzmann, Kharaghani and Tayfeh-Rezaie's classification through
$59$ gives nonexistence at $47$, $53$ and $59$~\cite[Section 4, Table 1]{HKT}.
Egan, Kotsireas, London and \'O Cath\'ain summarize nonexistence
at $35$, $47$, $53$ and $59$~\cite[Section 2.1]{EKLO}.
Lang and Schneider studied Turyn-type Williamson matrices, as described
in their title and abstract~\cite{LS};
Bright, Kotsireas and Ganesh credit their order-$61$
enumeration~\cite[p.~3]{BKG}.

We reuse Holzmann, Kharaghani and Tayfeh-Rezaie's row and pair
spectral bounds, expressed below as power spectral density (PSD) bounds,
and product-identity elimination~\cite[Eq.~(2), Theorem 1, algorithm 2(c--d)]{HKT}.
They fix two blocks, eliminate the fourth and solve linearized equations
modulo $4$ for the third before checking the original equations
\cite[Section 3, Eqs.~(4--5), steps 2(d)(ii)(A--E)]{HKT}.
Here \DJ okovi\'c--Kotsireas compression~\cite[Definition 3, Theorem 3]{DK},
in Bright--Kotsireas--Ganesh's formulation~\cite[Definition 11, Theorem 12]{BKG},
is followed by a multiplier restriction on one sequence and two-sided
PSD bounds before matching pairs of full sequences. Bright, Kotsireas and Ganesh give the spectral formulation in
Corollaries 8--9~\cite[Corollaries 8--9]{BKG}.
Our contribution is the complete order-$65$ enumeration.
The compressed conditions are necessary; their admissible tuples still
require a full-row search. Exhausting those lifts is the hard step.

We first translate the matrix equation into conditions on autocorrelations.
Compression to length $13$ and common multipliers then give representatives
of compressed tuples. Two enumerations check that these representatives
cover the compressed possibilities. For each representative, a common
multiplier fixing its compression restricts only one row list. Pairs of
full rows are matched using the PSD bounds and product identity, and any
match is checked by exact autocorrelations. The lemmas show that every
solution survives these operations, so the exhaustive absence of matches
completes the proof.

Nearby work includes Kharaghani, Mohammadian, Tayfeh-Rezaie and Zaitsev's
near-Williamson matrices~\cite[Definition 1.1, Table 6]{KMTZ} and
Bennett, Bright, Colinot and Nayak's enumeration allowing nonsymmetric
blocks~\cite[abstract]{BBCN}. Zhang's AI-assisted account describes work on
Hadamard order $668$ and Williamson order $65$, reporting
``novel non-existence evidence and mathematical insights'' while leaving
both problems unsolved~\cite[Zenodo record description]{Zhang}.

The proofs, the search code, the enumeration checkers, the witness checkers
and the text were produced by AI models (from OpenAI and Anthropic),
working under the author's direction. This work is computer-assisted and not yet reviewed by experts.

\section{Rows, autocorrelations, and spectra}\label{sec:rows}
The row sum and power spectral density give the necessary conditions for
individual sequences.

Lowercase $a,b,c,d$ denote the four first rows; the letters in the
order-$5$ example below are local scalars.

All indices are taken modulo the odd order $n$, and $H=(n-1)/2$.
For example, at order $5$ a normalized symmetric first row has the form
$(1,a,b,b,a)$, with $a,b\in\{1,-1\}$. In general a normalized row is a
sequence $x=(x_i)_{i\in\Z/n\Z}$ with $x_0=1$ and $x_i=x_{-i}$.
Define its periodic autocorrelation by
\[
 \PAF_x(s)=\sum_{i\in\Z/n\Z}x_i x_{i+s}.
\]
For a symmetric circulant matrix with first row $x$, the entry in
position $(0,s)$ of its square is $\PAF_x(s)$. Thus the matrix equation is
exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:paf}
 \PAF_a(s)+\PAF_b(s)+\PAF_c(s)+\PAF_d(s)
 =\begin{cases}4n,&s=0,\\0,&s\ne0.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Whenever a sum below runs over the four rows, it counts their four positions,
including repetitions. Autocorrelation is symmetric in $s$, so testing $0\leq s\leq H$ suffices.

A symmetric row of order $65$, normalized to start with $+1$, is
encoded by its $32$ remaining signs.
For a sequence $x$, its negative support is the set of indices where
$x_i=-1$; its weight is the number of such indices. The following encoding
records one index from each nonzero symmetric pair.
Encode it by the $H$-bit integer $m_x$ whose bit $i-1$ is one exactly
when $x_i=-1$, for $1\leq i\leq H$. The function
$\operatorname{popcount}(m_x)$ counts the one bits of this mask.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rowsum}
If Williamson matrices of odd order $n$ exist, their first rows may be
normalized to $x_0=1$. Each normalized row has row sum
$r_x=n-4\operatorname{popcount}(m_x)$, and the four row sums satisfy
$r_a^2+r_b^2+r_c^2+r_d^2=4n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Negation preserves every autocorrelation. Negative entries in a normalized
symmetric row occur in pairs, giving the stated row sum. Summing
\eqref{eq:paf} over all shifts gives the sum of the squares of the row sums.
\end{proof}
At order $65$ the nondecreasing lists of absolute row sums are exactly
\[
 (1,3,5,15),\ (1,3,9,13),\ (3,3,11,11),\ (3,7,9,11),\ (7,7,9,9).
\]
Each sign is forced by $r_x\equiv65\pmod4$, giving the allowed signed
values $1,-3,5,-7,9,-11,13,-15$. Permuting the four rows therefore permits
sorting by the absolute row sums without loss.

For frequency $k\in\Z/n\Z$, the real eigenvalue of the circulant is
\[
 \lambda_x(k)=1+2\sum_{i=1}^{H}x_i\cos(2\pi ik/n).
\]
The Fourier transform diagonalizes all circulant matrices. Consequently
\eqref{eq:paf} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral}
 \lambda_a(k)^2+\lambda_b(k)^2+\lambda_c(k)^2+\lambda_d(k)^2=4n
 \qquad(k\in\Z/n\Z).
\end{equation}
For symmetric sequences the power spectral density is $\lambda_x(k)^2$.
In particular every solution row satisfies $\lambda_x(k)^2\leq4n$.
The enumeration considers every mask and retains only permitted row sums
and rows satisfying this necessary spectral bound, with the numerical
slack justified in Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic}.

\section{Compression and multiplier orbits}\label{sec:cover}
Compression and common multipliers reduce the number of quadruples to
be searched. We describe the compressed classes and their symmetry reduction.

In this section capitals denote compressed sequences, while lowercase
letters continue to denote full rows.

For $n=qL$, with compression factor $q$, define the compression of a row $x$ by
\[
 X_j=\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}x_{j+Lt},\qquad j\in\Z/L\Z.
\]
Here $q=5$ and $L=13$. Write
$\PAF_X(s)=\sum_{j\in\Z/L\Z}X_jX_{j+s}$ for compressed autocorrelation.
Write $A,B,C,D$ for the compressions of $a,b,c,d$, respectively.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:compression}
For four rows satisfying \eqref{eq:paf}, their compressions satisfy
\[
 \PAF_A(s)+\PAF_B(s)+\PAF_C(s)+\PAF_D(s)
 =\begin{cases}4n,&s=0\pmod L,\\0,&s\ne0\pmod L.\end{cases}
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:compression}]
Expanding the compressed autocorrelation gives
$\PAF_X(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}\PAF_x(s+Lt)$.
For a nonzero residue $s$ none of these shifts is zero modulo $n$.
For $s=0$ exactly one is zero. Summing over the four rows proves the claim.
\end{proof}

A \emph{multiplier} is an invertible residue $u$ modulo $65$, acting on
a row by $x_i\mapsto x_{ui}$. It preserves \eqref{eq:paf}, since it
permutes the shifts. Its action on the compression depends only on its
residue modulo $13$. Opposite multipliers act identically on symmetric
rows. Every invertible residue modulo $13$ lifts to an invertible residue
modulo $65$: among $u,u+13,\ldots,u+52$, exactly one is divisible by $5$,
so the other four are coprime to $65$.

A class consists of the retained rows with the
same signed row sum and compressed vector.

Call a tuple of four classes \emph{admissible} when its compressed
vectors satisfy Lemma~\ref{lem:compression} and its row sums satisfy
Lemma~\ref{lem:rowsum}. A \emph{lift} is a tuple of four retained rows,
one from each class; it need not itself solve the matrix equation.
These are compression classes, rather than multiplier orbits; lifting
recovers the uncompressed sequences.
The enumeration producing compressed tuples is called the generator;
the independent enumeration checking its completeness is called the cover check.
Both use the following ordering convention.
Classes are ordered lexicographically by the seven entries
$(X_0,\ldots,X_6)$ of their symmetric compression. A class identifier is
its zero-based rank in this order; the data files use those identifiers. A \emph{sorted} class tuple
has nondecreasing absolute row sums and, within equal row sums,
nondecreasing class identifiers. Acting by a multiplier means applying it
to each compressed vector and then re-sorting within equal-row-sum
positions. The generator and cover check use this same order.

The admissible class tuples are enumerated by matching sums of two exact
compressed autocorrelation vectors to the complementary pair sums.
Every ordered class pair is included; equal classes are permitted.
Sorting positions of equal absolute row sum removes only row permutations.
The complete tuple set is also enumerated independently by matching the
exact integer vectors themselves.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cover}
Let one representative of each orbit of admissible sorted class tuples
under $(\Z/13\Z)^*/\{1,-1\}$ be retained. Every order-$65$ Williamson
solution is equivalent, by a common multiplier and a permutation of its
rows, to a lift of a retained tuple.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A solution's tuple is admissible by Lemma~\ref{lem:compression}.
Choose a multiplier carrying it to its orbit representative, lift that
multiplier modulo $65$, and apply it to all four full rows. Re-sort rows
of equal absolute row sum. The resulting solution has the representative
tuple. All lifts of the representative are searched, even if more than one
multiplier carries the original tuple to it. Multipliers permute the exact
eigenvalues. Transformed solution rows therefore still satisfy
$\lambda_x(k)^2\leq260$ and pass the numerical filter with its proved
margin.
\end{proof}
The compressed orbit representatives are checked for invalid, duplicate
or missing entries by independently enumerating the exact compressed tuples.

A further symmetry reduces one row list in each orbit representative. The subgroup
\[
 K=\{u\in(\Z/65\Z)^*:u\equiv1\pmod{13}\}=\{1,14,27,53\}
\]
fixes every compressed vector. Choose one designated position in the representative
and retain only rows whose mask is least in its $K$-orbit.
Applying a common element of $K$ that minimizes that row preserves a
solution in the same representative. It is essential that this restriction applies
to only one designated row; independent minimization of all four rows
would not be justified.

\section{Pair filtering and autocorrelation matching}\label{sec:join}
The PSD test and product identity retain every solution when pairs of
sequences are matched. A pairing is one of the three splits
$(a,b)\mid(c,d)$, $(a,c)\mid(b,d)$, or $(a,d)\mid(b,c)$.
The implementation may choose any of these pairings and any designated row; these choices affect cost only.
The displayed splits specify the left and right sides.

For a fixed representative, its four classes supply four lists of full
rows. Pairs are grouped by a fixed key function of their autocorrelation
sums and masks; on the opposite side we use the negative sums and
complementary masks. Equal data therefore give equal keys. A pair survives when it satisfies
the PSD tests. A key match is a surviving left pair and a surviving right
pair with equal keys. Appendix~\ref{app:join} supplies the exact hashing
and storage rules.

\subsection{Conservative numerical enclosures}\label{sec:arithmetic}
We use integer enclosures to make the spectral comparisons safe under
rounding. At order $65$, the error in each computed squared eigenvalue is
below $4\cdot10^{-11}$ under the arithmetic assumptions and cosine-table
checks detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}. The row filter rejects only
when the computed square exceeds $260+10^{-6}$; it therefore retains
every solution row.

Let $\widetilde\lambda_x(k)$ be the computed eigenvalue. Define integer
lower and upper enclosures
\[
 \ell_x(k)=\max\{0,\lfloor64\widetilde\lambda_x(k)^2-0.01\rfloor\},
 \qquad
 h_x(k)=\lceil64\widetilde\lambda_x(k)^2+0.01\rceil.
\]
The error bound, including the two additional floating-point additions,
implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:enclosure}
 \ell_x(k)\leq64\lambda_x(k)^2\leq h_x(k).
\end{equation}
Clamping the lower bound to zero preserves this enclosure, since a square
is nonnegative.

\subsection{The pair filter}
The opposite pair of row lists bounds the possible PSD contribution of each pair. Consider two lists $\mathcal A,\mathcal B$ on one side and
$\mathcal C,\mathcal D$ on the other, after restricting the designated row.
Set $Q=64\cdot260$. For each frequency let
\[
 U(k)=\max\{0,Q-\min_{c\in\mathcal C}\ell_c(k)
                    -\min_{d\in\mathcal D}\ell_d(k)\},
\]
\[
 V(k)=\max\{0,Q-\max_{c\in\mathcal C}h_c(k)
                    -\max_{d\in\mathcal D}h_d(k)\}.
\]
Retain a pair $(a,b)$ only when, at every frequency,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pair}
 \ell_a(k)+\ell_b(k)\leq U(k),\qquad
 h_a(k)+h_b(k)\geq V(k).
\end{equation}
The opposite side uses the same rule with the two sides interchanged.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pair}
Every pair belonging to a solution in the four lists passes
\eqref{eq:pair} on both sides.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For its complementary rows $c,d$, equation~\eqref{eq:spectral} gives
$64(\lambda_a(k)^2+\lambda_b(k)^2)=Q-64\lambda_c(k)^2-64\lambda_d(k)^2$.
Replacing each square by the appropriate lower or upper enclosure proves
the inequalities before clamping. Raising a negative upper bound to zero
only weakens the upper test. Raising a negative lower bound to zero changes
nothing, because $h_a+h_b$ is nonnegative.
\end{proof}
If a list is empty there is no solution in the representative. The implementation details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:join}.

\subsection{Williamson's product identity}
The second matching condition is Williamson's classical product identity
\cite[Theorem 14]{BKG}. We include its short argument to fix the normalization.

In set notation the negative positions form supports; their weights give
the row sums, and symmetric difference corresponds to exclusive-or in
the stored encoding. We retain that encoding in the following statement.
In its proof a sequence also denotes the corresponding element of the
cyclic group ring.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:product}
For an odd-order Williamson solution normalized by $x_0=1$ in each row,
$a_i b_i c_i d_i=-1$ for every nonzero index $i$. Equivalently,
$m_a\mathbin{\oplus}m_b\mathbin{\oplus}m_c\mathbin{\oplus}m_d=2^H-1$,
where $\oplus$ is bitwise exclusive-or on $H$ bits.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the integral group ring of the cyclic group of order $n$, write
$J$ for the sum of all group elements and $T_x$ for the sum of the
positions where row $x$ is negative. Then $x=J-2T_x$ and
$x^2=nJ-4|T_x|J+4T_x^2$. Symmetry and normalization make $|T_x|$ even.
Modulo $2$, the square $T_x^2$ equals the sum of the squares of its
individual group elements, because mixed terms occur twice. Reducing
$\sum_x x^2=4n\,1$ modulo $8$ and dividing by $4$ modulo $2$ gives
\[
 \sum_x\sum_{t\in T_x}t^2=1+J\pmod2.
\]
Squaring permutes a cyclic group of odd order. Every nonidentity position
therefore occurs in an odd number of the four negative sets, proving the
product identity and its mask form.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of the main theorem}\label{sec:overview}
The preceding reductions preserve every solution and reduce the exclusion to
the finite computation summarized in Table~\ref{tab:counts}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Negating a row preserves its autocorrelations, so every solution can be
normalized. Lemma~\ref{lem:rowsum} and the PSD bound retain its four rows.
A common invertible index change permutes autocorrelation shifts, preserving
\eqref{eq:paf}. Applying such a change to all four rows and permuting them
carries their compressions to a retained representative
(Lemmas~\ref{lem:compression} and~\ref{lem:cover}). Among the index changes
fixing all four compressions, choose one minimizing a designated row.
Applying it to all rows preserves the solution; only that position is restricted.

For $1\leq s\leq H$, equation~\eqref{eq:paf} gives
$\PAF_a(s)+\PAF_b(s)=-(\PAF_c(s)+\PAF_d(s))$.
The conservative PSD tests retain these pairs (Lemma~\ref{lem:pair}),
and their product condition holds by Lemma~\ref{lem:product}.
Equal integer inputs give equal matching keys in Appendix~\ref{app:matching},
so every solution produces a retained key match. The exhaustive computation
finds none (Table~\ref{tab:counts}), completing the exclusion.
\end{proof}

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:computation}
The exhaustive computation is supported by checks of the compressed
enumeration and validation against smaller orders.
The complete CPU search found no matching pairs (Table~\ref{tab:counts}).
The proof depends on exhaustive computation, the written reductions above,
and correct compiler, hardware, integer and IEEE binary64 arithmetic,
including the interval checks in Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}; the search
has not been formally verified and the records of the enumeration do not
constitute a short proof certificate.
One checker reuses the CPU row generator with separate compressed-enumeration
code; the other has independent row, cosine, enumeration and record-checking
code. They check completeness of the compressed enumeration and the
recorded totals, without independently rederiving all survivor or key-match
counts or recomputing every pair comparison. The distributed full search
is the way to repeat the decisive computation. Its implementation and
numerical details are in Appendices~\ref{app:join} and~\ref{app:numerics},
with commands and historical records in the accompanying checker documentation.

The archived unfiltered baseline enumeration, separate from the full-row
search, reproduces the published numbers of equivalence classes through
odd order $33$~\cite[Table 1]{HKT}; the counts are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:exhaustion}.

The validation also recovers the known solutions at $39$, $45$ and
$55$~\cite[Section 4, Table 1]{HKT}; the full validation with
both compression factors $5$ and $11$ discovers the order-$55$ solution
without a supplied witness. A separate NumPy implementation agrees on
all $3{,}309$ compressed representatives at order $45$ and recovers its solution.

The CPU computation took about $271$ CPU-hours; its processor models and
full elapsed time were not recorded. Two separately written GPU implementations
also found no solutions. The primary computation recorded about $23.8$
GPU-hours; the second used four NVIDIA GB10 GPUs for about $2.06$
GPU-hours of search ($2.14$ including setup and smaller-order checks).
Their different pair filters give different intermediate counts.
These historical computations share the mathematical specification and
were produced by the same team. The earlier GPU programs are not included
in the distributed CPU bundle, and not all original primary GPU records
were rechecked.

The search programs, input data and checker documentation accompany the
paper. The unchanged programs and data are archived with version 2 at
\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23622210}.

\section{Numerical error bound}\label{app:numerics}
Conservative error bounds justify the PSD comparisons of
Section~\ref{sec:arithmetic}.

The row generator and the search evaluate the cosine table in IEEE binary64
arithmetic, rounded to nearest, without fast-math transformations.
The numerical
bound assumes ordinary IEEE binary64 operations rather than x87 extended
intermediate precision; a build changing the arithmetic model requires
its own justification. The actual table entries are checked against interval evaluations of cosine
at $50$ decimal digits. Their errors are at most $2^{-49}$.
For order $65$ the eigenvalue sum is split into two sums of at most $16$
terms. Each partial sum has magnitude at most $16$, so the error in one
half is bounded by
\[
 16\,2^{-49}+16^2\,2^{-53}<6\cdot10^{-14}.
\]
Combining the halves, doubling, and adding one gives eigenvalue error below
$2.6\cdot10^{-13}$. Since $|\lambda_x(k)|\leq65$, the error in the
computed square, including rounding of the multiplication, is below
$4\cdot10^{-11}$. Fusing a multiplication and addition does not enlarge
this bound. The row filter rejects only when the computed square exceeds
$260+10^{-6}$, so it retains every solution row.


\section{Hashing and storage}\label{app:join}
The storage scheme preserves every pair equality required by a solution.
The fixed coefficients and key function are specified in the accompanying
implementation documentation. Their values affect collisions and cost, but
the argument needs only that both sides use the same function.

\subsection{Hash matching and exact verification}\label{app:matching}
We show that the matching keys retain every exact autocorrelation equality.

For each row, form a linear hash of its nonzero-shift integer
autocorrelations,
\[
 f(x)=\sum_{s=1}^{H}R_s\PAF_x(s)\pmod {2^{64}},
\]
using fixed odd coefficients $R_s$; their reproducible choice is described
in the accompanying implementation documentation. A solution satisfies
$f(a)+f(b)=-f(c)-f(d)$ and
$m_a\oplus m_b=(2^H-1)\oplus m_c\oplus m_d$.
The search combines these two necessary equalities into a single
$64$-bit key, using the same function on both sides. Specifically it uses
$\alpha\oplus R\beta$ for hash sum $\alpha$ and mask value $\beta$, with a fixed odd $R$,
and maps a zero key to one. The right side uses the negative hash sum and
the $H$-bit complement of its mask XOR. Equal inputs give equal keys;
hash collisions may add candidates but cannot remove a solution.

Moreover,
$ f(a)+f(b)+f(c)+f(d)
=\sum_{s=1}^{H}R_s(\PAF_a(s)+\PAF_b(s)+\PAF_c(s)+\PAF_d(s))=0$
modulo $2^{64}$, so their join keys are equal.

The join retains every pair with an equal key, including duplicates.
Its storage and restart rules, justified in Section~\ref{app:storage}, do
not remove any such pair.

Finally, each key match is checked using the four exact integer
autocorrelation vectors at shifts $0,\ldots,H$. Equation~\eqref{eq:paf}
then decides the matrix equation exactly. A separate witness checker can
also form the four matrices and multiply them directly. This last check
prevents false positives; it cannot repair a missing pair earlier in the
search.

\subsection{Storage}\label{app:storage}
The storage rules preserve all surviving pairs and their exact comparisons.

The pair bounds are formed in signed integer arithmetic before conversion
to unsigned 16-bit values. The stored row values are below $16{,}642$;
all intervening sums and differences fit the signed integer type.
Frequency reordering and vectorized comparisons do not change the
predicates. In a vectorized upper test, a row with $\ell_x>U$ is discarded
first, before forming $U-\ell_x$; the lower difference is
$\max\{0,V-h_x\}$. The remaining frequencies are tested with signed
integer sums. Empty lists contain no pairs and cannot yield a solution.

Every surviving pair is retained, including repeated keys and repeated
sequences. Looking up a key examines all pairs with that key. If storage
is insufficient, the whole attempt restarts with pairs partitioned by
the high bits of the same key on both sides; equal keys therefore remain
together. Every reported order-$65$ representative completed without such
partitioning. The accompanying implementation documentation specifies the
storage scheme.
