\begin{abstract}
Every noncrossing partition lattice of a finite well-generated complex
reflection group admits a symmetric chain decomposition: a partition
into saturated chains whose bottom and top ranks sum to the rank of the
lattice. This answers the chain part of a question of Henri M\"uhle,
who proved symmetric Boolean decompositions for the groups $G(d,d,n)$,
symmetric chain decompositions for the exceptional types of rank at
most four other than $H_4$, and the strong Sperner property for every
well-generated group. With the theorems of Simion and Ullman for the
symmetric groups and of Reiner for type $B$, and the isomorphism of
Bessis and Corran between the lattices of $G(d,1,n)$ and of type $B$, six
exceptional types remained: $H_4$, $G_{33}$, $G_{34}$, $E_6$, $E_7$
and $E_8$. For these, computer-found chains are checked exactly, and a
finite criterion proves that each checked graph is the whole interval.
Reflection
automorphisms of Reiner, Ripoll and Stump carry the chains to every
Coxeter element; a product construction treats reducible groups. Lean
checks the abstract walk criterion, the product construction and the
transfer along order isomorphisms, but not the group computations or
the cited results.

This work has not yet been reviewed by experts. The proofs, the Lean formalization, the certificate checkers and the text were produced by AI models (from OpenAI and Anthropic), working under the author's direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A noncrossing partition of a circularly ordered set groups its points
into blocks whose convex hulls do not cross. Ordered by refinement,
these partitions form the classical noncrossing partition lattice,
introduced by Kreweras~\cite{Kreweras}. Biane and Brady treated
noncrossing partitions inside the symmetric group \cite{Biane,Brady},
and Brady--Watt and Bessis extended the construction to reflection
groups \cite{BW,BessisDual}. There refinement is replaced by absolute
order, in which an element lies below another when a shortest
factorization of the first into reflections extends to one of the
second, and the noncrossing partitions are the interval below a
Coxeter element. This interval is a lattice for every well-generated
group \cite[introduction, first paragraph]{Muhle}. For groups generated
by involutive reflections, the standing assumption of Bessis's
Section~8, this is Bessis's Lemma~8.6 \cite[pp.~848 and~851]{Bessis},
whose proof combines \cite{BessisDual}, Brady and Watt's case-free proof
\cite{BW2008}, Bessis and Corran's treatment of $G(e,e,r)$ \cite{BC} and
computer checks. The lattice property is not used below.

A graded poset of rank $n$ has a \emph{symmetric chain decomposition}
if its elements can be partitioned into saturated chains whose bottom
and top ranks sum to $n$ (Section~\ref{sec:overview}). Such a
decomposition implies the strong Sperner property
\cite[remark after Question~1.2]{Muhle}.
\begin{samepage}
Henri M\"uhle asks \cite[Question~1.1]{Muhle}:
\begin{quote}
Does the lattice of $W$-noncrossing partitions always admit a symmetric
decomposition into saturated chains or Boolean lattices for every
well-generated complex reflection group $W$?
\end{quote}
\end{samepage}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} below answers its chain part. The Boolean part
is not settled here.

Rodica Simion and Daniel Ullman constructed symmetric decompositions
for classical noncrossing partitions \cite{SU}. Victor Reiner proved
the type-$B$ chain assertion \cite[Theorem~13]{Reiner}; Patricia Hersh's
local symmetric-group actions give the stronger Boolean decomposition
in type $B$ \cite[Theorems~5 and~7]{Hersh}. Work of David Bessis and
Ruth Corran identifies the $G(d,1,m)$ lattices with type $B$ for
$d\geq2$ \cite{BC}; see Section~\ref{sec:reduction}. M\"uhle proves the Boolean assertion for
$G(d,d,m)$ and the chain assertion for the exceptional types of rank
at most four other than $H_4$
\cite[Theorem~1.3 and Proposition~4.2]{Muhle}.
Christos A.~Athanasiadis and Victor Reiner attribute a type-$D$ symmetric
chain decomposition to Eleni Tzanaki, citing a private communication
\cite[author manuscript, Section~7, item~4]{AR}; M\"uhle also records this
attribution \cite[introduction, after Theorem~1.3]{Muhle}.
This left six exceptional types: $H_4$, $G_{33}$, $G_{34}$, $E_6$,
$E_7$ and $E_8$. We prove the following; the terminology is made
precise in Section~\ref{sec:overview}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $W$ be a finite well-generated complex reflection group of essential
rank $n$. For each irreducible factor choose any Coxeter element, and
let $c$ be their product. The noncrossing partition lattice
$\NC(W,c)$ has a partition into nonempty saturated chains whose
bottom and top ranks sum to $n$. The trivial group, of rank zero, is
included.
\end{theorem}

Here a Coxeter element is any regular element of order $h$, in the
broader sense of Reiner, Ripoll and Stump; Section~\ref{sec:overview}
compares it with the classical notion.

M\"uhle proved that these lattices are rank-symmetric, rank-unimodal
and strongly Sperner \cite[Theorem~1.4]{Muhle}. By a theorem of Griggs
\cite{Griggs}, the LYM (equivalently, normalized matching) property
together with rank symmetry and unimodality implies a symmetric chain
decomposition; see \cite[Theorem~4.1]{Muhle}. M\"uhle used GAP and Sage
to verify these hypotheses for the exceptional types of rank at most
four other than $H_4$ \cite[Proposition~4.2]{Muhle}; the six types above
were not covered. We construct their chains by iterating Griggs's
matching trick, as used by Tomon \cite{Griggs}\cite[\mbox{\S\S3--4}]{Tomon},
and verify the chains and the whole interval directly. We do not test
normalized matching.

The reduction uses the classification of Shephard and Todd \cite{ST},
the exact models build on work of Armstrong, Bessis, Coxeter and Todd,
Oura and Sekiguchi, and Conway and Sloane (Appendix~\ref{app:models}),
and the passage between Coxeter elements uses the theorem of Reiner,
Ripoll and Stump \cite{RRS}. The proof combines exact finite
computations with general lemmas checked in Lean;
Section~\ref{sec:checking} says what each covers.

\section{Definitions and proof overview}\label{sec:overview}
A \emph{complex reflection} on a finite-dimensional complex vector
space $V$ is a nonidentity finite-order linear transformation fixing
a hyperplane pointwise. A finite complex reflection group is a finite subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$
generated by reflections. Its \emph{essential rank} is
$\dim V-\dim\Fix(W)$. It is \emph{well-generated} if it can be generated
by that many reflections. Restricting $W$ to a $W$-stable complement
of $\Fix(W)$ gives an isomorphic reflection group of the same essential
rank, with the same reflections, reflection lengths and values of
$\rank(I-w)$. We therefore allow models with a fixed subspace, as for
$G_{33}$ in Appendix~\ref{app:models}.

Let $T$ be the set of all reflections in $W$. It is closed under
inversion and conjugation. The \emph{reflection length} $\ell_T(w)$ is
the smallest number of factors from $T$ in a product equal to $w$.
A \emph{reflection word} of length $k$ for $w$ is a factorization
$w=t_1\cdots t_k$ with all $t_i\in T$; it is \emph{reduced} if
$k=\ell_T(w)$.
The \emph{absolute order} and its interval below $c$ are
\begin{align*}
 u\leq_T v&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
       \ell_T(u)+\ell_T(u^{-1}v)=\ell_T(v),\\
 \NC(W,c)&=[1,c]_T.
\end{align*}
The rank of an element in this interval is $\ell_T(w)$.

By the Chevalley--Shephard--Todd theorem
\cite[Theorem~5.1]{ST}\cite[Theorem~(A)]{Chevalley}, for the essential
rank-$n$ representation the algebra of invariant
polynomials $\C[V]^W$ is a polynomial algebra on $n$ homogeneous
generators. Their degrees $d_1\leq\cdots\leq d_n$ are independent
of the choice of homogeneous generators and are called the
\emph{invariant degrees} of $W$. For irreducible $W$, let $h=d_n$
be the largest invariant degree. A vector
is \emph{regular} if it lies outside every reflecting hyperplane. A
\emph{Coxeter element} here means a regular element of order $h$: it
has an eigenvector that is regular, with eigenvalue of order $h$.
This is the definition of Reiner, Ripoll and Stump
\cite[Definition~1.1 and the paragraph after it]{RRS}; the classical definition requires the
eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i/h}$, and for real groups gives the products of
the simple reflections. The regular elements of order $h$ need not form
a single conjugacy class. For a reducible group the choice is made in
each irreducible factor, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

For example, $\NC(S_3,(123))$ has rank sizes $1,3,1$. A maximal chain
$1<(12)<(123)$, together with the two singleton chains $(13)$ and
$(23)$, is a symmetric chain decomposition. The maximal chain has
endpoint ranks $0,2$, and each singleton has rank $1$.

\begin{definition}
In a finite graded poset of rank $n$, a \emph{saturated chain} has a
cover relation between each consecutive pair. It is \emph{symmetric}
if its endpoint ranks sum to $n$. A \emph{symmetric chain
decomposition} is a partition of the elements into nonempty symmetric
saturated chains.
\end{definition}

The proof has three parts. First, published decompositions leave only
six irreducible types. For one Coxeter element in each, a
\emph{certificate} is a finite data file listing elements of $W$ by
exact labels that determine their matrices (Appendix~\ref{app:models}),
the downward reflection steps between them, and a partition of them
into chains. Second, the list of all reflections and all downward
reflection steps from the listed elements show that the listed graph is
all of $[1,c]_T$. The key observation is that, on a
reflection walk of length $n$ from $c$ to $1$ with
$\rank(I-c)=n$, the quantity $\rank(I-w)$ must drop at every step.
This turns interval completeness into a finite closure check.
Finally, reflection-preserving automorphisms transport the chains to
every Coxeter element, and a grid construction combines factor chains
for reducible groups.

The argument does not assume that reflection length equals fixed-space
codimension on the entire complex reflection group. It proves that
equality only for the certified interval.

\section{Absolute-order intervals}\label{sec:finite}
The finite criterion below applies in any faithful finite-dimensional
representation in which $\rank(I-t)=1$ for every $t\in T$.

Put $q(w)=\rank(I-w)$. The identity
\[
                    I-uv=(I-u)+u(I-v)
\]
implies $q(uv)\leq q(u)+q(v)$. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qbound}
 q(w)\leq\ell_T(w),\qquad q(w)\leq q(wt)+1\quad(t\in T).
\end{equation}
The second inequality follows by applying subadditivity to
$w=(wt)t^{-1}$. A \emph{reflection walk} has steps
$w\longmapsto wt$ with $t\in T$; its \emph{vertices} are the elements
it visits.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:walk}
Suppose $q(c)=n$ and $c$ has a reflection word of length $n$.
Then $\ell_T(c)=n$. An element $x$ belongs to $[1,c]_T$ if and only
if it lies on a reflection walk from $c$ to $1$ of length $n$.
Every step of every such walk lowers $q$ by one. At any vertex $x$
on the walk, $q(x)=\ell_T(x)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The word and \eqref{eq:qbound} give
$n=q(c)\leq\ell_T(c)\leq n$.
If $x\leq_T c$, reduced words for $x^{-1}c$ and $x$, removed in
reverse order, give a right-reflection walk $c\to x\to1$ of total
length $n$. Conversely, if such a walk takes $k$ steps to reach $x$
and $n-k$ more to reach $1$, then
\[
 \ell_T(x^{-1}c)\leq k,\qquad \ell_T(x)\leq n-k.
\]
The triangle inequality for reflection length forces their sum to be
$n$, so $x\leq_T c$.

The $n$ steps lower $q$ from $n$ to zero. By \eqref{eq:qbound} each
step lowers it by at most one. The sum of these decreases is $n$,
so each is one. Thus $q(x)=n-k$. Since
$q(x)\leq\ell_T(x)\leq n-k$, equality holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Finite interval criterion]\label{prop:criterion}
Suppose $T$ is the entire reflection set, $q(c)=n$, and a reflection
word of length $n$ for $c$ is supplied. Let $S$ be a finite set of
distinct elements of $W$ containing $c$ and $1$. Suppose that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each $x\in S$ lies on a reflection walk $c\to x\to1$ of total
      length $n$;
\item for each $x\in S$ and $t\in T$, if $q(xt)=q(x)-1$, then
      $xt\in S$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $S=[1,c]_T$, and $q$ is its absolute-order rank. Its downward
covers are exactly the pairs $x\longmapsto xt$ with $x,xt\in S$ and
$q(xt)=q(x)-1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first condition and Lemma~\ref{lem:walk} give
$S\subseteq[1,c]_T$ and $q=\ell_T$ on $S$. If $y\in[1,c]_T$,
choose a length-$n$ walk through $y$. Every step lowers $q$ by one.
Starting at $c\in S$, the second condition keeps that walk in $S$;
hence $y\in S$.

For a pair in the asserted downward edge set, write $y=xt$.
The equality $\ell_T(y)=\ell_T(x)-1$ and the fact that
$y^{-1}x=t^{-1}$ is a reflection give $y<_T x$. Their ranks differ
by one, so this is a cover. Conversely, if $y<_T x$, a reduced word
for $y^{-1}x$ extends a reduced word for $y$ to one for $x$.
Its successive prefixes give all intervening absolute ranks. They
also lie below $c$, by extending further to a reduced word for $c$.
If $y<_T x$ is a cover, there can therefore be only one added
reflection. This is precisely a downward reflection step of rank one.
\end{proof}

Thus the criterion proves that the computed graph is the whole
interval with its cover relations, not just a subposet of it.

\section{The exceptional cases}\label{sec:models}
The six certificates use the exact reflection-group models specified
in Appendix~\ref{app:models}.
The complete chain partitions and interval graphs are in the
earlier archived verification package~\cite{Archive},
\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23605555}.

The chains were found by extending chains outward from the middle
ranks, one rank at a time, by bipartite perfect matchings, iterating
Griggs's matching trick \cite{Griggs} as used by
Tomon~\cite[\mbox{\S\S3--4}]{Tomon}. How they were found plays no role in the
proof: the final certificates are checked directly for disjointness,
coverage, saturation and endpoint symmetry.

For each of the six types, the checks detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:checks} establish that the candidate reflections are
exactly the elements $t$ of the group with $\rank(I-t)=1$; that $c$ is
a product of $n$ reflections, regular of order $h$, with $q(c)=n$;
that the listed graph satisfies both conditions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:criterion}, so it is $[1,c]_T$ with its covers;
and that the chains form a symmetric chain decomposition of it.
Table~\ref{tab:finite} records the sizes.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:six}
For each of $H_4,G_{33},G_{34},E_6,E_7,E_8$, the supplied certificates
give a symmetric chain decomposition of $\NC(W,c)$ for the chosen
Coxeter element $c$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The exact checks of Appendix~\ref{app:checks} establish the hypotheses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:criterion} and every condition in the definition
of a symmetric chain decomposition. All six certificates pass these
checks.
\end{proof}

\section{From six types to all groups}\label{sec:reduction}
We assemble the finite inputs with the published cases, and prove the
two elementary transfer steps needed for the full statement.

\subsection{Irreducible groups}
Write $G(r,p,m)$ for the monomial group whose nonzero entries are
$r$th roots of unity and whose product of nonzero entries is an
$(r/p)$th root of unity, where $p\mid r$. The Shephard--Todd
classification \cite{ST}, as summarized in \cite[\S2.2]{Muhle} (which
credits Orlik and Solomon's Table~2 \cite{OS1980}), gives the list of
irreducible well-generated groups below.
Each group in the list has a symmetric chain decomposition of
$\NC(W,c)$ for one Coxeter element $c$, as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item $G(1,1,m)=S_m$, of essential rank $m-1$ in its permutation
  representation. Its noncrossing interval is the classical
  lattice~\cite[Section~4.1]{BessisDual}, and the decomposition is
  Simion--Ullman's theorem \cite[Theorem~2]{SU}, restated in
  \cite[Theorem~3.6 and Corollary~3.7]{Muhle}.
\item $G(d,1,m)$ with $d\geq2$. Bessis and Corran's poset of
  noncrossing partitions of type $(d,1,m)$ \cite[Definition~1.11]{BC}
  is isomorphic to the one for $d=2$ (their paragraph after
  Corollary~8.2) and is the lattice of simple elements of a Garside
  structure for the braid group of $G(d,1,m)$ (their remark at the end
  of \S7). M\"uhle states the resulting
  isomorphism $\NC(G(2,1,m))\cong\NC(G(d,1,m))$ for all $d\geq2$,
  crediting Bessis and Corran
  \cite[introduction, paragraph before Theorem~1.3]{Muhle}. For the
  identification of the type-$B$ interval with Reiner's centrally
  symmetric partitions see \cite[Theorem~4.9]{BW},
  \cite[Section~4.2]{BessisDual} and
  \cite[Section~1.4, after Lemma~1.12]{BC}. Reiner's Theorem~13
  supplies the chain decomposition \cite{Reiner}.
\item $G(d,d,m)$ with $d,m\geq2$: \cite[Theorem~1.3]{Muhle}. The group
  $G(2,2,2)$ is reducible, a product of two groups of order two, and is
  also covered by Lemma~\ref{lem:product}.
\item Rank one: the group is cyclic, all its nonidentity elements are
  reflections, and the interval is the single chain $1<c$.
\item The $26$ exceptional types, with indices
\[
 4,5,6,8,9,10,14,16,17,18,20,21,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,30,
 32,33,34,35,36,37.
\]
Proposition~4.2 of \cite{Muhle} covers those of rank at most four
other than $G_{30}=H_4$. From the classification, these are the twenty
types
\[
 4,5,6,8,9,10,14,16,17,18,20,21,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,32;
\]
in particular $G_{32}$ has rank four. The remaining six are exactly
those in Proposition~\ref{prop:six}.
\end{itemize}
The rank-zero interval is a singleton.

M\"uhle's Theorem~1.3 gives a \emph{symmetric Boolean decomposition}:
a partition into intervals isomorphic to Boolean lattices whose bottom
and top ranks sum to the rank of the whole. Each such interval is a
product of two-element chains, so the product construction of
Lemma~\ref{lem:product} refines it into symmetric chains; this is the
Boolean-to-chain implication used in the cited family cases
\cite[Observation~10]{Petersen}, as credited in
\cite[Proposition~2.1]{Muhle}.

\subsection{Every choice of Coxeter element}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} allows all Coxeter elements in the regular-element
sense. By Reiner--Ripoll--Stump \cite[Proposition~1.4]{RRS},
for any two Coxeter elements $c,c'$ of an irreducible well-generated
group there is a group automorphism $\phi$ with
$\phi(T)=T$ and $\phi(c)=c'$. Applying $\phi$ to a shortest
reflection word, and then doing the same with $\phi^{-1}$, proves
\[
                       \ell_T(\phi(w))=\ell_T(w).
\]
The defining equality for absolute order now gives
\[
 u\leq_T v\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \phi(u)\leq_T\phi(v).
\]
Thus $\phi$ restricts to a rank-preserving order isomorphism
$[1,c]_T\to[1,c']_T$, as in \cite[Corollary~1.5]{RRS}.
An order isomorphism preserves covers,
partitions, and endpoint ranks; it carries a symmetric chain
decomposition to one for $c'$.

\subsection{Products of groups and chains}
A finite complex reflection group is the direct product of irreducible
reflection groups acting on the summands of a decomposition of $V$
into irreducible representations \cite[\S1, p.~817]{Bessis}; by
Section~\ref{sec:overview} the trivial summand $\Fix(W)$ may be
dropped. It therefore suffices to treat $W=W_1\times W_2$ acting on
$V_1\oplus V_2$ with $\Fix(W_i)=0$, and to induct on the number of
factors. For this essential product representation, fixed-space
codimensions add. A reflection of $W$ is consequently exactly $(t,1)$
or $(1,u)$ with $t$ or $u$ a reflection in its factor.

The factors of a well-generated group are well-generated. Let $n_i$
be the essential rank of $W_i$, and suppose $W$ is generated by
$n_1+n_2$ reflections. Each generator lies in one factor, so the
generators in $W_i$ generate $W_i$. A group generated by $k$
reflections fixes pointwise the intersection of their reflecting
hyperplanes in $V_i$, which has codimension at most $k$; since
$\Fix(W_i)=0$ has codimension $n_i$, at least $n_i$ generators lie in
$W_i$. The split is therefore exactly
$n_1$ and $n_2$, and each factor is well-generated.

 Projecting any reflection word and concatenating shortest
factor words give the two inequalities proving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:productlength}
 \ell_T((x,y))=\ell_{T_1}(x)+\ell_{T_2}(y).
\end{equation}
For each factor, the triangle inequality says
\[
 \ell_{T_i}(x_i)+\ell_{T_i}(x_i^{-1}c_i)\geq\ell_{T_i}(c_i).
\]
By \eqref{eq:productlength}, the absolute-order equality for the
product holds exactly when both factor inequalities are equalities.
The same calculation for two arbitrary product elements shows that
their absolute order is coordinatewise. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intervalproduct}
 [1,(c_1,c_2)]_T=[1,c_1]_{T_1}\times[1,c_2]_{T_2}
\end{equation}
as ranked posets, with ranks added.

The standard product construction is due to de Bruijn, van Ebbenhorst
Tengbergen, and Kruyswijk \cite{BTK}; see also Srinivasan's mention
\cite[\S1]{Srinivasan}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:product}
The product of two finite graded posets with symmetric chain
decompositions has a symmetric chain decomposition.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Partition the product into products of factor chains. Take chains
with bottom ranks $p,q$ and lengths in steps $a,b$. Their product
is a grid $[0,a]\times[0,b]$ whose element $(i,j)$ has rank
$p+q+i+j$. For each integer $0\leq k\leq\min(a,b)$ take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grid}
 (k,0),(k,1),\ldots,(k,b-k),
 (k+1,b-k),\ldots,(a,b-k).
\end{equation}
Empty final portions are omitted. A vertex $(i,j)$ lies on exactly
the chain with label $\min(i,b-j)$: when $i\leq b-j$ it is on
the vertical portion, and when $i>b-j$ it is on the horizontal
portion. These chains therefore partition the grid.

Each step increments exactly one coordinate and is a cover in its
factor, so is a cover in the product poset. The endpoint ranks are
$p+q+k$ and $p+q+a+b-k$. Their sum is
$(2p+a)+(2q+b)$, the sum of the factor ranks by symmetry of the
factor chains. Thus every chain is symmetric in the product.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Every irreducible factor of $W$ is well-generated. The classification
reduction, Proposition~\ref{prop:six}, and the
automorphism argument give the assertion for every irreducible factor
and every Coxeter choice. Equations~\eqref{eq:productlength}
and~\eqref{eq:intervalproduct} identify a reducible group's interval
with the ranked product of its factor intervals. Repeated application
of Lemma~\ref{lem:product} gives the required decomposition.
The empty product is the rank-zero singleton, whose singleton chain
is symmetric.
\end{proof}

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:checking}
The finite group calculations used GAP 4.15.1 \cite{GAP}, with
SmallGrp 1.5.4 \cite{SmallGrp}. Arithmetic is exact in the rational and
cyclotomic fields specified in Appendix~\ref{app:models}. The interval
generation and the reflection and interval checks of
Section~\ref{sec:models} are GAP programs; the two chain checkers are
written in Python and JavaScript.

The Lean theorem \texttt{IntervalCertificate.exact\_interval} proves
the interval equality in the following exact form. Let $E$ be a relation
on a type $V$, let $q:V\to\mathbb N$, let $S$ be a predicate on $V$,
let $c,z\in V$ and $n\in\mathbb N$. Write $x\xrightarrow[E]{k}y$
for an $E$-walk of length $k$, and put
\[
 J_n(x)\ \Longleftrightarrow\
 \exists k,l\in\mathbb N,\quad k+l=n,\quad
 c\xrightarrow[E]{k}x\xrightarrow[E]{l}z.
\]
Its statement is
\[
\begin{gathered}
 \bigl(\forall x,y,\ E(x,y)\Rightarrow q(x)\leq q(y)+1\bigr),\qquad
 q(c)=n,\quad q(z)=0,\quad S(c),\\
 \bigl(\forall x,y,\ S(x)\land E(x,y)\land q(y)+1=q(x)
                 \Rightarrow S(y)\bigr),\\
 \bigl(\forall x,\ S(x)\Rightarrow J_n(x)\bigr)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \forall x,\ S(x)\Longleftrightarrow J_n(x).
\end{gathered}
\]
Here $E$ is right multiplication by a reflection, $z=1$, and
$q(w)=\rank(I-w)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:walk} identifies $J_n$ with
$[1,c]_T$; the two certificate conditions in
Proposition~\ref{prop:criterion} supply the word witnesses and closure.
The cover argument remains the written proof of that proposition.

Appendix~\ref{app:lean} lists the other Lean lemmas. The link with
reflection groups is stated in Lean but not proved there; the six
group computations are checked in GAP, and the classification and
family results are cited, not formalized.

\section{Exact reflection-group models}\label{app:models}
The coordinates below specify the representations used for the finite
checks. They use the Coxeter representations described by Drew
Armstrong \cite{Armstrong}, the invariant degrees tabulated by David
Bessis \cite[Table~2]{Bessis}, and the Coxeter--Todd root coordinates
\cite{CT1953} with the named generators described by Manabu Oura and
Jiro Sekiguchi \cite{OS}, building on John Conway and Neil Sloane
\cite{CS}.

For $H_4$, $E_6$, $E_7$, and $E_8$ we use their geometric Coxeter
representations \cite[\S2.1 and Figure~2.1]{Armstrong}. Let $s_i$
be the simple reflection corresponding to a simple root $\alpha_i$.
For $i\ne j$, the Coxeter parameter $m_{ij}$ is the label of the
Coxeter diagram: $2$ for nonadjacent nodes, $3$ for an unlabeled edge,
and the edge label otherwise; set $m_{ii}=1$. The order of $s_is_j$
then equals $m_{ij}$. The form has
$B(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)=-\cos(\pi/m_{ij})$, in particular
$B(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)=1$, and the simple reflection
is $v\mapsto v-2B(\alpha_i,v)\alpha_i$. The $H_4$ diagram is the
path with edge labels $5,3,3$. The $E_n$ diagram has branching-arm
lengths $2,1,n-4$. Closing the simple roots under these reflections
gives an exact finite root set; the action on it is faithful because
it spans the representation. A product of the simple reflections is
the chosen Coxeter element.

For the two nonreal types, put $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and
$\theta=1+2\omega$. The root vectors are the coordinate permutations
of
\[
 \pm\theta(\omega^a,-\omega^b,0,0,0,0)
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \pm(\omega^{a_1},\ldots,\omega^{a_6}),
 \qquad \sum_i a_i\equiv0\pmod3,
\]
where all exponents lie in $\{0,1,2\}$. These are the
Coxeter--Todd coordinates presented in \cite[\S2]{OS} and
\cite[\S2.3, Table~2]{CS}. Each vector $v$ has squared norm $6$.
In row-vector convention its reflection matrix is
\[
                  s_v=I-\frac{\overline v^{\,t}v}{3}.
\]
Shephard--Todd's six named reflections~\cite[p.~298]{ST},
$P_1,P_2,P_3,Q_1,R_1,R_2$, generate
$G_{34}$; omitting $P_3$ gives $G_{33}$
\cite[\S2 and \S6.1]{OS}. The fixed hyperplanes, in this order, are
\[
 x_2=x_3,\quad x_3=x_4,\quad x_4=x_5,\quad
 x_1=x_2,\quad x_1=\omega x_2,\quad \sum_i x_i=0.
\]
These hyperplanes specify the correspondence with the named generators,
rather than merely identifying a group by its order. The $G_{33}$
model retains a fixed line in six coordinates; its essential rank is
five. This fixed summand changes neither $q$ nor reflection length.

Each model acts by permutations of its exact root list. A vertex label
is the list of images of an ordered spanning basis, expressed as root
indices. It determines a unique matrix. The second checker of Appendix~\ref{app:checks} reconstructs
that matrix, checks the action on every root, and verifies membership in
the generated permutation group.

\section{Details of the exceptional checks}\label{app:checks}
\subsection*{Reflections and Coxeter elements}
The root formulas provide a candidate reflection set. Its completeness
is checked independently of the formula: the checker computes all
conjugacy classes of the generated group, tests $\rank(I-g)$ on every
class representative, and requires every member of every rank-one class
to occur in the candidate set. Rank is constant on a conjugacy class,
and the class sizes must sum to the group order. Conversely, it checks
that each candidate is a distinct element of the group of order two
with $\rank(I-t)=1$, and that the number of candidates equals the total
size of the rank-one classes. Thus this step establishes
the entire reflection set, subject to the exact group algorithms
described in Section~\ref{sec:checking}. In these six groups the
reflections are involutions; no such restriction is imposed on the
published family inputs.

The selected element $c$ is given as a word in $n$ reflections.
The checker verifies $q(c)=n$, its order $h$, and an exact eigenvector
for $e^{2\pi i/h}$ that is fixed by none of the reflections. This last
test puts the eigenvector outside every reflecting hyperplane, so
establishes regularity. The invariant degrees, and hence the relevant
values of $h$, are taken from \cite[Table~2]{Bessis}; they are not
inferred from an observed group order.

\subsection*{Interval and chain verification}
For each group, one program starts from $c$ and tests every right
multiplication by a reflection, retaining exactly the steps that lower
$q$ by one; it does not read the chain partition. A second, separately
written checker reconstructs the listed elements from their labels and
computes ranks by its own exact row elimination. For every vertex $x$ and every
reflection $t$, it checks the downward-closure condition and requires
that the recorded edges are exactly all resulting pairs.

The checker also verifies paths from $c$ to every vertex and from every
vertex to $1$. Every edge lowers $q$ by one, so these paths have
total length $n$. All hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:criterion} are
therefore checked. The resulting graph is the full absolute-order
interval with its actual covers.

Two further, separately written chain checkers read the chains and
the interval graphs just verified. They require nonempty chains, every
vertex exactly once, recorded covers between successive ranks, and
endpoint ranks summing to $n$: precisely a symmetric chain
decomposition. Section~\ref{sec:checking} names the software.

\section{Scope of the Lean development}\label{app:lean}
Besides \texttt{IntervalCertificate.exact\_interval}
(Section~\ref{sec:checking}), the Lean development, which uses only
Lean's standard library, covers the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Lean proves, for abstract posets and groups: the grid construction
  of Lemma~\ref{lem:product}; that a bijection preserving ranks and
  covers carries a symmetric chain decomposition to one; that a
  reflection-preserving group isomorphism preserves reflection length
  and absolute order; the additivity \eqref{eq:productlength} for a
  product whose reflections lie in one factor; the split of the $26$
  exceptional indices into the twenty cited types and the six certified
  ones; and the one-element empty product.
\item Lean states, but does not prove, the link with reflection groups:
  the overall statement is written as a specification with the
  hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:overview}.
\item The six group computations are checked in GAP, not in Lean, and
  the classification and family results are cited, not formalized.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Further related work}
Two related questions are not addressed here. Drew Armstrong asked
whether $\NC^{(k)}(W)$ is strongly Sperner for every finite Coxeter
group $W$ and positive integer $k$
\cite[Section~3.5, Open Problem~3.5.12]{Armstrong}; for $k=1$ this is
M\"uhle's Theorem~1.4, and the chains here concern only $k=1$. Nate
Harman, Rylan Rosas, Charles Troutman~III, and Chris Wu introduce
strong $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-actions and conjecture their equivalence with
symmetric chain decompositions
\cite[Definition~1.1 and Conjecture~1.2]{HRTW}; the chains here are
constructed directly.

Recent related work includes Michael Dougherty and Gina Root's
symmetric chain results under conditions on planar hull configurations
\cite[Theorem~A]{DR}, and Alice Gao, Xin-Bei Liu, Arthur Yang, and James Zhao's
real-rooted symmetric decompositions of the $h$-polynomials of order
complexes of ordinary and Fuss noncrossing partition posets for finite
Coxeter groups (interlacing for Fuss parameter at least two)
\cite[Theorem~1.5]{GLYZ}. The former concerns a geometric class of partition
lattices; the latter decomposes polynomials rather than the elements
of an absolute-order interval. These results address different
extensions of classical noncrossing-partition structure.

Further neighboring work on noncrossing partitions and absolute order
includes Christian Gaetz and Yibo Gao (2020)
on the Sperner property of the absolute order \cite{GG2020}; Colin Defant
and Nathan Williams (2026) on full-twist and noncrossing models
\cite{DW2026}; Thomas Gobet (2026) on absolute moved spaces
\cite{Gobet2026}; and Eugenio Cainelli, Giovanni Paolini and Riccardo
Zaccaria (2026) on quasi-Coxeter intervals \cite{CPZ2026}.
