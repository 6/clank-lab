\begin{abstract}
A convex polytope is uniform if its symmetries act transitively on its
vertices and its facets are uniform, down to regular polygons.
We give a computer-assisted completeness proof for the known convex
uniform 5-polytopes: Wythoff constructions of types $A_5$, $B_5$ and $D_5$,
prisms over convex uniform 4-polytopes, and products of regular polygons
with convex uniform polyhedra.
The theorem holds conditional on the cited 3-D and Conway--Guy--Möller
4-D classifications, the correctness of the written reductions in
Sections~\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:caseB}, and the correctness of the
certificate checkers.
For faces with at most 16 sides, enumeration covers all possible vertex
neighborhoods, identifies 628 known polytopes and rules out one additional
candidate; a face with at least 17 sides forces a product.
This extends Möller's method to dimension five and produces no new examples.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Uniform polytopes}

All polytopes are convex. A \emph{polygon} is uniform if it is regular.
For $d\ge3$, a $d$-polytope $P$ is \emph{uniform} if its symmetry group
$\Sym(P)$ acts transitively on its vertices and each of its facets is a
uniform $(d-1)$-polytope. Then every face of $P$ is uniform (faces of
facets are faces), and all edges have the same length: the edges of a
uniform polytope have one length by induction on the dimension, two facets
sharing a ridge share its edges, and the facet graph (facets adjacent
through ridges) is connected. We always scale to \emph{unit edges}.
Uniform 3-polytopes are the uniform polyhedra, uniform 4-polytopes are
also called uniform polychora, and uniform 5-polytopes uniform polytera.
The Lean predicates \texttt{Uniform5.Composition.Uniform} and
\texttt{UnitUniform} in
\file{lean/Uniform5/CompositionGeometry.lean} use this same geometric
definition and unit-edge normalization.

Notation used throughout:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $\{p\}$ is the regular $p$-gon with unit edges, of circumradius
  $r_p = 1/(2\sin(\pi/p))$; the distance between the two neighbors of a
  vertex of $\{p\}$ is $\ch_p:= 2\cos(\pi/p)$.
\item $P_q=\{q\}\times[0,1]$ is the $q$-gonal prism ($P_4$ is the cube) and
  $A_q$ the $q$-gonal antiprism ($A_3$ is the octahedron), with unit edges.
  The height of $A_q$ is $h_A(q)$ with
  $h_A(q)^2 = 1-\frac{1}{4\cos^2(\pi/(2q))}$, and its circumradius is
  $R_{A_q}$ with $R_{A_q}^2=r_q^2+h_A(q)^2/4$.
\item For polytopes $X\subset\R^a$, $Y\subset\R^b$ the product
  $X\times Y\subset\R^{a+b}$ is again a polytope; $\{p\}\times\{q\}$ is a
  \emph{duoprism}, $K\times\{\}:=K\times[0,1]$ a \emph{prism}. A product of
  uniform polytopes with unit edges is uniform with unit edges.
\item $\Sym$, $\Stab$, $\aff$, $\conv$, $\relint$ have their usual
  meanings; $\VF$ denotes a vertex figure (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Wythoffian polytopes {\cite{Wythoff1918,Coxeter1935}}]\label{def:wythoff}
Let $W$ be a finite irreducible Coxeter group of rank $k$ acting on
$\R^k$, with simple roots $\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{k-1}$ (nodes of the
Coxeter diagram), and let $\emptyset\ne J\subseteq\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ (the
\emph{ringed} nodes). Let $x_J$ be the point at distance $1/2$ from the
mirrors of the ringed nodes and on the mirrors of the others, on the
positive side of all of them. The \emph{Wythoffian polytope}
$W_J=\conv(W x_J)$ is uniform with unit edges. We name it by the group and
the ring pattern, e.g.\ $B_5$ \texttt{00001}; node orders are those of
Table~\ref{tab:names} in Appendix~\ref{app:names}.
\end{definition}

The two non-Wythoffian non-prismatic convex uniform 4-polytopes are the
\emph{snub 24-cell} (the 600-cell minus the vertices of an inscribed
24-cell) and the \emph{grand antiprism} (the 600-cell minus two great
decagons in orthogonal planes) \cite{ConwayGuy1965}.

\subsection{The theorem}

This paper presents a completeness statement with explicit computational
lemmas and reproduction commands. It is a specialist classification
result about the known five-dimensional list, not a classification in
arbitrary dimension or a new general method. It is a
computer-assisted result conditional on the cited 3-D and 4-D
classifications (Facts~\ref{fact:3d} and~\ref{fact:4d}); the separate Lean
composition checks an implication from named inputs, with the limits
stated in Section~\ref{sec:lean}.

\begin{maintheorem}\label{thm:main}
Assume Facts~\ref{fact:3d} and~\ref{fact:4d}.
Every convex uniform 5-polytope is congruent, after scaling to unit edges,
to one of the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(K\arabic*)},leftmargin=3em]
\item a Wythoffian polytope $W_J$ of one of the Coxeter groups $A_5$,
  $B_5$, $D_5$ (19 distinct $A_5$ forms, 31 $B_5$ forms
  and 8 further $D_5$ forms);
\item a prism $K\times\{\}$ over a convex uniform 4-polytope $K$;
  45 Wythoffian bases, plus the snub 24-cell and grand antiprism, give 47
  prisms, of which 46 are not already in (K1), since the tesseract prism
  is the 5-cube;
\item a product $\{p\}\times Q$ of a regular polygon, $p\ge3$, with a
  convex uniform polyhedron $Q$: a Platonic or Archimedean solid, a prism
  $P_q$ (giving the \emph{duoprism prisms}
  $\{p\}\times\{q\}\times\{\}$), or an antiprism $A_q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{maintheorem}

The descriptions overlap (the 5-cube is a $B_5$ Wythoffian, the prism over
the tesseract and $\{4\}\times$cube; prisms over polyhedral prisms,
antiprism prisms and duoprisms are of the form (K3)). The theorem asserts
coverage, not disjointness. Counting the Coxeter--Johnson constructions
\cite{Coxeter1935,CoxeterRP,Johnson1966} with the four-dimensional bases
of \cite{Moller2004} gives $19+31+8+(47-1)=104$ in this convention:
the tesseract prism is counted once as the 5-cube. The older Open Problem
Garden total of 105 \cite{OPG} has not been reconciled with that convention;
it is not used by the proof. The proof instead generates every ring pattern
and product and tests congruence (Section~\ref{sec:known}). Every polytope in (K1)--(K3)
is convex and uniform, so the theorem is a classification. It is the
union of two theorems, proved in Sections~\ref{sec:caseA}
and~\ref{sec:caseB}:

\begin{theorem}[Case A]\label{thm:A}
Every convex uniform 5-polytope all of whose 2-faces have at most 16 sides
is congruent to one of the 628 distinct polytopes of the list $\mathcal
K_{16}$ of Section~\ref{sec:known} (all members of (K1)--(K3) whose
polygons have at most 16 sides).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Case B]\label{thm:B}
Every convex uniform 5-polytope with a 2-face of at least 17 sides is
congruent to $\{n\}\times Q$ with $Q$ a convex uniform polyhedron, or to
$\{q\}\times A_n$, for integers $n\ge17$ and $q\ge3$.
\end{theorem}

Both conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:B} are of type (K3). So
Theorems~\ref{thm:A} and~\ref{thm:B} give the Main Theorem.

\subsection{History and status}

Gosset \cite{Gosset1900} enumerated the semiregular polytopes (regular
facets) in all dimensions; G.~Blind and R.~Blind \cite{Blind1991}
proved that list complete. Elte's 1912 monograph \cite{Elte1912} is another
early treatment of higher-dimensional semiregular polytopes. Coxeter developed the Wythoff construction and the theory of
uniform polytopes \cite{CoxeterRP,CoxeterLHM1954}, and Johnson's thesis
\cite{Johnson1966} set up the general theory. The convex uniform polyhedra
are the Platonic and Archimedean solids, prisms and antiprisms; the list of
all uniform polyhedra, convex or not, was shown complete by Sopov
\cite{Sopov1970} and Skilling \cite{Skilling1975}. In dimension four Conway and Guy announced in 1965 a
computer-assisted completeness proof, finding one new non-Wythoffian
polytope, the grand antiprism \cite{ConwayGuy1965}; a complete account
of a computer-assisted proof is Möller's thesis
\cite{Moller2004thesis,Moller2004}: 1000
edge neighborhoods, each with a forced circumradius, and 64 vertex
neighborhoods, each matched with a known polychoron, with the prism and
antiprism families treated by hand.

The present proof builds directly on Möller's classification and its
edge-neighborhood, forced-radius and vertex-neighborhood enumeration
strategy. The published three-dimensional classification supplies the
possible polyhedral faces, and Conway--Guy and Möller supply the possible
four-dimensional facets. Coxeter's and Johnson's constructions supply the
known families whose completeness is at issue here. These foundational
classifications and constructions are credited inputs; the contribution of
this paper is the conditional five-dimensional completeness argument and
its checked computations.

Schulte's 2017 Handbook chapter states that ``a complete list is known
only for $d=4$'' \cite{Schulte2017}. Winter's Appendix E.3 states:
``it is unknown whether there exist non-prismatic non-Wythoffian uniform
polytopes in dimension five or above.'' \cite[p.~167]{Winter2021}
The Open Problem Garden and community catalogs also record the question
as open \cite{OPG,PolytopeWiki}. These sources support the status of the
problem; they are not completeness premises.

Johnson's 1966 thesis \cite{Johnson1966} develops constructions in
arbitrary dimension, including dimension five; its discussion of known
enumeration (pp.~226, 291--296) does not establish completeness of the
convex five-dimensional list.
Johnson's unpublished 1991 manuscript \cite{Johnson1991} is cited by
Schulte for the four-dimensional list; its full text is unavailable, so
no five-dimensional completeness claim is attributed to it here.
Bowers's and Klitzing's catalogs \cite{Bowers,Klitzing} document known
examples and nomenclature; Bowers's search includes nonconvex polytera
and is not presented here as an exhaustive search of convex examples.
Neither catalog is a completeness premise, and the construction lists
used here are generated from Wythoff and Cartesian-product recipes.

The verification design was developed with Càllisto's honeycomb work
\cite{CallistoUniform} as a known methodological precedent, including its

The geometric enumeration method used here is Möller's.

\subsection{Dependence on the 3- and 4-dimensional classifications}
\label{sec:inputs}

The proof uses two classifications as cited inputs. They enter only
through the following two facts.

\begin{fact}[3-D classification {\cite[Section 18.5]{Schulte2017}, \cite{Grunbaum2009}}]\label{fact:3d}
A convex uniform polyhedron is a Platonic solid, one of the 13
Archimedean solids, a prism $P_q$ ($q\ge3$) or an antiprism $A_q$
($q\ge4$). It is determined up to congruence by its vertex configuration
(the cyclic sequence of polygon sizes around a vertex). In particular a
convex uniform polyhedron with a $q$-gonal face, $q\notin\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$,
is $P_q$ or $A_q$.
\end{fact}

\begin{fact}[4-D classification {\cite{ConwayGuy1965,Moller2004thesis}}]\label{fact:4d}
A convex uniform 4-polytope is one of: the Wythoffians of $A_4$, $B_4$,
$D_4$, $F_4$, $H_4$; the snub 24-cell; the grand antiprism; a prism
$Q\times\{\}$ over a convex uniform polyhedron $Q$; a duoprism
$\{p\}\times\{q\}$. In particular a convex uniform 4-polytope with a
$q$-gonal 2-face, $q\notin\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$, is a duoprism
$\{q\}\times\{p\}$ (including $P_q\times\{\}=\{q\}\times\{4\}$) or the
antiprism prism $A_q\times\{\}$.
\end{fact}

The vertex-transitivity in our definition matters: the
pseudo-rhombicuboctahedron (elongated square gyrobicupola) has congruent
vertex configurations but is not vertex-transitive, and is not uniform
\cite{Grunbaum2009}. Fact~\ref{fact:4d} is itself computer-assisted. As a
check of the method, the certified shared pipeline in dimension four
recovers all 152 known objects at family cutoff 14, including the grand
antiprism and snub 24-cell (Section~\ref{sec:control}). This bounded positive control is
not a link of the 5-D proof or a proof of the cited all-parameter 4-D
classification.

\subsection{Strategy}

Let $P$ be a convex uniform 5-polytope with unit edges and circumradius
$R$. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} proves the reduction: $P$ is inscribed in a
sphere; around every 2-face $G$ the facets form a cycle, and $R$ is a root
of an explicit \emph{closure equation} of that cycle; at a vertex $v$ the
neighbors of $v$ span the vertex figure $\VF$, a 4-polytope inscribed in
a 3-sphere whose facets are the vertex figures of the facets of $P$ at
$v$; two distinct vertices are at least one edge apart (the separation
lemma), which lets interval enclosures decide vertex identity when they
are narrow enough (and report it as undecided otherwise);
and the star of one vertex, if it forces the stars of its neighbors,
forces $P$.

\emph{Case A} (Section~\ref{sec:caseA}): all polygons have at most 16
sides. Then every facet belongs to a finite library of 181 congruence
classes of uniform 4-polytopes. Stage~1 enumerates every cycle of library
facets around a polygon and certifies all roots of its closure equation
(14,965 cycles); radii that can occur together form 563 \emph{radius
classes} after a combinatorial filter. Stage~2 enumerates candidate
signatures covering every actual vertex figure in each class (629 in
total). Stage~3 covers every actual star by retained encodings, certifies
the scalar-root assertion, and proves identification and forcing
conditional on realization. For 628 encodings, any represented actual
star is identified with that of a separately constructed known polytope
$Q$ and forces $P\cong Q$. The one extra candidate is excluded from every
convex uniform polytope by a development that gets stuck; no exact local
realization of that candidate is asserted.

Here \textbf{181 counts four-dimensional facet types}, not
five-dimensional polytopes. At the same polygon cutoff the known 5-D
list has 890 names representing 628 congruence classes
(Section~\ref{sec:known}); without the cutoff it includes infinite
families. The facet library is checked by exact construction and incidence
checks and by a separate rebuild in \file{checks/independent-s1-b2/}
(Computation~\ref{comp:link}). Neither rebuild proves Möller's
classification anew.

\emph{Case B} (Section~\ref{sec:caseB}): some polygon $F$ has $n\ge17$
sides. Around $F$ only three cycle types are possible, each forcing $R$ by
a formula in $n$ and finitely many parameters. A certified computation
(B2) shows that this $R$ is never a root of any Case-A cycle, so every
polygon at a vertex lies in a facet containing a large polygon. A written
structural argument, with two more certified computations ((P1) and (W)),
then forces $P$ to be a product.

\paragraph{Why the cutoff is 16/17.}\label{par:cutoff}
Seventeen is a sufficient, not an optimal, threshold: it exceeds every
exceptional polygon size in the lower-dimensional classifications and
satisfies $2\sin(2\pi/17)<1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:long}). The B2, P1 and W
certificates cover the entire remaining range. The small-face radius cover
has $\max\Omega=63.2195709614\ldots$; it includes closure roots that need
not extend to any polytope, so this is not the largest radius of a known
polytope with at most 16-sided faces. B2 checks $17\le n\le397$ and the
corresponding auxiliary parameters, then uses
$r_{398}>63.344>\max\Omega$ and monotonicity for the tails
(Section~\ref{sec:radii}).

The computations state the finite or interval claims they check and the
written arguments needed to interpret them. Section~\ref{sec:computations}
records shared dependencies; Section~\ref{sec:lean} states the formal scope.

\paragraph{What independence means here.}
Separate implementations share mathematical constructions, some input lists,
model certificates and arithmetic libraries. The exact-exclusion checks
also share the stage-1 construction code. P1's sampled tails are non-proof
checks; its proof uses written angle, chord and monotonicity arguments.
Section~\ref{sec:computations} specifies these dependencies.

\paragraph{Why this does not yet classify dimension six.}
In dimension six, the facets would be the five-dimensional families proved
complete here, but completeness of that input list alone does not supply a
finite search. The codimension-three faces are now polyhedra rather than
polygons; their neighborhoods require new incidence and metric data, and
the five-dimensional vertex figures make assembly and forcing larger.
Most importantly, the large-polygon fan argument of Case B does not classify
all unbounded families in dimension six. A new parameter reduction and
certified bounds would be needed before a finite replay could prove
completeness there.

\section{The reduction}\label{sec:prelim}
%======================================================================

Throughout this section $P$ is a convex uniform $d$-polytope with unit
edges, $d\ge3$ (we use $d=5$ from Section~\ref{sec:closure} on), with
vertex set $V$.

\subsection{Inscribed sphere and face centers}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sphere}
Let $c$ be the centroid of $V$. Then all vertices lie on the sphere of some
radius $R$ about $c$, and $c$ lies in the interior of $P$. For every face
$\Phi$ of $P$ the orthogonal projection $o_\Phi$ of $c$ onto $\aff\Phi$ is
the circumcenter of $\Phi$, and lies in the relative interior of $\Phi$.
If $\Phi$ has circumradius $R_\Phi$, then
$|c-o_\Phi|=\sqrt{R^2-R_\Phi^2}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every symmetry permutes $V$ and so fixes $c$; by vertex-transitivity all
vertices are equidistant from $c$. The centroid of the vertices of a
full-dimensional polytope is an interior point. The vertices of $\Phi$ lie
on the intersection of the sphere with $\aff\Phi$, a sphere in $\aff\Phi$
about $o_\Phi$; they affinely span $\aff\Phi$, so $o_\Phi$ is the unique
circumcenter of $\Phi$. Since $\Phi$ is uniform, applying the first part to
$\Phi$ shows that its circumcenter is its vertex centroid, which is
relatively interior. The last formula is Pythagoras.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The separation lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Separation lemma]\label{lem:sep}
Let $X$ be a convex polytope inscribed in a sphere, with at least three
vertices, let $v$ be a vertex and $w$ a vertex nearest to $v$ among the
other vertices. Then $[v,w]$ is an edge of $X$. Consequently, in a convex
uniform polytope with unit edges any two distinct vertices are at distance
at least $1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $B$ be the closed ball with diameter $[v,w]$, center $m$ and radius
$\rho=|v-w|/2$. If a vertex $x\notin\{v,w\}$ lay in $B$, the angle
$\angle vxw$ would be at least $90^\circ$, so
$|v-w|^2\ge|v-x|^2+|x-w|^2>|v-x|^2$, contradicting the choice of $w$. Let
$S$ be the circumsphere, with center $c$ and radius $R$, and consider the
difference of powers
$f(x)=(|x-m|^2-\rho^2)-(|x-c|^2-R^2)$, an affine function. It vanishes at
$v$ and $w$, and at every other vertex $x$ we have $|x-c|=R$ and
$|x-m|>\rho$, so $f(x)>0$. In particular $f$ is not constant, so
$H=\{f=0\}$ is a hyperplane, $X\subseteq\{f\ge0\}$, and $X\cap H$ is the
face $\conv\{v,w\}$. So $[v,w]$ is an edge. If all edges have length 1,
the nearest other vertex of any vertex is at distance 1, so all distances
between distinct vertices are at least 1.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Decision rule]\label{cor:decide}
Let $x,y$ be two vertices of one convex uniform polytope with unit edges
(possibly hypothetical), and let $[\ell,u]$ be a rigorous enclosure of
$|x-y|^2$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item if $\ell\le0$ and $u<1$, then $x=y$;
\item if $0<\ell$ and $u<1$, we have a contradiction (the configuration
  does not occur);
\item if $0<\ell$ (and $u\ge1$), then $x\ne y$;
\item if $\ell\le0$ and $u\ge1$, nothing is concluded: $x=y$ and $x\ne y$
  both remain possible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

Case (d) is \emph{undecided}. A search that meets it must either refine
the enclosure, keep both possibilities, or stop and report the branch as
open; treating the two points as distinct (or as equal) would not be
conservative. Every search used by the proof
handles (d) as undecided, and the full replays recorded below met it
zero times (Sections~\ref{sec:S2}, \ref{sec:U2} and~\ref{sec:U3}):
\texttt{cert\_vf.py} (\texttt{identify}) raises \texttt{Undecided}, counts
it, and refuses to return a complete class; \texttt{check\_u2} raises
\texttt{PrecisionError} and exits nonzero; \file{checks/check_u3} uses the
stricter equality bound $u<1/4$ and turns undecided cases into \texttt{OPEN},
never into a refutation.

\subsection{Vertex figures}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:vf}
Let $P$ be a convex uniform $d$-polytope with unit edges and circumradius
$R$, centered at $0$, and $v$ a vertex. The neighbors of $v$ (the other
ends of the edges at $v$) are exactly the vertices at distance $1$ from
$v$. They lie on the hyperplane $H=\{x: x\cdot v=R^2-\tfrac12\}$, every other
vertex except $v$ lies strictly on the side $x\cdot v<R^2-\frac12$, and
$\VF:=H\cap P=\conv(\text{neighbors of }v)$ is a $(d-1)$-polytope
inscribed in a sphere of radius $r=\sqrt{1-1/(4R^2)}$ about
$o_{\VF}=(1-\frac{1}{2R^2})v$. The faces of $\VF$ correspond to the faces
of $P$ containing $v$ (a $j$-face $\Phi\ni v$ gives the $(j-1)$-face
$H\cap\Phi$). For a facet $K\ni v$ of circumradius $R_K$, $\VF(K):=H\cap K$
is inscribed in a sphere of radius $r_K=\sqrt{1-1/(4R_K^2)}$ in a
hyperplane of $H$ at distance $\sqrt{r^2-r_K^2}$ from $o_{\VF}$. An edge of
$\VF$ coming from a $p$-gon has length $\ch_p=2\cos(\pi/p)$. The point
$o_{\VF}$ lies in the relative interior of $\VF$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:sep} the vertices at distance 1 from $v$ are neighbors,
and conversely neighbors are at distance 1. For a vertex $x$,
$|x-v|^2=2R^2-2x\cdot v$, so $|x-v|=1$ iff $x\cdot v=R^2-\frac12$, and
$|x-v|>1$ (every other vertex $\ne v$) iff $x\cdot v<R^2-\frac12$. Hence $H$
separates $v$ from all non-neighbors, and $H\cap P$ is the convex hull of
the neighbors; this is the standard vertex figure, whose face lattice is
that of the faces through $v$. A neighbor $x$ makes an angle $\theta$
with $v$ at the center with $2R\sin(\theta/2)=1$, so its distance from
the axis $\R v$ is $R\sin\theta=\cos(\theta/2)=\sqrt{1-1/(4R^2)}$. The
same computation inside $K$ gives $r_K$; the circumcenter of $\VF(K)$ is
the projection of $o_{\VF}$, which gives the distance. In a regular
$p$-gon the two neighbors of a vertex are $\ch_p$ apart. Finally, write
$o_{\VF}=t_0v$ with $t_0=1-1/(2R^2)$. Since $0$ is the centroid of the $N$
vertices, $q:=-v/(N-1)$ is the centroid of the vertices other than $v$, so
$q\in P$, and $q\cdot v\le R^2-\frac12$, since every other vertex $x$ has
$x\cdot v\le R^2-\frac12$, with equality exactly for the neighbors. If some
vertex other than $v$ is not a neighbor, the inequality is strict, i.e.\
$-1/(N-1)<t_0$; also $t_0<1$. Then $o_{\VF}$ lies on the open segment from
$q$ to $v$, which passes through the interior point $0$; every point of
this open segment is interior to $P$ (it lies strictly between the
interior point $0$ and a point of $P$, or is $0$). Hence $o_{\VF}$ is an
interior point of $P$ lying in $H$, i.e.\ a relative interior point of
$\VF=H\cap P$. If every vertex other than $v$ is a neighbor (as in the
5-simplex), then $\VF$ is the convex hull of all other vertices, $t_0=-1/(N-1)$
and $o_{\VF}=q$ is the centroid of the vertices of $\VF$, which is a
relative interior point of $\VF$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The center of the vertex figure need not lie on the segment from $v$
to the center of $P$: when $R<1/\sqrt2$, $t_0<0$; for the 5-simplex,
$R^2=5/12$ and $t_0=-1/5$. The centroid argument above handles both
cases, including 4-simplex facets when applied in Lemma~\ref{lem:D}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Cycles around a polygon and the closure equation}
\label{sec:closure}

From now on $d=5$. Let $G$ be a 2-face of $P$, a $p$-gon with circumcenter
$o_G$ and circumradius $\rho=r_p$, and let $N$ be the 3-dimensional
orthogonal complement of the direction space of $\aff G$. The faces of $P$
containing $G$ are cells (3-faces) and facets; modulo $\aff G$ the tangent
cone of $P$ at $G$ is a pointed polyhedral 3-cone in $N$, whose extreme
rays correspond to the cells $C\supset G$ and whose 2-faces correspond to
the facets $K\supset G$. Going around the cone gives a cyclic sequence
$(K_1,C_1,K_2,C_2,\dots,K_k,C_k)$, $k\ge3$, where facet $K_i$ contains the
cells $C_{i-1}$ and $C_i$ (indices mod $k$). Let $u_C\in N$ be the unit
vector of the ray of $C$ and $\alpha_i$ the dihedral angle of the
4-polytope $K_i$ at its 2-face $G$, between the cells $C_{i-1}$ and $C_i$;
this is the angle between $u_{C_{i-1}}$ and $u_{C_i}$. We call
$(p;K_1,C_1,\dots,K_k,C_k)$ the \emph{cycle} of $G$; cycles are taken up to
rotation and reversal.

\begin{lemma}[Closure equation]\label{lem:closure}
With this notation, let $w=c-o_G\in N$, $h=|w|=\sqrt{R^2-\rho^2}$, and for
each cell $C\supset G$ of circumradius $R_C$ let
$d_C=\sqrt{R_C^2-\rho^2}>0$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item $\sum_i\alpha_i<2\pi$;
\item $w\cdot u_C=d_C$, so the angle $\delta_C$ between $w$ and $u_C$
  satisfies $\cos\delta_C=d_C/h$;
\item $w$ lies strictly inside the cone spanned by the $u_C$, and with
  $\theta_i\in(0,\pi)$ the angle at $\hat w=w/h$ of the spherical triangle
  $(\hat w,u_{C_{i-1}},u_{C_i})$,
  \[
  \cos\theta_i=\frac{\cos\alpha_i-\cos\delta_{C_{i-1}}\cos\delta_{C_i}}
  {\sin\delta_{C_{i-1}}\sin\delta_{C_i}},\qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i=2\pi;
  \]
\item $R>R_{K}$ for every facet $K\supset G$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) The intersection of a pointed polyhedral 3-cone with the unit sphere
is a convex spherical polygon in an open hemisphere, whose side lengths
are the face angles $\alpha_i$; its perimeter is less than $2\pi$.
(b) By Lemma~\ref{lem:sphere} applied to $C$, $o_C$ is the projection of
$c$ on $\aff C$ and $o_G$ the projection of $o_C$ on $\aff G$; so
$o_C-o_G\in N$ points along the ray of $C$ (as $o_C\in\relint C$), has
length $d_C>0$, and $w-(o_C-o_G)$ is orthogonal to $\aff C$, hence to
$u_C$. (c) The tangent cone of $P$ at $G$ is the sum of the direction
space of $\aff G$ and a pointed cone $T_N\subset N$, and the interior of
$P$ lies in $o_G$ plus its interior; since $c$ is interior and $w=c-o_G\in
N$, $w$ lies in the interior of $T_N$, the cone spanned by the $u_C$. The triangles $(\hat w,u_{C_{i-1}},u_{C_i})$ are then
non-degenerate, their angles at $\hat w$ fill the full angle around
$\hat w$, and the formula is the spherical law of cosines for the side
$\alpha_i$ opposite $\hat w$. (d) $|c-o_K|=\sqrt{R^2-R_K^2}$ is the
distance from the interior point $c$ to the hyperplane of $K$, which is
positive.
\end{proof}

In the variable $s=1/R$ the cosines become
$\cos\delta_C(s)=d_C\,s/\sqrt{1-\rho^2s^2}$, increasing in $s$, and the
\emph{closure function} of the cycle is
\[
g(s)=\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i(s)-2\pi,\qquad 0\le s\le 1/R_{\max},
\]
where $R_{\max}=\max_iR_{K_i}$ and $s=0$ stands for $R=\infty$.
\begin{remark}\label{rem:clip}
On this domain $\delta_{C_{i-1}}+\delta_{C_i}\ge\alpha_i$, so the argument
of the arccosine is at least $-1$: at $s=1/R_{K_i}$ the point $\hat w$ is
the direction of $o_{K_i}-o_G$, which lies on the arc from $u_{C_{i-1}}$
to $u_{C_i}$ (equality), and the $\delta$'s decrease in $s$. If instead
$|\delta_{C_{i-1}}-\delta_{C_i}|>\alpha_i$ the triangle does not exist; on
such $s$ the configuration is impossible. The implementations clip the
lower end at $-1$ (sound by this remark) and discard boxes on which a
triangle is certified impossible.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Why the closure equation is not an identity.}
For an admissible cycle, $\theta_i(0)=\alpha_i$ and hence
$g(0)=\sum_i\alpha_i-2\pi<0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(a).
Thus the closure equation cannot hold identically on its radius domain.
For the finite library we have the stronger, checked monotonicity that
also rules out an accumulation of roots at an endpoint. Put
$a=d_A^2$, $b=d_B^2$, $D=R_K^2-\rho^2$ and $c=\cos\alpha$ for a word,
and let $u=1/(R^2-\rho^2)$. Its closure-angle cosine is
\[
 f(u)=\frac{c-\sqrt{ab}\,u}{\sqrt{(1-au)(1-bu)}}.
\]
Since $D>a,b>0$, the sign of $f'(u)$ on $[0,1/D]$ is the sign of
\[
 -\sqrt{ab}+\tfrac12c(a+b)
 +u\bigl(\tfrac12\sqrt{ab}(a+b)-cab\bigr).
\]
This is affine in $u$. The independent stage-1 certificate checks strict
negativity at both endpoints for all 558 library words; hence every
closure angle, and their sum, increases strictly in $u$ on the
nondegenerate domain. Each Case-A cycle has at most one admissible finite
root (Computation~\ref{comp:S1}). A flat cycle has $g(0)=0$ but its
closure function is still not identically zero: the same strict
monotonicity makes the closure sum exceed $2\pi$ for $u>0$. Flat cycles are excluded in any
event by convexity. For Case B, Section~\ref{sec:radii} gives the explicit
radius formulas and finite root equations. The finite-radius conclusion
therefore uses more than an appeal to analyticity alone.

Two kinds of cycles satisfy the closure equation only
spuriously and are excluded exactly:

\begin{lemma}[Exact exclusions]\label{lem:exclusions}
\textup{(Flat.)} A cycle with $\sum\alpha_i=2\pi$ exactly is not the cycle
of a 2-face of a convex 5-polytope. \textup{(Degenerate.)} A root at
$R=R_{\max}$ exactly does not give a convex uniform polytope.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(a) and (d).
\end{proof}

For a flat cycle, $g(0)=0$, the formal infinite-radius limit.
Degenerate roots put the center on a facet hyperplane. Interval
arithmetic cannot certify these equalities; they are proved exactly
(Computations~\ref{comp:S1} and~\ref{comp:exact}). A local angle-sum identity alone does not give a global honeycomb.

\subsection{Faces, simple vertices}

\begin{lemma}[Face lemma]\label{lem:face}
Let $X$ be a polytope, $\Phi$ a face of $X$ and $p,q$ vertices of
$\Phi$. If $[p,q]$ is an edge of $X$, it is an edge of $\Phi$. Conversely
every edge of $\Phi$ is an edge of $X$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$[p,q]\subseteq\Phi$ by convexity, and $[p,q]=[p,q]\cap\Phi$ is an
intersection of two faces of $X$, hence a face of $X$ contained in
$\Phi$, hence a face of $\Phi$. The converse holds because a face of a
face is a face.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Simple vertices]\label{lem:simple}
A vertex $x$ of degree 4 in a 4-polytope is simple: each facet containing
$x$ contains exactly three of the four edges at $x$, and each 3-subset of
the edges at $x$ is the set of edges at $x$ of exactly one facet.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The vertex figure of $x$ is a 3-polytope with four vertices, a
tetrahedron; its facets (all four triangles) correspond to the facets
through $x$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stars and local uniqueness}

The \emph{star} of a vertex $v$ is the set of facets of $P$ containing
$v$, each with its full vertex set.

\begin{lemma}[Local uniqueness gives global uniqueness]\label{lem:induction}
Let $S$ be the star of $v_0$ in some convex uniform 5-polytope, and
suppose: for every convex uniform 5-polytope $P$ with star $S$ at $v_0$
and every neighbor $w$ of $v_0$, the star of $P$ at $w$ is one and the
same set $\Sigma_w$, independent of $P$. Then any two convex uniform
5-polytopes $P_1$, $P_2$ with star $S$ at $v_0$ are equal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We show by induction on the edge distance from $v_0$ that $P_1$ and $P_2$
have the same vertices and the same stars there. Suppose $P_1$ and $P_2$
have the same star $\Sigma$ at a vertex $w$. By vertex-transitivity of
$P_1$ there is an isometry $g_1$ with $g_1(v_0)=w$ and $g_1(S)=\Sigma$.
Then $g_1^{-1}(P_1)$ and $g_1^{-1}(P_2)$ are convex uniform 5-polytopes
with star $S$ at $v_0$; by hypothesis they have the same star at every
neighbor $u'$ of $v_0$. Applying $g_1$, $P_1$ and $P_2$ have the same
star at every neighbor $u=g_1(u')$ of $w$ (the neighbors of $w$ are
read off the common star $\Sigma$). The edge graph of a polytope is
connected (Balinski \cite{Balinski1961}), and a polytope is the convex
hull of its vertices, so $P_1=P_2$.
\end{proof}

The induction is written out here because a symmetry of $P_1$ mapping
$v_0$ to $w$ need not be a symmetry of $P_2$; the hypothesis is applied to
$g_1^{-1}(P_i)$, not to $P_i$.

\subsection{The case split}

A 2-face is \emph{large} if it has at least 17 sides. \emph{Case A}: $P$
has no large 2-face. \emph{Case B}: $P$ has a large 2-face. The cases are
disjoint and exhaustive. Polygon sizes $7,9,11,\dots,16$ (which occur only
in prisms, antiprisms and duoprisms) belong to Case A, together with the
sizes $3,4,5,6,8,10$ of the non-prismatic polyhedra and 4-polytopes.

The following certificate supplies a certified correspondence from cells
and labels to every polygon-orbit and angle entry used by stage 1,
including the word-angle property.

\begin{lemma}[A word determines its angle]\label{lem:wordangle}
Let $G$ be a $p$-gon of a uniform 4-polytope $K$ (circumradius $R_K$),
between cells $A$, $B$ of circumradii $R_A$, $R_B$, and let $\rho=r_p$.
Then the dihedral angle of $K$ at $G$ is
\[
\alpha=\arccos\sqrt{\frac{R_A^2-\rho^2}{R_K^2-\rho^2}}
      +\arccos\sqrt{\frac{R_B^2-\rho^2}{R_K^2-\rho^2}} .
\]
In particular the word $(K,p,A,B)$ determines $\alpha$, even when its
polygon occurrences lie in different orbits of $\Sym(K)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the plane through the center $f$ of $G$ perpendicular to $G$, the two
cells give rays $u_A,u_B$ bounding a convex wedge of angle $\alpha$, and
the center $O$ of $K$ projects strictly inside it (Lemma~\ref{lem:sphere}
applied to $K$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(b),(c) one dimension down).
With $D=|O-f|^2=R_K^2-\rho^2$ we have $(O-f)\cdot u_A=\sqrt{R_A^2-\rho^2}>0$
and likewise for $B$, since the projection of $O$ on each cell is its
relatively interior circumcenter. The direction of $O-f$ lies between the
rays, so the two angles to it add to $\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Model constructions}\label{sec:models}

The computations use explicit constructions (``models'') of the library
facets and of the known 5-polytopes. It is not enough that these are \emph{named} correctly: the proof uses that
each constructed point set is the vertex set of a uniform polytope with
unit edges, with the stated facet types and complete vertex orbits, since
the separation lemma is applied to model vertices. These properties are
supplied as follows (\file{checks/model_audit/}).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{Wythoff constructions, antiprisms and products}
  (\file{checks/model_audit/wythoff_proof.md}): a written proof, from standard
  facts on finite Coxeter groups, of the supporting-face theorem, unit
  edges and recursive uniformity of $W_J$ for every binary ring pattern of
  the groups used, of the product and antiprism constructions, of the
  facet rule and of the named aliases; and an explicit contract under
  which an indexed interval orbit enumeration returns exactly the orbit,
  index by index.
\item \emph{Snub 24-cell and grand antiprism}: full convex-hull uniformity of
  the 96- and 100-vertex subsets of the 600-cell and containment of the
  actual indexed coordinates, checked by two independent implementations
  (rational-pair and number-field), each rejecting eight corruptions.
\item \emph{Snub cube and snub dodecahedron}: complete algebraic
  constructions with exact generator and index identities and coordinate
  containment, checked independently with interval/Krawczyk and
  rational-dyadic arithmetic, each rejecting nine corruptions.
\item \emph{Runtime guard}: \texttt{model\_guard.py} enforces in

\section{Case A: every 2-face has at most 16 sides}\label{sec:caseA}

In this section $P$ is a convex uniform 5-polytope with unit edges,
circumradius $R$, center $c$, all of whose 2-faces have at most 16 sides.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:A7} every facet of $P$ is congruent to a canonical type
of $\mathcal L_{16}$, and every cell is a uniform polyhedron with at most

\subsection{Stage 1: cycles and their radii}\label{sec:S1}

A \emph{library cycle} is a cycle $(p;K_1,C_1,\dots,K_k,C_k)$ with
$p\le16$, $k\ge3$, each $K_i$ a canonical type having a $p$-gon orbit
between cells of types $C_{i-1}$, $C_i$ (dihedral angle $\alpha_i$), and
$\sum\alpha_i$ not certified to exceed $2\pi$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:closure},
the cycle of every 2-face of $P$ is a library cycle. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:wordangle} the word $(K,p,A,B)$ of a polygon orbit
determines its dihedral angle, so the word $(p;K_1,C_1,\dots,K_k,C_k)$
determines the angles and the closure function; stage 2 and stage 3 treat
cycles as such words.

\begin{computation}[S1: stage 1]\label{comp:S1}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item The list $\mathcal C_{16}$ of library cycles, up to rotation and
  reversal, has 14,965 elements, and it is complete.
\item For each cycle the zeros of its closure function $g$ on
  $[0,1/R_{\max}]$ (with the domain extended to the upper end of the ball
  enclosing $1/R_{\max}$) lie in a listed finite set of \emph{root
  intervals}, formed by merging retained leaves of width below
  $10^{-20}$ in $s$; this threshold does not bound the width of a merged
  interval. Of the cycles, 14,428 have at
  least one root interval, 537 have none, and none is undecided.
\item Exactly 7,080 cycles have $\sum\alpha_i=2\pi$ exactly (flat), and
  exactly 57 root intervals are the root $R=R_{\max}$ exactly
  (degenerate); each is proved by exact algebra.
\end{enumerate}
\end{computation}

\noindent\emph{What is computed.} \texttt{cert\_stage1\_5d.py} (Arb,
160--200 bits) enumerates cycles as closed walks in the multigraph whose
vertices are cell types and whose edges are the $p$-gon orbits of the
facet types, pruning when the certified angle sum exceeds $2\pi$. The
smallest facet angle in the library is $\pi/3$ (in $\{3\}\times\{q\}$ at
its $q$-gons, the interior angle of the triangle), so a non-flat cycle has
at most 5 facets and a library cycle at most 6 (six angles $\pi/3$ are
flat); the search goes to 8.
On $[0,1/R_{\max}]$ the closure function is evaluated on boxes by ball
arithmetic, using the monotonicity of $\cos\delta_C(s)$ for the range of
each $\delta_C$ and the clipping of Remark~\ref{rem:clip}; a box is
discarded when the enclosure of $g$ excludes $0$ or a triangle is
certified impossible, and the remaining boxes are bisected below width
$10^{-20}$ and merged into root intervals. Adjacent retained leaves can
produce a merged interval wider than $10^{-20}$; no upper bound of that
size is needed for coverage or the later whole-class root check.
Root intervals are written as
outward-rounded 45-digit decimal bounds with a margin of $10^{-32}$; the
merge of adjacent boxes is exact.
\texttt{exact\_angles.py} proves flatness, on the exact cosines of
Computation~\ref{comp:exact} only: angles that are rational
multiples of $\pi$ are verified as such exactly (a rational-angle guess is
accepted only after an exact cosine identity), the others are combined
into the claim $\exp(i\sum\alpha_j)=1$, which is verified by an exact
minimal-polynomial zero test (sympy), and the ball of $\sum\alpha_j$
lies in $(\pi,3\pi)$; so $\sum\alpha_j=2\pi$. The output records 30/31/32 proved cosine tuples at
$P_{\mathrm{ctl}}=6/10/16$, including
$2\arccos(-\sqrt6/4)+\arccos(-1/4)=2\pi$, and
$\arccos(-\sqrt{1/3-2\sqrt5/15})+\arccos(-\sqrt{1/3+2\sqrt5/15})+\arccos(-1/\sqrt5)=2\pi$
(pentagonal antiprism, icosidodecahedron and dodecahedron angles). All
degenerate roots occur in 3-cycles. For a 3-cycle the link is a rigid
spherical triangle and the closure has one explicit solution: with $\Gamma$
the Gram matrix of the three cell rays ($\Gamma_{ij}=\cos\alpha$) and
$d_i=\sqrt{R_{C_i}^2-\rho^2}$, the unnormalized center offset is
$w=\sum\lambda_iu_i$ with $\lambda=\Gamma^{-1}d$ (for the unit direction
$\hat w$ multiply by the positive scale $1/|w|$); the center lies on the
hyperplane of facet $K$ iff the coefficient of the ray opposite $K$
vanishes, a Cramer determinant, which is proved exactly zero. Since $\Gamma$
is nonsingular, the center offset is unique, and an exactly zero
coefficient already excludes a strictly interior center, whatever the signs
of the other two; the exclusion uses nothing more. A positive
rescaling of the center does not change the vanishing determinant.

\emph{Proof route.} The stage-1 run uses input tables certified by
Computation~\ref{comp:link}, with the exclusions on the exact inputs of
Computation~\ref{comp:exact}. \emph{Second implementation.} The stage 1 of
\file{checks/independent-s1-b2/} (Section~\ref{sec:indep}) enumerates all closed
walks of length three to six on its own library (every angle is at least
$\pi/3$, so longer walks exceed $2\pi$ and length six is flat), and isolates
roots using a strict monotonicity proved for each word: in the variable
$u=1/(R^2-\rho^2)$ the derivative of the cosine of each closure angle has
the sign of an affine function of $u$, certified strictly negative at both
ends of the admissible interval, so each angle increases strictly in $u$
(decreases in $R$) and every non-flat cycle has at most one root. It reproduces the 14,965
cycles with identical words and statuses, the 7,080 flat cycles, 57
degenerate roots and 537 cycles without roots, and 7,291 retained roots,
each contained in the recorded interval of the same cycle; its root
intervals are narrower. \emph{Shared with the proof route:} the alias
table, the construction theorem and, for the exclusions, the exact
construction code of Computation~\ref{comp:exact}; both use Arb
(python-flint) and SymPy.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:omega}
Let $X$ be any convex uniform 5-polytope with unit edges and circumradius
$R_X$, and $G$ a 2-face of $X$ all of whose facets (around $G$) belong to
$\mathcal L_{16}$, with $G$ at most 16-gonal. Then $R_X$ lies in a root
interval of the cycle of $G$, and that interval belongs to $\Omega$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The cycle of $G$ is a library cycle (Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(a)), hence
in $\mathcal C_{16}$; it is not flat (Lemma~\ref{lem:exclusions});
$1/R_X$ is a zero of $g$ in $[0,1/R_{\max})$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(c),(d), so it lies in a root interval, which is not
a degenerate one (Lemma~\ref{lem:exclusions}).
\end{proof}

The facets around $G$ need not be in $\mathcal L_{16}$ when $X$ has larger
polygons elsewhere; Corollary~\ref{cor:omega} is stated for any $X$
because Case B uses it.

\paragraph{Radius classes and the closure filter.}
A \emph{radius class} is a connected component of the overlap graph of
the intervals of $\Omega$ (closed intervals that meet are joined); its
hull is the class interval. A class carries the set of cycles with an
interval in it. The \emph{closure filter} then removes, repeatedly until
nothing changes, every cycle of a class containing a facet type $K$ such
that some polygon orbit $(p,C_a,C_b)$ of $K$ is not a side
($K$ between $C_a$ and $C_b$ at a $p$-gon) of any remaining cycle of the
class. A class \emph{survives} if cycles remain.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:filter}
All cycles of the 2-faces of $P$ at a vertex $v$ lie in one radius class,
the class containing $R$, and none of them is removed by the closure
filter.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:omega} each of these cycles has a root interval in
$\Omega$ containing $R$, so all these intervals lie in the class of $R$.
Let $K$ be a facet of $P$ at $v$ and $(p,C_a,C_b)$ a polygon orbit of $K$.
Since $K$ is vertex-transitive, the orbit has a member $G\ni v$, a 2-face
of $P$ whose cycle contains $K$ between $C_a$ and $C_b$; this cycle is in
the class of $R$. So the set of cycles of $P$'s 2-faces at $v$ is never
touched by the filter (by induction over the iterations).
\end{proof}

\begin{computation}[FILTER]\label{comp:filter}
The intervals of $\Omega$ form 4,224 radius classes, of which 563 survive
the closure filter.
\end{computation}
\noindent The program \texttt{class\_filter.py} applies this
combinatorial filter to the stage-1 output.
The program rejects unresolved, unknown, missing or malformed statuses,
malformed or non-finite surviving intervals, and invalid, duplicate or
overlapping exclusion lists; it reconstructs the complete canonical cycle
inventory from the facet table and compares it with the input (so a dropped
record of either status is detected); it compares endpoints and forms
class hulls in exact decimal arithmetic; and before forming classes it runs
both LINK checkers on the actual table and the exact authentication of
Computation~\ref{comp:exact}. Rerun on the pinned stage-1 roots with
regenerated exact exclusions, it gives
760/1,790/4,224 radius classes and 136/278/563 surviving classes at
$P_{\mathrm{ctl}}=6/10/16$, with all class files byte-identical to their pins; 23 controls pass.
\emph{Second implementation.} \file{checks/independent-merge-filter/} independently computes exact rational interval components and
fixed-point deletions, with explicit missing-word witnesses. Its separate
certificate checker imports no search code; both checkers reject the same

$\mathcal H_6$, $\mathcal H_{10}$ and $\mathcal H_{16}$ controls

\subsection{Stage 2: vertex figures}\label{sec:S2}

Let $I$ be a surviving class with interval $[R_{\rm lo},R_{\rm hi}]$. By
Lemmas~\ref{lem:vf} and~\ref{lem:filter}, if $R\in I$ then the vertex
figure $\VF$ of $P$ at $v$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\VF$ is a convex 4-polytope inscribed in the 3-sphere of radius
  $r=\sqrt{1-1/(4R^2)}$; each facet is $\VF(K)$ for a facet type $K$ that
  occurs in a cycle of $I$, lying in a hyperplane at distance
  $\sqrt{r^2-r_K^2}$ from the center, with the center on its inner side;
\item every ridge (2-face) of $\VF$ is the vertex figure of a cell and lies
  in exactly two facets of $\VF$, with the same cell label;
\item around every edge of $\VF$ (a polygon of $P$ at $v$) the facets and
  ridge labels, read cyclically, form a cycle of $I$;
\item distinct vertices of $\VF$ are at distance at least 1
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}).
\end{enumerate}
A \emph{signature} of a vertex figure is its multiset of facet types
together with the multiset of canonical cyclic words around its edges.

\begin{computation}[S2: stage 2]\label{comp:S2}
For each of the 563 surviving classes, an exhaustive search, sound for
every $R$ in the class interval, outputs a finite list of complete
candidate encodings; their signatures are 629 in total (one class, the class at
$R=1.0505014948\ldots$ formed by a cycle of three 5-cells and a 16-cell
around a triangle, has none). For every $R$ in the class and every vertex
figure satisfying (a)--(d), its signature is one of the signatures listed
for the class. This is a coverage statement: retaining an encoding does
not prove that all its assembly equations have a simultaneous exact
solution. Existence of the known models is supplied separately by
Section~\ref{sec:models} and their named constructions.
\end{computation}

\noindent\emph{Why such a search is exhaustive.} Fix the least facet type
$t_0$ present: the first facet is $X_{t_0}$ in a fixed position, which is
no loss of generality after an isometry. Repeatedly take an open ridge (a
ridge in only one placed facet). In the true vertex figure exactly one
other facet contains it (by (b)). Trying every facet type $\ge t_0$, every
ridge of that type with the same label and size, every cyclic and reversed
correspondence of the ridge polygon, and both positions of the hinge (the
two hyperplanes through the ridge at the right distance from the center;
only the one on the side opposite the existing facet can occur, by step 2
of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:D}) therefore includes the true facet. A branch may be cut only for a
certified violation of (a)--(d): a vertex certified strictly outside a
facet hyperplane; two vertices at a certified distance in $(0,1)$; three
facets at a ridge or a label clash; a closed cycle around a VF-edge that is
not a cycle of the class. These rejection rules are exhaustive. Rounded or approximately
symmetric equivalences are insufficient for this purpose. The
proof route below removes candidates only by \emph{exact} equivalences.

\begin{lemma}[Exact equivalence of placements]\label{lem:equiv}
Let $g$ be an exact isometry of the model vertex figure $X_K$ fixing its
center, preserving its labeled ridges and VF-edge polygon sizes, and
mapping an ordered source ridge correspondence $o$ to another one $o'$
(that is, $g(o_i)=o'_i$). Then for every admissible $R$ in the class
($R>R_K$) the facets placed over a given target ridge by $o$ and by $o'$,
with the hinge on the side opposite to the existing facet, coincide as
labeled point sets.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$g$ fixes the template center, so it preserves the distance of the source
ridge from it and carries its outward normal to that of $o'$. With the
hinge on the opposite side, the new facet's normal is computed from
$d_K(R)$, that distance and the target ridge alone, so it is the same for
both options. If $A_o$, $A_{o'}$ are the two gluing maps, $A_{o'}\circ g$
and $A_o$ have the same ordered ridge images and the same outward normal;
they agree on three non-collinear ridge vertices and on the facet center,
which is not in their affine plane, and so they are equal on the facet
hyperplane. Hence the placed facets coincide as functions of $R$, whether
or not $g$ reverses orientation, and $g$ preserves the adjacent labels
used by later filters.
\end{proof}

\noindent\emph{Proof route} (\texttt{check\_u2} with \texttt{stage2}; see). The independent stage-2 search
(Section~\ref{sec:U2}) builds every $\VF(K)$ directly from the
guarded exact construction models of Section~\ref{sec:models}
(\texttt{exact\_types.py}): metric constants by directed interval

cell certificates, no inverse cosine. The only reductions are exact

options. The \emph{equivalence certificate}
\texttt{equivalences-p16.json} lists, for each of the 181 types, every
ordered ridge correspondence (cyclic and reversed): 5,108 in total, 1,437
retained and 3,671 discarded, each discarded one with an explicit exact
permutation carrying it to a retained one, so that Lemma~\ref{lem:equiv}
applies. Two checkers (\texttt{audit\_certificates.py} and the
separately written \texttt{check\_equivalences.py}, which imports

correspondence, labels, edge types, the hinge flag, full coverage of the
options, exact group membership and that every representative is retained;
each accepts all 5,108 correspondences and rejects all 905 deliberate
corruptions (five per type). Vertex identity uses the stricter bound

\subsection{Model ridge lists}\label{sec:ridges}

\begin{computation}[RIDGES: certified ridge lists]\label{comp:ridges}
For each of the 181 canonical types $K$, the list of 2-faces of the model
vertex figure $X_K$ (which point sets, their cyclic order, their cell
labels) is correct.
\end{computation}

\noindent\emph{What is checked} (\texttt{cert\_ridges.py}), ridge by
ridge:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{Face.} A plane through three consecutive points of the ridge;
  every other point of $X_K$ certified strictly on one side; the ridge's
  remaining points on the plane \emph{exactly}: for Wythoffian types the
  neighbors of $K[0]$ are recomputed in exact arithmetic ($\Q$ for $A_4$,
  $D_4$; $\Q(\sqrt2)$ for $B_4$, $F_4$; $\Q(\sqrt5)$ for $H_4$), matched to
  the model points by the separation lemma inside $K$, and the differences
  have exact affine rank 2; \texttt{sadi} and \texttt{gap} likewise in
  $\Q(\sqrt5)$; the polyhedral base ridge of a prism or antiprism prism lies
  in a plane by construction (its points and $K[0]$ have last coordinate
  exactly $1/2$, and lie on two spheres); all other ridges are triangles.
\item \emph{Cyclic order.} For consecutive points $a,b$ every other ridge
  point is certified strictly on one side of the line $ab$ in the ridge
  plane; so consecutive pairs are sides, hence edges of $X_K$.
\item \emph{Completeness.} Every side lies in exactly two listed ridges and
  every point in a listed ridge; since the face graph of a 3-polytope is
  connected, the list contains every 2-face.
\item \emph{Label.} A side of the ridge of a cell is the vertex figure of a
  $q$-gon, of length $\ch_q$; $q$ is pinned by the certified bracket
  $4\cos^2(\pi/(q-1))<|a-b|^2<4\cos^2(\pi/(q+1))$ (the function
  $4\cos^2(\pi/q)$ increases with $q$). The cyclic sequence of sizes is the
  cell's vertex configuration, which determines the cell
  (Fact~\ref{fact:3d}); it must match exactly one label.
\end{enumerate}
Result: 181/181 types certified; 823 ridges (747 triangles, 55 exactly
coplanar by exact arithmetic, 21 by construction), 1,277 VF-edges, each in
exactly two ridges. Five wrong
lists (a wrong label, a dropped ridge, a wrong cyclic order, a square split
into two triangles, a non-coplanar four-point set) are rejected, and a
non-coplanar set has exact affine rank 3.
This certifies the local data of the models; that the model points are the

\subsubsection{Congruence from discrete data}

\emph{Discrete data.} A vertex figure's data are, for each of its facets,
the facet's point set, its library type $K$, and a correspondence $c$ (model
point $t\mapsto$ VF point $c(t)$). Correspondences are taken modulo a set
$E_K$ of \emph{exact} isometries of $X_K$ fixing $K[0]$: the permutations
induced by generators of $\Stab_K(K[0])$ (exact reflections and diagram
automorphisms, matched by the separation lemma inside $K$), and, for prisms
over polyhedra, the relabellings $t\mapsto s\pm t$ of the polyhedron's
vertex-figure polygon that preserve its vertex configuration (a rotation or
reflection of the circle, exactly; this adds the mirror symmetry of the
snub vertex figures, which $\Stab$ does not contain). Elements that do not
map labeled ridges to ridges with the same label are dropped. A missing
element of $E_K$ can only make the check below fail, never pass.

\begin{lemma}[Congruence from discrete data]\label{lem:D}
Let $X$, $X'$ be convex uniform 5-polytopes with the same circumradius $R$
and vertex figures $V$, $V'$ indexed by the same set, with the same facets:
the same index sets, the same types, and correspondences that agree
modulo $E_K$. Then a linear isometry $A$ with $AV_i=V'_i$ for all $i$
exists.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(1) Take a facet $f$ with $V[S_f]=g(X_K)$ and $V'[S_f]=g'(X_K)$ and
$c'=c\circ e$ with $e\in E_K$ realized by the isometry $\varepsilon$. Then
$V[c(t)]\mapsto V'[c(t)]$ is $g'\varepsilon^{-1}g^{-1}$, an isometry. Both
facet sets lie in hyperplanes at the same distance $\sqrt{r^2-r_K^2}$ from
the center $O$, so it extends to a linear isometry $A$ of $\R^4$.
(2) Let $f'$ share a ridge $\rho$ (at least three non-collinear points)
with $f$. Project to the 2-plane $W$ orthogonal to the directions of
$\rho$: $\rho$ becomes a point $p$, and the hyperplanes through $\rho$
become lines through $p$, tangent at their feet $T=dn$ to circles about
$O$. The center $O$ is interior to the vertex figure (Lemma~\ref{lem:vf}),
so it lies in the open wedge at $p$ bounded by the two facet lines (angle
below $\pi$). Each facet's circumcenter lies on its ray; it is relatively
interior to the facet (the facet is the vertex figure of a uniform
4-polytope), so $T\ne p$. Hence the two feet lie strictly on opposite
sides of the line $pO$, and the hyperplane of $f'$ is the tangent line from
$p$ at distance $d_{K_{f'}}$ on the side opposite to $f$'s foot; it is the
same for $AV$ and $V'$. (3) Inside that hyperplane $AV[S_{f'}]$ and
$V'[S_{f'}]$ are related by an index-preserving isometry, as in (1), which
fixes $\rho$ pointwise and the common circumcenter, which is not in the
plane of $\rho$; so it is the identity. (4) The facet graph of $V$ through
ridges is connected, so $AV=V'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Coset comparison]\label{lem:coset}
Let $S$ be the full star at $v_0$ of a hypothetical convex uniform
polytope of circumradius $R$, centered at the origin, and let $T$ be a
group of exact linear isometries fixing $S$. At a neighbor $w$, let $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ be a complete,
nonempty list of candidate linear isometries for its star, with a true
congruence $g_i$ among them. Suppose that for every $j$ there is
$\tau_j\in T$ and a certified Frobenius-norm bound
$\|g_1\tau_j-g_j\|_F\le\varepsilon_j$, and
$2R\max_j\varepsilon_j<1/2$. Suppose each $g_j$ has anchor vertices
$a$ in $S$ spanning $\mathbb R^5$, and every image $g_j(a)$ belongs
to a known facet through $w$. Then every candidate gives the same full
star at $w$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The identity
\[
g_i\tau_i^{-1}\tau_j-g_j
=(g_i-g_1\tau_i)\tau_i^{-1}\tau_j+(g_1\tau_j-g_j)
\]
bounds the operator norm by $\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j$.
On an anchor of norm $R$, the two images are less than $1/2$ apart.
Both are vertices of the hypothetical polytope: one lies in the true
star $g_i(S)$, the other in the known facet. By Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}
they coincide. The spanning anchors determine the isometry, so
$g_j=g_i\tau_i^{-1}\tau_j$ and $g_j(S)=g_i(S)$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Stage 3: \texorpdfstring{\texttt{check\_u3/}}{check_u3/}}\label{sec:U3}
The stage-3 checker (Proposition~\ref{prop:u3} below) compares stars by exact
encodings; the following lemma says what an
encoding records and why equal encodings give equal stars.

\begin{lemma}[Rooted determinism]\label{lem:rooted}
Fix a common circumcenter $c$ and an admissible radius $R$ (a radius in the class interval with
$R>R_K$ for every facet type $K$ used). A \emph{rooted encoding} of a star
at $v_0$ records the full template type of an initial facet and its root
frame (the images of the template's base vertex and its neighbors), and,
along a connected traversal of the facet adjacency graph of the vertex
figure (facets adjacent through cells containing $v_0$), the type of each
further facet and the ordered correspondence of its model neighbor
indices, taken modulo the exact stabilizer of the base vertex in the
\emph{full} template. If two stars at the same $R$ have equal rooted
encodings in root frames whose identification also identifies $c$, they
are equal as sets of full facets.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The root frame fixes the template map on the base vertex and its
neighbors, which affinely span the facet hyperplane, so it fixes the full
root facet. Suppose a facet $F$ is fixed and $C\ni v_0$ is a cell of $F$
at which the traversal reaches the next facet $F'$ of type $K'$. The affine
hull of $C$ is fixed. The center of $F'$ is at distance
$\sqrt{R^2-R_{K'}^2}>0$ from the fixed common center $c$, so the hyperplane
of $F'$ is one of the two hyperplanes through $\aff C$ at that distance,
and, as in step (2) of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:D} one dimension up
(the center of the polytope is interior, the facet centers are relatively
interior by Lemma~\ref{lem:sphere}), it is the one whose foot lies on the
side opposite to that of $F$. The ordered correspondence fixes the images
of $v_0$ and of the vertex-figure points of $C$; together with the center
of $F'$, which is not in $\aff C$, they affinely span the hyperplane of
$F'$, so the isometric template map, and hence the full facet $F'$, is
unique. Changing a correspondence by an element of the full-template base
stabilizer does not change the placed full facet. The facet graph of the
vertex figure is connected, so induction reaches every facet. If the
stars are given as functions of $R$ on an interval, the conclusion is
equality of the placement functions at each common admissible $R$; the
unique-root step then selects one value.
\end{proof}

Neither the coarse signature nor the larger group $E_K$ of isometries of
the vertex figure $X_K$ would do here: for the snub-cube prism a mirror
fixes the vertex figure but not the full facet (after
reflecting one snub-cube-prism facet of $\{3\}\times$ snub cube, the two
facets contain distinct vertices only $0.2956$ apart).
\file{checks/check_u3} (\texttt{canon.py}, \texttt{search.py}) uses full-template
stabilizers and records the ordered model-neighbor indices, as the lemma
requires.

\begin{proposition}[stage 3, proof route]\label{prop:u3}
Let $P$ be a convex uniform 5-polytope with all 2-faces at most 16-gonal,
and let $\sigma$ be the signature of its vertex figure at $v_0$, in the
surviving class $I$ containing $R$. If $\sigma$ has verdict \texttt{KNOWN}
with name $Q$, then $P\cong Q$; if $\sigma$ has verdict \texttt{REFUTED},
$P$ does not exist.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $X$ be the actual star of $P$ at $v_0$. By step 1 it is represented
by the single retained encoding of $\sigma$;
by step 2, $R$ is the unique root on the whole class interval. If the
verdict is \texttt{KNOWN}, then $Q$'s own star is a realization of a
signature of the class (stage 2 and step 1 applied to $Q$, whose facets lie
in the library) and every other encoding of the class is excluded for
$Q$ (step 3; $Q$ entered the class because its radius lies in $I$).
Its star therefore has the same encoding as $X$ and, by step 2, the same
radius; Lemma~\ref{lem:rooted} makes the stars equal in identified root
frames. Thus after an isometry $P$ and $Q$ have the same star
$X$ at $v_0$. By step 4 any convex uniform
5-polytope with star $X$ at $v_0$ has one and the same star at every
neighbor of $v_0$; Lemma~\ref{lem:induction} gives $P=Q$ after the
isometry. If the verdict is \texttt{REFUTED}, the development of step 5
excludes every possibility (Computation~\ref{comp:dev}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}}

\section{Case B: a 2-face with at least 17 sides}\label{sec:caseB}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Setting}

In this section $P$ is a convex uniform 5-polytope with unit edges,
circumradius $R$, center $c$, having a large 2-face (at least 17 sides).
By vertex-transitivity every vertex lies in a large 2-face. Fix a vertex
$v$; $\VF$ is its vertex figure, a convex 4-polytope inscribed in a
3-sphere of radius $r=\sqrt{1-1/(4R^2)}<1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:vf}). Faces of
$\VF$ correspond to faces of $P$ at $v$: VF-vertex $=$ edge of $P$ at $v$
(we identify it with the neighbor), VF-edge $=$ 2-face, VF-ridge $=$
cell, VF-facet $=$ facet. A VF-edge is \emph{long} if its 2-face is large;
its length is $\ch_n\ge\ch_{17}=2\cos(\pi/17)=1.96595\ldots$. A facet is
\emph{large} if it contains a large 2-face. For a large 2-face $F\ni v$
with VF-edge $e$, the \emph{fan} of $F$ (or of $e$) is the set of
VF-facets $\VF(K)$ with $K\supset F$, and its vertices are the \emph{fan
vertices}.

\subsection{Facts about the families}

\begin{fact}[F2]\label{fact:F2}
Let $q\notin\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$. A convex uniform polyhedron with a $q$-gonal
face is $P_q$ or $A_q$; a convex uniform 4-polytope with a $q$-gonal 2-face
is a duoprism $\{q\}\times\{p\}$ ($p\ge3$, including $P_q\times\{\}=\{q\}\times\{4\}$)
or $A_q\times\{\}$ (Facts~\ref{fact:3d} and~\ref{fact:4d}). In particular
this holds for every large $q$, and for $q\in\{7,9,11,\dots,16\}$
(``family polygons''), which (P1) uses.
\end{fact}

\begin{fact}[F3]\label{fact:F3}
In $\{n\}\times\{q\}$ and in $A_n\times\{\}$ every vertex lies on an
$n$-gon. At a vertex $v$:
$\VF(\{n\}\times\{q\})$ is the tetrahedron $\conv(a,b,g,g')$, where $a,b$
are the neighbors on the $n$-gon and $g,g'$ those on the $q$-gon:
$|ab|=\ch_n$, $|gg'|=\ch_q$, $ab\perp gg'$, and the other four edges have
length $\sqrt2$. $\VF(A_n\times\{\})$ is the pyramid with apex $z$ (the
prism edge) over the trapezoid $(a,b,y,x)=\VF(A_n)$: $ab$ is the VF-edge of
the $n$-gon, $|by|=|yx|=|xa|=1$ (the three triangles at $v$), the diagonals
$ay$, $bx$ have length $\sqrt{1+\ch_n}$, and the four apex edges have
length $\sqrt2$.
\end{fact}

These follow directly from the stated product and antiprism constructions.

\begin{lemma}[F5: cycle types]\label{lem:F5}
Let $F$ be a 2-face of $P$ with $n\notin\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$ sides. Then the
cycle of $F$ is one of
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item $\{n\}\times\{q_1\},\dots,\{n\}\times\{q_k\}$ (all cells $P_n$), with
  $k\in\{3,4,5\}$ and $\sum_i(1-2/q_i)<2$;
\item $A_n\times\{\}$, $A_n\times\{\}$, $\{n\}\times\{q\}$;
\item $A_n\times\{\}$, $A_n\times\{\}$, $\{n\}\times\{q_1\}$,
  $\{n\}\times\{q_2\}$ with $\{q_1,q_2\}\in\{\{3,3\},\{3,4\},\{3,5\}\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Fact~\ref{fact:F2} the cells at $F$ are $P_n$ or $A_n$ and the facets
$\{n\}\times\{q\}$ or $A_n\times\{\}$. The face angles of the tangent cone
at $F$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}) are $\pi(1-2/q)$ for $\{n\}\times\{q\}$
(between two $P_n$) and $\pi/2$ for $A_n\times\{\}$ (between $A_n$ and
$P_n$), with sum below $2\pi$. Both neighbors of an $A_n$ ray are
$A_n\times\{\}$, each containing one $A_n$ cell at $F$, so $j$ rays of type
$A_n$ cost $2j$ distinct right angles, and $j\le1$. If $j=0$ we get (i),
with $k\ge3$ and $k\le5$ since each term is at least $1/3$. If $j=1$ there
are $m\ge0$ duoprisms between the two $P_n$ rays, and the cycle has $m+2$
rays. The tangent cone at $F$ is a full-dimensional pointed cone in the
3-dimensional space $N$, so a section of it by a positive affine functional
is a convex polygon and the cone has at least three extreme rays; hence
$m=0$ is impossible. $m=1$ is (ii); $m=2$ needs $1/q_1+1/q_2>1/2$, i.e.\
(iii); $m\ge3$ gives a sum of at least $\pi+3\pi/3$, too large.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Long edges are disjoint]\label{lem:long}
Two long VF-edges share no vertex.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A long edge $[a,b]$ subtends at the center of the VF sphere an angle
$\theta$ with $\sin(\theta/2)=\cos(\pi/n)/r\ge\cos(\pi/17)$, so
$\theta\ge\pi-2\pi/17$ ($158.82^\circ$). If $[a,b]$ and $[a,b']$ are both
long, $b$ and $b'$ lie within $2\pi/17$ ($21.18^\circ$) of the antipode of
$a$, so $|b-b'|\le2r\sin(2\pi/17)<0.7225<1$, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:long} is used in Section~\ref{sec:W}: the Arb
certificate of (W) leaves out the ends of the long edges themselves, and
this lemma covers them.

\subsection{The radius forced by a large 2-face}\label{sec:radii}

Let $F$ be an $n$-gon of $P$ at $v$, $n\notin\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$ (so that
Lemma~\ref{lem:F5} applies), with center $o_F$ in the plane
$L$, and write $c=o_F+W$ with $W\perp L$; so $R^2=r_n^2+|W|^2$. A neighbor
$v+u$ of $v$ with $u\perp L$ is on the circumsphere iff $u\cdot W=1/2$
(expand $|v+u-c|^2=R^2$ using $u\cdot(v-o_F)=0$).

\paragraph{Type (i).} The cells at $F$ are $F\times[v,v+u_i]$ with unit
$u_i\perp L$; consecutive $u_i,u_{i+1}$ span the $q_i$-gon of
$\{n\}\times\{q_i\}$ at $v$, so $|u_i-u_{i+1}|=\ch_{q_i}$. All $u_i$ satisfy
$u_i\cdot W=1/2$, so they lie on a circle of radius $S=\sin\alpha$ on the
cone of half-angle $\alpha$ about $W$, $\cos\alpha=1/(2|W|)$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:closure}(c), $W$ is strictly inside the cone spanned by the
$u_i$, i.e.\ the circle's center is strictly inside the inscribed polygon of
the $u_i$: all central angles $2\arcsin(\cos(\pi/q_i)/S)$ are below $\pi$
and they sum to $2\pi$. Let
$f_c(S)=\sum_i2\arcsin(\cos(\pi/q_i)/S)$ on $[c_{\max},1]$,
$c_{\max}=\max_i\cos(\pi/q_i)$; $f_c$ is strictly decreasing and
$f_c(1)=\sum\pi(1-2/q_i)<2\pi$. Hence:

\begin{lemma}[Type (i) radius and closability]\label{lem:typei}
A configuration $c=(q_1,\dots,q_k)$ occurs in a type-(i) cycle only if it
is \emph{closable}: $f_c(c_{\max})>2\pi$. Then $f_c(S)=2\pi$ has a unique
solution $S\in(c_{\max},1)$, the \emph{configuration radius}
$R_c=1/(2\sqrt{1-S^2})$ depends only on the multiset $c$, and
\[R^2=r_n^2+R_c^2.\]
Moreover $R_c\ge r_{q_{\max}}$. For $k=3$, closability is equivalent to the
triangle with sides $\ch_{q_1},\ch_{q_2},\ch_{q_3}$ being acute; for $k=4$
to the circle's center being strictly inside the inscribed quadrilateral.
If $f_c(c_{\max})=2\pi$ exactly (\emph{degenerate}), the center lies on the
hyperplane of a facet, which is impossible.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All but the formula $R_c\ge r_{q_{\max}}$ and the triangle criterion is
above. $S\ge\cos(\pi/q_{\max})$ gives
$R_c^2=1/(4(1-S^2))\ge1/(4\sin^2(\pi/q_{\max}))$. For three rays: the foot of
the perpendicular from $0$ to the plane of $u_1u_2u_3$ is the circumcenter
$o_T$ of the chord triangle, and $o_T\cdot u_i=|o_T|^2$ for each $i$, so
$W=o_T/(2|o_T|^2)$ (both satisfy the three equations $x\cdot u_i=1/2$, which
have a unique solution); so $W$ is interior to
the cone iff $o_T$ is interior to the triangle iff the triangle is acute,
and a right triangle puts $W$ in the plane of a facet. The same argument
with the circle gives the $k=4$ form.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[four or five rays]\label{rem:k4}
For $k\ge4$ a configuration is never degenerate, and it is closable if and
only if $\sum_i(1-2/q_i)<2$. Indeed, at $S=c_{\max}$ one maximal term of
$f_c$ equals $\pi$, and each of the other $k-1\ge3$ terms is strictly larger
than $2\arcsin(\cos(\pi/q_i))=\pi(1-2/q_i)\ge\pi/3$ because $c_{\max}<1$; so
$f_c(c_{\max})>2\pi$ even when maximal entries are tied. Since $f_c$ is
strictly decreasing, a root in $(c_{\max},1)$ exists exactly when
$f_c(1)=\pi\sum(1-2/q_i)<2\pi$ (at equality the only root is $S=1$, which
gives no finite $R_c$). This makes the closability classification used in
B2 and (P1) checkable by hand for $k\ge4$. The exact angle condition already
excludes $k\ge6$, so the finite enumerations need $k=3,4,5$ only; the B2
checker rejects cycles with fewer than three or more than five entries,
and the (P1) closure routines reject fewer than three, explicitly.
\end{remark}

Exactly three configurations with entries at most 16 are degenerate:
$(3,3,4)$, $(3,4,6)$, $(3,5,10)$, since
$\ch_3^2+\ch_3^2=\ch_4^2$, $\ch_3^2+\ch_4^2=\ch_6^2$ and
$\ch_3^2+\ch_5^2=\ch_{10}^2$ (the last is $1+(3+\sqrt5)/2=(5+\sqrt5)/2$). The
configuration $(3,5,q)$ is not closable for $q\ge11$, since
$1+\ch_5^2=3.618\ldots<\ch_q^2$; similarly $(3,3,q)$ for $q\ge5$ and $(3,4,q)$
for $q\ge7$. For $c''$ a sequence such that $(n,c'')$ is closable, write
\[\delta_{c''}(n):=R_{(n,c'')}^2-r_n^2.\]

\begin{lemma}[closed forms]\label{lem:deltas}
Let $n\ge17$, $\theta=\pi/n$, $c=\cos\theta$ and $x=c^2$. Then
\[
\delta_{(4,4)}(n)=\tfrac14,\qquad
\delta_{(3,6)}(n)=\frac{x}{4x-1}=\frac{1}{4-\sec^2\theta},\qquad
\delta_{(3,3,3)}(n)=\frac{2c+1}{8(1+c)}=\frac{h_A(n)^2}{4}.
\]
$\delta_{(3,6)}$ decreases and $\delta_{(3,3,3)}$ increases with $n$, so
for $n\ge17$, including the limit $n=\infty$,
$\delta_{(3,6)}(n)\in[1/3,\ \delta_{(3,6)}(17)]$ with
$\delta_{(3,6)}(17)=\cos^2(\pi/17)/(4\cos^2(\pi/17)-1)=0.3372617\ldots<0.337262$,
and $\delta_{(3,3,3)}(n)\in[h_A(17)^2/4,\ 3/16]$ with
$h_A(17)^2/4=0.1869633\ldots$; the value $3/16$ is attained only in the
limit $n=\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a chord triangle with squared sides $A,B,C$ the circumradius satisfies
$S^2=ABC/((A+B+C)^2-2(A^2+B^2+C^2))$, and $R_c^2=1/(4(1-S^2))$,
$r_n^2=1/(4(1-x))$. For $(n,3,6)$ the sides are $2c,1,\sqrt3$, which gives
$S^2=3x/(-1+8x-4x^2)$ and $\delta_{(3,6)}=x/(4x-1)$; the triangle is acute
since $1/2<x<1$, so this is the closable solution. For $(n,4,4)$ the sides
are $2c,\sqrt2,\sqrt2$, giving $S^2=1/(2-x)$ and $\delta=1/4$ (the prism
$P_n$); this triangle is also acute. For $(n,3,3,3)$ put $S^2=1/(3-2c)$
and $\sin\alpha=1/(2S)$; then $\pi/6<\alpha<\pi/4$, the three unit chords
subtend $2\alpha$ each, the remaining arc $2\beta=2\pi-6\alpha$ lies in
$(\pi/2,\pi)$, and $\sin\beta=\sin3\alpha=\sin\alpha(3-4\sin^2\alpha)=2c\sin\alpha$,
so its chord is $2S\sin\beta=2c$: all four arcs are below $\pi$ and sum to
$2\pi$. Substitution gives $\delta_{(3,3,3)}=(2c+1)/(8(1+c))
=\bigl(1-1/(2(1+c))\bigr)/4=h_A(n)^2/4$ (the antiprism $A_n$), using
$\cos^2(\theta/2)=(1+c)/2$. Finally
$d\delta_{(3,6)}/dc=-2c/(4c^2-1)^2<0$ and
$d\delta_{(3,3,3)}/dc=1/(8(1+c)^2)>0$, and $c$ increases to $1$ with $n$.
\end{proof}

The limiting value $3/16$ is included in the closed range used by (P1).
The partner filter uses the valid bound $0.3373$ for
$\delta_{(3,6)}(17)$; the exact rational function, rather than a rounded
constant, is evaluated throughout the parameter interval.

\paragraph{Type (ii).} The $A_n$ cell has its second $n$-gon at height
$h_A=h_A(n)$ along a unit vector $\hat h\perp L$, rotated by $\pi/n$. The
edge condition gives $h_A^2=1-1/(4\cos^2(\pi/(2n)))$, and equidistance from
the two $n$-gons gives $W\cdot\hat h=h_A/2$, so
$R_{A_n}^2=r_n^2+h_A^2/4$. The other two rays $z_1,z_2$ (lateral edges of
the $P_n$ cells) make right angles with $\hat h$ and the angle $\pi(1-2/q)$
with each other, so they lie in the plane orthogonal to $\hat h$ in
$L^\perp$; the component $W'$ of $W$ there satisfies $W'\cdot z_j=1/2$ and is
the center offset of the $q$-gon, $|W'|=r_q$. Hence
\[R^2=R_{A_n}^2+r_q^2.\]

\paragraph{Type (iii).} The rays are $\hat h,z_1,w,z_2$. As in (ii),
$W=(h_A/2)\hat h+W'$ with $W'\in\spn(z_1,z_2)$, $W'\cdot z_j=1/2$, so with
$\phi$ the angle between $z_1$ and $z_2$ and $\gamma:=\cos\phi$,
$|W'|^2=1/(2(1+\gamma))$ and $R^2=R_{A_n}^2+1/(2(1+\gamma))$. Writing
$w=\alpha z_1+\beta z_2+\eta\hat h$ with $w\cdot z_j=c_j:=\cos(\pi(1-2/q_j))$,
the remaining condition $W\cdot w=1/2$ reads $h_A\eta=1-(\alpha+\beta)$, where
$\alpha+\beta=(c_1+c_2)/(1+\gamma)$ and $\eta^2=1-|\alpha z_1+\beta z_2|^2$.
Convexity of the cone $(\hat h,z_1,w,z_2)$ puts $w$ and $\hat h$ on opposite
sides of $\spn(z_1,z_2)$, so $\eta<0$ (the \emph{geometric} branch).
Squaring, with $N=1-\gamma^2-c_1^2-c_2^2+2\gamma c_1c_2$ (so that
$\eta^2=N/(1-\gamma^2)$) and $M=1+\gamma-c_1-c_2$, gives the polynomial
equation
\[
h_A^2\,N\,(1+\gamma)-M^2\,(1-\gamma)=0,
\]
to be solved on the domain $-1<\gamma<1$ together with the norm condition
$N\ge0$ and the unsquared sign $\eta<0$. When $c_1=c_2$ (the pair $(3,3)$)
the polynomial has the spurious factor $1-\gamma$, which is canceled,
leaving a quadratic; otherwise it is a cubic. The radius identity above
holds at every geometric root. At $n=17$, for instance, the roots in
$(-1,1)$ are one for $(3,3)$ and two each for $(3,4)$ and $(3,5)$, and exactly one of them is geometric in each case. B2 checks \emph{all roots in $(-1,1)$}
(Computation~\ref{comp:B2}), so no claim about their number is needed.
The (W) checker parametrizes the type-(iii) fan by $\cos(\phi/2)$
and recovers $n$ from it, without root finding.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:B2}
Every 2-face $G$ of $P$ lies in a large facet. Consequently every VF-edge
at $v$ lies in a VF-facet $\VF(K)$ with $K$ large, and $K$ contains a large
2-face through $v$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $G$ is large there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, suppose no facet
around $G$ is large; then all of them have only polygons of at most 16
sides, so they are in $\mathcal L_{16}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:A7}) and $R\in\Omega$
by Corollary~\ref{cor:omega}. But $R$ is also the radius forced by the cycle
of a large 2-face of $P$, which is of type (i), (ii) or (iii)
(Lemma~\ref{lem:F5}) with the parameters of Computation~\ref{comp:B2}
(type (i) configurations are closable by Lemma~\ref{lem:typei}; in type
(iii), $\gamma=\cos\phi$ is a root of the polynomial in $(-1,1)$, and B2
covers every such root), so $R\notin\Omega$. For the second part, a large facet $K$ is $\{m\}\times\{q\}$
or $A_m\times\{\}$ with $m\ge17$ (Fact~\ref{fact:F2}), and every vertex of
$K$, in particular $v$, lies on an $m$-gon of $K$ (Fact~\ref{fact:F3}).
\end{proof}

Let $s\ge1$ be the number of large 2-faces at a vertex (the same at every
vertex).

\subsection{Exactly one large 2-face at each vertex}\label{sec:s1}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:s1}
If $s=1$, then $P\cong\{n\}\times Q$ for a convex uniform polyhedron $Q$,
or $P\cong A_n\times\{q\}$.
\end{theorem}

Fix $v$, its large 2-face $F$ (an $n$-gon with plane direction $L$) and
$e=[a,b]$ its VF-edge. By Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} and $s=1$, every VF-edge
lies in a VF-facet $\VF(K)$ with $K\supset F$; so \emph{every VF-edge, and
hence every VF-vertex, lies in a facet of the fan of $e$}. The fan is
given by the cycle type of $F$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:F5}).

\begin{proof}[Type (i)]
The fan facets are $T_i=\conv(a,b,x_i,x_{i+1})$, where $[v,x_i]$ is the
lateral edge of the $i$-th cell $F\times[v,x_i]$; so $x_i-v\perp L$, and the
neighbors of $v$ are exactly $a,b,x_1,\dots,x_k$. The $x_i$ are distinct
(two $P_n$ cells at $F$ with the same lateral edge span the same 3-space).

\emph{Product lemma.} For a vertex $t$ let $F_t$ be its unique large
2-face. By vertex-transitivity, the local picture at $t$ is the one at
$v$ (a symmetry mapping $v$ to $t$ maps $F$ to $F_t$, by uniqueness). For a
neighbor $t'$ of $t$, either $t'\in F_t$, and then $F_{t'}=F_t$; or $t'-t$
is a lateral edge, orthogonal to the plane of $F_t$, and the duoprism
$\{n\}\times\{q_i\}$ containing the edge $tt'$ contains the translate
$F_t+(t'-t)$, a large 2-face at $t'$, so $F_{t'}=F_t+(t'-t)$. These
translates come from the actual prism cells at the edge, not from the
shape of the vertex figure. The edge graph of $P$ is connected
\cite{Balinski1961}, so every $F_t$ is a translate $F+\tau_t$ with
$\tau_t\perp L$ (each step adds $0$ or a vector orthogonal to $L$).
Hence the vertex set is $F_0+X$, with $F_0$ the polygon $F$ centered at the
origin of $L$ and $X$ a finite set in an affine copy of $L^\perp$, and
$P=\conv F_0+\conv X\cong\{n\}\times Q$ with $Q=\conv X$, a 3-polytope since
$\dim P=5$. Every point of $X$ is a vertex of $Q$ (a point $(f,x)$ of a
product is a vertex iff both factors are vertices). $Q$ has unit edges and
regular faces, because its edges and 2-faces $G$ give edges and 2-faces
$\{f\}\times G$ of $P$; a symmetry $g$ of $P$ maps $F_t$ to $F_{g(t)}$, so
its linear part preserves $L$ and $L^\perp$ and $g$ induces an isometry of
$Q$; $\Sym(P)$ is transitive on the $F_t$, hence on $X$. So $Q$ is a convex
uniform polyhedron.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Type (ii)]
The fan consists of the pyramids $\Pi_1=(z_1;a,b,y,x)$,
$\Pi_2=(z_2;a,b,y,x)$ (the two $A_n\times\{\}$ sharing the $A_n$ cell $C$,
$\VF(C)=(a,b,y,x)$) and $T=\conv(a,b,z_1,z_2)$. The neighbors of $v$ are
$a,b,x,y$ (in $C$) and $z_1,z_2$, with $z_j-v$ orthogonal to the
3-space $M$ of $C$. \emph{Cell partition:} for a vertex $t$ let $C_t$ be
the unique $A_n$ cell containing $F_t$ (one $A_n$ ray per cycle). For
$t'\in C_t$, $t'$ lies on exactly one of the two $n$-gons of $C_t$ ($n\ge4$),
which is then $F_{t'}$, so $C_{t'}=C_t$. The other two neighbors $t'$ of
$t$ satisfy $t'-t\perp M_t$, and the $A_n\times\{\}$ containing $tt'$ gives
$C_{t'}=C_t+(t'-t)$. So the vertex set is $C_0+X$ with $X$ in a 2-plane
orthogonal to $M$, $P=A_n\times\conv X$, and $\conv X$ is a
vertex-transitive equilateral convex polygon with unit edges, i.e.\ a
regular polygon $\{q\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Type (iii) does not occur]
The fan consists of $\Pi_1$, $\Pi_2$ as in (ii) and $T_1=\conv(a,b,z_1,w)$,
$T_2=\conv(a,b,w,z_2)$, where $w-v$ is the lateral edge of the $P_n$ cell
between the two duoprisms. The seven points $a,b,x,y,z_1,z_2,w$ are
distinct: $a-v,b-v\in L$; $x-v$ and $y-v$ are neither in $L$ nor orthogonal
to $L$ ($x,y$ lie on the other $n$-gon of the antiprism, rotated by $\pi/n$
and at height $h_A>0$), and $x\ne y$; $w-v$, $z_1-v$, $z_2-v$ are orthogonal
to $L$ and are the lateral edges of three different $P_n$ cells. Since
every VF-edge lies in a fan facet, the VF-edges are exactly the edges of
these four facets:
\[ab,\ ax,\ xy,\ yb,\ z_ja,\ z_jb,\ z_jx,\ z_jy\ (j=1,2),\ wa,\ wb,\ wz_1,\ wz_2.\]
In particular $z_1z_2$ is not a VF-edge, and $x$ has exactly the VF-edges
$xa,xy,xz_1,xz_2$, $y$ exactly $yb,yx,yz_1,yz_2$, and $w$ exactly
$wa,wb,wz_1,wz_2$; each of $x,y,w$ is simple (Lemma~\ref{lem:simple}). The
triangle $(x,y,z_1)$ is a 2-face of $\Pi_1$, hence a VF-ridge, lying in
$\Pi_1$ and exactly one other VF-facet $K'$. At $x$, $\Pi_1$ has the edges
$\{xa,xy,xz_1\}$, so $K'$ has $\{xy,xz_1,xz_2\}$; thus $xa$ is not an edge of
$K'$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:face} $a\notin K'$. Likewise, at $y$, $b\notin
K'$. If $w$ were in $K'$, its $K'$-edges would be among $wz_1,wz_2$ (the
others go to $a,b$), degree at most 2 in a 3-polytope, impossible. So
$K'=\conv(x,y,z_1,z_2)$, a tetrahedron, and its edge $z_1z_2$ is an edge of
$\VF$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:face}, converse), which it is not.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Two or more large 2-faces at each vertex}\label{sec:s2}

The fan of a large $n$-gon $F$ at $v$ is \emph{paired} if one of its
facets contains a second large 2-face through $v$: a type-(i) cycle with
exactly one $q_i=m\ge17$ (two such entries would give an angle sum of at
least $2(1-2/17)+1/3>2$), or type (ii) with $q=m\ge17$. Otherwise it is
\emph{unpaired}: type (i) with all $q_i\le16$, type (ii) with $q\le16$, or
type (iii) ($q_1,q_2\le5$). In a paired fan the second large 2-face's
VF-edge (the \emph{partner edge}) is $[x_1,x_2]$ in type (i) and
$[z_1,z_2]$ in type (ii).

The paired type-(i) configurations are, by the angle sum and
Lemma~\ref{lem:typei}: $(m,4,4)$, $(m,3,3,3)$, $(m,3,6)$ for all $m\ge17$;
$(m,3,7)$ for $m\le41$; $(m,3,8)$ for $m\le23$; $(17,3,9)$; $(m,4,5)$ for
$m\le19$; and $(m,3,3)$, $(m,3,4)$, $(m,3,5)$ are not closable.

\subsubsection{(P1): the family-polygon filter}

Let $c$ be an unpaired type-(i) configuration (entries at most 16) around
$F$ containing a family polygon size $q\in\{7,9,11,\dots,16\}$. The $q$-gon
$Q_0$ of the fan is a 2-face of $P$ lying in the fan facet
$\{n\}\times\{q\}$, so its cycle (Lemma~\ref{lem:F5} with $q$ in place of $n$)
contains $\{q\}\times\{n\}$. Type (iii) would need both duoprisms at $Q_0$ to
have parameter at most 5, so the cycle of $Q_0$ is of type (i) with a
configuration $(n,c'')$, or of type (ii) with the duoprism
$\{q\}\times\{n\}$. Comparing radii with $R^2=r_n^2+R_c^2$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item type (ii) at $Q_0$: $R^2=R_{A_q}^2+r_n^2$, so $R_c^2=R_{A_q}^2$, i.e.\
  $R_c^2-r_q^2=h_A(q)^2/4$;
\item type (i) at $Q_0$: $R^2=r_q^2+R_{(n,c'')}^2=r_q^2+r_n^2+\delta_{c''}(n)$,
  so $R_c^2-r_q^2=\delta_{c''}(n)$, where $(n,c'')$ is closable with
  $(1-2/n)+\sum_{p\in c''}(1-2/p)<2$. For $n\ge17$ the angle sum leaves
  $c''\in\{(3,3),(3,4),\dots,(3,9),(4,4),(4,5),(3,3,3)\}$ and restricts
  $(3,7)$ to $n\le41$, $(3,8)$ to $n\le23$, $(3,9)$ to $n=17$ and $(4,5)$ to
  $n\le19$; closability (Lemma~\ref{lem:typei}) removes $(3,3)$, $(3,4)$,
  $(3,5)$ (for $(3,5)$: $1+\ch_5^2<\ch_n^2$ for $n\ge11$, so the chord triangle
  is obtuse), and the remaining ones are closable in these ranges.
\end{itemize}

\begin{computation}[P1]\label{comp:P1}
Of the 168 cyclic configurations with entries in $3..16$ and angle sum below
$2\pi$, 137 are closable, 28 not closable and 3 degenerate. Of the 137, 83
contain a family polygon. For 67 of these 83 there is a family polygon $q$
in $c$ such that $X:=R_c^2-r_q^2$ is certified different from $h_A(q)^2/4$,
from $1/4$ ($c''=(4,4)$), from the range $[0.186963,3/16]$ of
$\delta_{(3,3,3)}$, from the range $[1/3,0.337262]$ of $\delta_{(3,6)}$, and from
each of the finitely many values $\delta_{(3,7)}(n)$, $n\le41$,
$\delta_{(3,8)}(n)$, $n\le23$, $\delta_{(3,9)}(17)$, $\delta_{(4,5)}(n)$,
$n\le19$. The 16 others are $(4,4,q)$ and $(3,3,3,q)$, $q\in\{7,9,11,\dots,16\}$.
\end{computation}

\noindent Hence the 67 configurations do not occur as fans in a convex
uniform 5-polytope. The 16 survivors are genuine ($\{n\}\times P_q$ and
$\{n\}\times A_q$: $\delta_{(4,4)}=1/4=R_{P_q}^2-r_q^2$, and $(3,3,3,q)$ is the
vertex configuration of $A_q$). \emph{What is computed.} The first implementation,
\texttt{caseb\_famfilter.py} (Arb, 200 bits; $R_c$ by certified bisection
of $f_c$; closability by the sign of $f_c(c_{\max})-2\pi$; the three
degenerate configurations by the exact right-triangle identities; the $c''$
list hard-coded with its $n$ ranges, which the program checks against the
angle sum). Independent checker \file{checks/check_caseb/p1check.py}
(\texttt{mpmath.iv} at 200 bits, sympy for the exact identities) recomputes
the $c''$ list from the angle condition with closability tested, uses
closed forms for $(4,4)$, $(3,6)$, $(3,3,3)$ over all $n\ge17$ including
$n=\infty$, and gets the same 168/137/28/3/83/67 and the same 16
survivors, with no sporadic $(c'',n)$ coincidence. The Arb implementation is a
second rigorous implementation only together with the written rational
angle and chord arguments above for its hard-coded $c''$ list and $n$
ranges; its sampled tails are non-proof checks. Corruptions: changing
$\delta_{(4,4)}$ by $10^{-30}$, or using $h^2/2$ for $h^2/4$ in $R_{A_q}^2$, makes
the corresponding survivors disappear (75 excluded).
\subsubsection{(W): the wedge lemma}\label{sec:W}

A point $p$ is \emph{admissible} for a fan if it lies on the VF sphere
($|p-v|=1$, $|p-c|=R$), on the closed inner side of every fan facet's
hyperplane, and at distance at least 1 from every fan vertex. Every
VF-vertex that is not a fan vertex is admissible ($\VF$ is convex and the
fan facets are VF-facets; Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}). An \emph{allowed} fan
vertex is a fan vertex other than $a$, $b$ and, for a paired fan, the
partner ends. The partner ends are distinct from the other fan vertices: in
type (ii) the apexes satisfy $z_j-v\perp M$ while $x-v,y-v\in M$; in type (i) the $x_i$ are distinct
lateral edges.

\begin{computation}[W]\label{comp:W}
For each fan in the list below and every real $n\ge17$, including the
limit $n=\infty$ (and, in (c), every real $m\ge17$ including $m=\infty$;
in (d), every \emph{integer} $m$ in the stated range), there are no two
points $p,p'$ with $|p-p'|\ge\ch_{17}$, each admissible or
an allowed fan vertex, other than two fan vertices. The list:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item type (i): the 70 closable configurations with entries at most 16
  that are not excluded by (P1);
\item type (ii), $q=3,\dots,16$; type (iii), $(3,3)$, $(3,4)$, $(3,5)$;
\item paired: $(m,4,4)$, $(m,3,3,3)$, $(m,3,6)$ and type (ii) with $q=m$, all
  $m\ge17$ and $m=\infty$;
\item paired, bounded, with $m$ an integer: $(m,3,7)$, $17\le m\le37$;
  $(m,3,8)$, $17\le m\le22$; $(m,4,5)$, $17\le m\le18$, for all real
  $n\ge17$; and $(m,3,7)$, $38\le m\le41$, $(23,3,8)$, $(17,3,9)$,
  $(19,4,5)$ at the integer point $n=m$ only.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, for each fan in the list, every pair of fan vertices other than
$e$, the partner edge and the diagonals $x_ix_j$ of a type-(i) fan has
distance below $\ch_{17}$ (the largest is the 16-gon chord $1.961571\ldots$;
in type (iii) $|z_1-z_2|\le1.943206$).
\end{computation}

\noindent\emph{Scope of the two implementations.} The Arb
certificate tests pairs in which each point is admissible or an
\emph{allowed} fan vertex, so it omits pairs with one end in
$\{a,b\}$ or at a partner end, and its fan-vertex distance bound covers
pairs not involving $a$ or $b$; the omitted cases are supplied by
Lemma~\ref{lem:long} and by the explicit lengths of Fact~\ref{fact:F3}
(Lemma~\ref{lem:Wuse}). The independent checker tests every fan vertex,
including $a$, $b$ and the partner ends (its test V), and every pair of fan
vertices other than $e$ and the partner edge (its test F), and certifies
them. Its certification flag (\texttt{CERTIFIED}) requires test F only for the
pairs of the ``Moreover'' clause; the type-(i) diagonals are tested in the
same run and reported separately, and in the full replays none of them is
long (``long type-(i) diagonals: none''). The proof does not need that
supplementary result, since Lemma~\ref{lem:Wuse} excludes diagonals
geometrically.

\noindent\emph{Parameter domains}.
The continuous parameter $n$ (and $m$ in (c)) is covered by compact charts
that include the limits; the bounded families of (d) are generated at the
36 integer slices $(m,3,7)$, $17\le m\le41$; $(m,3,8)$, $17\le m\le23$;
$(17,3,9)$; $(m,4,5)$, $17\le m\le19$, and are \emph{not} certified for
non-integer $m$. Integer $m$ suffices for polytopes. Both
implementations enforce these 36 integer slices and the seven integer
points $n=m$ (fractional or out-of-range $m$ is refused before any
arithmetic, and the checker's output records the parameter domain of every
row).

\noindent\emph{The seven exceptional fans.} Over the full range of $n$,
(W) fails for $(m,3,7)$, $m=38,\dots,41$, $(23,3,8)$, $(17,3,9)$, $(19,4,5)$,
with rigorous interval witnesses at large finite $n$ (e.g.\ $n=547$ for
$(41,3,7)$ and $(19,4,5)$), and also for the (P1)-excluded configurations
$(3,11,13)$, $(4,7,9)$, $(5,6,7)$. For the seven paired fans, the partner
$m$-gon's own cycle restricts $n$:

\begin{lemma}[Partner-radius filter]\label{lem:partner}
If the fan of $F$ (an $n$-gon) is paired of type (i) with configuration
$(m,c_1)$, $c_1$ one of $(3,7)$, $(3,8)$, $(3,9)$, $(4,5)$, then the fan of the
partner $m$-gon is of type (i) with configuration $(n,c_2)$ and
$\delta_{c_1}(m)=\delta_{c_2}(n)$, or of type (ii) with
$\delta_{c_1}(m)=h_A(m)^2/4$. For the seven exceptional fans this forces
$c_2=c_1$ and $n=m$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
From $F$: $R^2=r_n^2+R_{(m,c_1)}^2=r_n^2+r_m^2+\delta_{c_1}(m)$. The partner's
cycle contains $\{m\}\times\{n\}$, so it is of type (i) with a configuration
$(n,c_2)$ or of type (ii) with $q=n$ (type (iii) needs parameters at most
5); this gives $R^2=r_m^2+r_n^2+\delta_{c_2}(n)$ or $R^2=R_{A_m}^2+r_n^2=r_m^2+h_A(m)^2/4+r_n^2$.
For the seven fans $\delta_{c_1}(m)>99$ (from $99.67$ for $(17,3,9)$ to
$1040.7$ for $(41,3,7)$), while $h_A^2/4<1/4$,
$\delta_{(4,4)}=1/4$, $\delta_{(3,3,3)}<3/16$, $\delta_{(3,6)}\le0.3373$, so $c_2$ is
one of the bounded families, and a certified comparison of the finitely
many values (\file{checks/check_caseb/partner.py} and \texttt{wpoint.py}) leaves only
$c_2=c_1$, $n=m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Wuse}
Let $F$ be a large 2-face at $v$ whose fan is in the list of
Computation~\ref{comp:W} (with the actual $n$, $m$ of $P$). Then the only
long VF-edges with an end among the fan vertices or an admissible point
are $e$ and, for a paired fan, the partner edge. In particular every long
VF-edge is $e$ or the partner edge.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $[p,p']$ be a long VF-edge other than $e$ and the partner edge. Its ends
are VF-vertices, each a fan vertex or admissible. Neither end is $a$, $b$ or
a partner end, by Lemma~\ref{lem:long} (those are already ends of a long
edge). So both are admissible or allowed fan vertices, and
Computation~\ref{comp:W} leaves only the case that both are fan vertices.
Pairs of fan vertices other than diagonals are shorter than $\ch_{17}$
(Computation~\ref{comp:W}, last part; pairs with $a$ or $b$ have the
explicit lengths $\sqrt2$, $1$ or $\sqrt{1+\ch_n}<\sqrt3$ of
Fact~\ref{fact:F3}). A diagonal $x_ix_j$ of a type-(i) fan is not a
VF-edge: the $x_i$ satisfy $(x_i-v)\perp L$, $|x_i-v|=1$ and lie on the VF
hyperplane, so they lie on a circle; the fan edges $x_ix_{i+1}$ are
VF-edges, so the circle order is the fan order (otherwise two fan edges
would cross in their relative interiors, and a face containing the
crossing point would contain all four ends); for $k\ge4$ a diagonal crosses
another diagonal in their relative interiors, so a face containing it would
contain four vertices; for $k=3$ there are no diagonals. Since every
VF-vertex is a fan vertex or admissible, the last claim follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The Arb certificate of (W) does not test pairs in which one
point is $a$, $b$ or a partner end, and Lemma~\ref{lem:long} supplies
exactly those cases. The independent checker tests them directly
(test V over all fan vertices) and certifies them, so (W) as used here
holds with or without the lemma.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{The reduction to two paired 2-faces}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reduction}
If $s\ge2$, then $s=2$ and the two large 2-faces $F_1$ ($n$-gon) and $F_2$
($m$-gon) at $v$ lie in a common facet $D=\{n\}\times\{m\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $F$ be a large 2-face at $v$. If its fan is unpaired: a type-(i)
configuration excluded by (P1) cannot occur (Computation~\ref{comp:P1});
every other unpaired fan is in the list of Computation~\ref{comp:W}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:Wuse} shows that $e$ is the only long VF-edge, so $s=1$, a
contradiction. So every large 2-face at $v$ has a paired fan. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:partner} the paired fan of $F$ is in the list of
Computation~\ref{comp:W} at the actual $n$, $m$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:Wuse}
shows that the long VF-edges are exactly $e$ and the partner edge. So
$s=2$, and the two large 2-faces lie in the fan facet $\{n\}\times\{m\}$
that makes the fan paired.
\end{proof}

\noindent The paired type-(i) configurations not in the list of
Computation~\ref{comp:W}, $(m,3,3)$, $(m,3,4)$, $(m,3,5)$, are not closable.
The closability criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:typei} excludes $(3,5)$:
a spherical triangle of rays exists for $(n,3,5)$, $n<30$, but the center
lies outside it. The paired families are covered by
Computation~\ref{comp:W}(d) and Lemma~\ref{lem:partner}.

\subsubsection{The paired theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:paired}
If every vertex lies in exactly two large 2-faces $F_1$ ($n$-gon) and $F_2$
($m$-gon), and they lie in a common facet $D=\{n\}\times\{m\}$, then $P$ is
$\{n\}\times\{m\}\times\{\}$, $A_m\times\{n\}$ or $A_n\times\{m\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $e_1=[a,b]$ and $e_2=[c,d]$ be the VF-edges of $F_1$, $F_2$; then
$\VF(D)=\conv(a,b,c,d)$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:B2} every VF-edge lies in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$ or $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$, so every VF-vertex is a vertex of
one of the two fans. The fan of $F_1$ is of type (i) with configuration
$(m,q_2,\dots,q_k)$, $x_1=c$, $x_2=d$, or of type (ii) with apexes
$\{z_1,z_2\}=\{c,d\}$ (as $T=\VF(D)$); likewise for $F_2$ with $n$. The angle
sum allows only $k=3$, or $k=4$ with configuration $(m,3,3,3)$ (the
configurations $(m,4,p,p')$ and $(m,3,p,p')\ne(m,3,3,3)$ exceed $2\pi$).

\emph{Simple-vertex step.} A vertex $z$ of $\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$ not in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ has all its VF-edges in $\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$. If it has
degree 4, each 3-subset of its edges spans a VF-facet
(Lemma~\ref{lem:simple}), and a VF-facet containing $c$ and $d$ contains
the edge $e_2$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:face}), hence belongs to $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$.

\emph{(i)/(i), $k=3$.} $z=x_3$ has the VF-edges $za,zb,zc,zd$ in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$. If $z\notin\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$, the facet spanned by
$\{za,zc,zd\}$ contains $e_2$, so $z\in\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ after all. In a
type-(i) fan of $e_2$ every vertex other than $c,d$ is at distance $\sqrt2$
from $c$ and $d$ (Fact~\ref{fact:F3}), so
$\ch_{q_2}=|z-d|=\sqrt2=|z-c|=\ch_{q_3}$: the configuration is $(m,4,4)$. The
fan of $F_2$ is not of type (ii) (case (i)/(ii) with $k=3$, below) and
not of type (i) with $k=4$ (case (i)/(i), $k=4$, below, with the roles of
$F_1$ and $F_2$ exchanged), so by the same argument it is $(n,4,4)$, with the
same third vertex $z$. \emph{Translate argument:} for a vertex $t$ let $D_t$ be the
facet containing its two large 2-faces. For $t'\in D_t$, both large 2-faces
of $D_t$ at $t'$ are large 2-faces of $P$, so $D_{t'}=D_t$. The
$\{n\}\times\{4\}$ and $\{m\}\times\{4\}$ facets at the edge $vz$ contain
$F_1+\tau$ and $F_2+\tau$, $\tau=z-v\perp\spn D$, so $D_z=D+\tau$. Hence the
vertex set is $D_0+X$ with $X$ on a line, every point of $X$ is a vertex, so
$|X|=2$ and $P=\{n\}\times\{m\}\times\{\}$.

\emph{(i)/(i), $k=4$: both fans of type (i), that of $F_1$ with
configuration $(m,3,3,3)$.} Here
$|x_3-d|=|x_4-c|=\ch_3=1\ne\sqrt2$, so neither $x_3$ nor $x_4$ lies in a
type-(i) fan of $e_2$. Then both are simple with all edges in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$: $x_3$ has $x_3a,x_3b,x_3d,x_3x_4$ and $x_4$ has
$x_4a,x_4b,x_4x_3,x_4c$. The facet $G$ at $x_3$ spanned by
$\{x_3a,x_3d,x_3x_4\}$ contains $x_4$ and $a$, hence the edge $x_4a$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:face}); it does not contain $b$ (else $x_3b$ would be a
fourth edge of $G$ at the simple vertex $x_3$). So at $x_4$ its edges are
$\{x_4a,x_4x_3,x_4c\}$, and $G$ contains $c$ and $d$, so $G\in\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$
and $x_3\in\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$, a contradiction. Impossible.

\emph{(i)/(ii).} $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ is the pair of $A_m\times\{\}$ with
apexes $a,b$ and trapezoid $(c,d,y',x')$, with $|dy'|=|y'x'|=|x'c|=1$ and
diagonals $\sqrt{1+\ch_m}\in[1.7222,\sqrt3)$, strictly between $\ch_5$ and
$\ch_6$, hence not a chord $\ch_q$ of any polygon. If $k=3$, $x_3$ must lie in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ (as above) at distances $\ch_{q_2}$, $\ch_{q_3}$ from $d$,
$c$; but the vertices of $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ other than $a,b,c,d$ are $x',y'$,
each at distance $1$ from one of $c,d$ and at the diagonal distance from the
other, and the diagonal is not a chord; impossible. If
$k=4$, $(m,3,3,3)$: by the argument of the previous case one of $x_3,x_4$
lies in $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$; by symmetry say $x_3$; $|x_3-d|=1$ forces
$x_3=y'$. If $x_4\notin\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$, $x_4$ is simple and its facet
spanned by $\{x_4a,x_4x_3,x_4c\}$ meets the pyramid $\Pi(a;c,d,y',x')$ in a
face containing $a,c,y'$; since $c,y'$ are opposite vertices of the base, no
proper face of the pyramid contains these three, so the facet is the
pyramid and $x_4\in\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$. Hence $\{x_3,x_4\}=\{y',x'\}$ and the
neighbors of $v$ are those of $\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$. \emph{Cell partition:}
at every vertex exactly one large 2-face has a type-(ii) fan (symmetries
preserve fan types; this also holds when $n=m$); let $C_t$ be its $A_m$
cell. For $t'\in C_t$ the $m$-gon of $C_t$ through $t'$ has $C_t$ in its
cycle, so it is not of type (i): it is the type-(ii) 2-face at $t'$, and
$C_{t'}=C_t$. The other neighbors $a,b$ of $v$ are the apexes of
$\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$, so $a-v$ and $b-v$ are orthogonal to the 3-space of
$C_v$ and $C_a=C_v+(a-v)$. So $P=A_m\times\conv X$ with $\conv X$ a regular
polygon, here $F_1$: $P=A_m\times\{n\}$. The case (ii)/(i) gives
$A_n\times\{m\}$.

\emph{(ii)/(ii).} The trapezoid vertex $x$ of $\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$ (edges
$xa,xy,xc,xd$ in $\mathrm{fan}(e_1)$, the apexes being $c,d$) must lie in
$\mathrm{fan}(e_2)$ (otherwise the facet spanned by $\{xy,xc,xd\}$ contains
$e_2$), so $x\in\{x',y'\}$ at distance $1$ or $\sqrt{1+\ch_m}$ from $c$. But
$|x-c|=\sqrt2$, since $c$ is an apex of a prism over the $A_n$ cell
containing $x$. Impossible.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:B}}

Let $P$ have a large 2-face and let $s$ be the number of large 2-faces at a
vertex. If $s=1$, Theorem~\ref{thm:s1} gives $\{n\}\times Q$ or
$A_n\times\{q\}=\{q\}\times A_n$. If $s\ge2$, Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}
gives $s=2$ with both large 2-faces in one duoprism, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:paired} gives $\{n\}\times\{m\}\times\{\}$, $\{n\}\times A_m$
or $\{m\}\times A_n$. \qed

\subsection{Proof of the Main Theorem}

A convex uniform 5-polytope either has all 2-faces with at most 16 sides
(Theorem~\ref{thm:A}: one of the 628 polytopes of $\mathcal K_{16}$, all of
type (K1)--(K3)) or has a 2-face with at least 17 sides
(Theorem~\ref{thm:B}: of type (K3)). \qed

\subsection{Arithmetic and software}\label{sec:arithmetic}

The first implementations use ball arithmetic (Arb through python-flint
\cite{Johansson2017}) at 200 bits unless stated, and exact algebra in
$\Q$, $\Q(\sqrt2)$, $\Q(\sqrt5)$ (hand-written fields and SymPy
minimal polynomials). In the proof routes (Section~\ref{sec:computations}), every
decision that cuts a branch, excludes a case or identifies two objects is
either a certified inequality between enclosures, an exact algebraic
identity, an exact equivalence certified by an explicit permutation, a
combinatorial fact, or an equality of two vertices of one (possibly
hypothetical) polytope decided by Corollary~\ref{cor:decide}(a); an
undecided case stops the computation or is reported \texttt{OPEN}, and the
final runs report zero such cases. Floating-point arithmetic is used to
propose (orderings, candidate correspondences, witnesses); the one
remaining decision made from a double is covered by the proved coordinate
lemma of Section~\ref{sec:U3}. The \file{checks/check_u3/} geometric and transcendental routines and
\file{checks/check_caseb/} use \texttt{mpmath.iv}, exact arithmetic or
outward-rounded binary64 intervals. Those routines avoid Arb, but the
complete replay also calls exact model and root-audit code using Arb.
Both \file{checks/independent-s1-b2/} and
\file{checks/independent-s23/} use Arb and SymPy; their independence is in
implementation, not arithmetic library. All public stages use the same
Python environment, specified in \file{checks/requirements.txt}.

\paragraph{Exact angle decisions versus enclosures.}
Angles are compared exactly through algebraic cosines, positive sines and
phase products, rather than by equating decimal arccosines. In stage~1,
flat angle sums $\sum\alpha_i=2\pi$ and degenerate closure roots use the
exact operands of Computation~\ref{comp:exact} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:wordangle}: the non-snub squared cosines lie in
$\Q$, $\Q(\sqrt2)$ or $\Q(\sqrt5)$, with positive square roots and,
where needed, further algebraic number-field extensions fixed by the
constructions. The phase identity
$\prod_j(\cos\alpha_j+i\sin\alpha_j)=1$ is exact; a certified interval
for the sum in $(\pi,3\pi)$ fixes its winding. In B2, polygon angle-sum
inequalities $\sum(1-2/q_i)<2$ are rational comparisons, and the three
right-triangle degeneracies are identities in $\Q(\sqrt5)$
(\texttt{b2check.py}, \texttt{angle\_ok}, \texttt{exact\_degenerate};
Lemma~\ref{lem:typei}). General angles, strict sign/branch bounds,
closure-root isolation, radius separation and continuous parameter ranges
use outward-rounded Arb balls or certified intervals instead
(Computations~\ref{comp:lib}, \ref{comp:S1}, \ref{comp:root},
\ref{comp:B2}, \ref{comp:P1}, \ref{comp:W}). An interval containing zero
never proves an algebraic equality; exact residuals and sign information
supply those decisions.

\subsection{Computational evidence and shared dependencies}\label{sec:computations}

The computations consist of exact model checks, the facet-library and
stage-1 cycle checks, radius separation, the finite stage-2/3 searches,
and Case B's fan and parameter-domain checks. The packaged commands and
inputs are described in the bundle's \file{README.md}. Their paths are
relative to the bundle root.

\paragraph{Shared inputs.}
Independently written programs do not give fully independent proofs of
every premise. The exact-exclusion checks share the exact constructions
used by the independent stage-1 library. The stage-2/3 implementations
share stage-1 class intervals, facet and known-name lists, exceptional-model
certificates, the separation lemma and other written geometry. The alias
table and construction theorems are also shared. The implementations use
common arithmetic libraries, including Arb and SymPy. P1's second
implementation depends on the written angle, chord and monotonicity
arguments for its finite lists and tails.

\paragraph{Written mathematical premises.}
Facts~\ref{fact:3d} and~\ref{fact:4d} are cited classification theorems,
not re-proved here. Wythoff and product constructions, the geometric
reductions, coverage and interpretation of the finite search, and the
unbounded-parameter reductions use the written arguments in this paper.
A successful executable check establishes its stated finite or interval
claims; it does not replace these arguments or establish priority.

\subsection{The four-dimensional control}\label{sec:control}
A bounded control using the shared interval root solver and certified
stage-2/3 engines with exact four-dimensional facet adapters completed
639 classes at family cutoff 14. It recovered all 152 known objects
(185 construction names), including the grand antiprism and snub 24-cell,
and refuted 33 other realizations with no unresolved, missing or extra
object. All 1,476 support witnesses passed the local checker; deletion and
support corruptions were rejected. This is a bounded non-proof control, separate from the public replay;
it does not re-prove Möller's all-parameter classification.

\subsection{How to reproduce}
First follow the Python environment setup in the bundle's
\file{checks/README.md}, creating the single \texttt{.venv} environment. The checks require Python 3.11 or later and Bash 4.4 or later.
From the top of the bundle, run
\begin{verbatim}
PYTHON="$PWD/.venv/bin/python" bash checks/verify-public.sh full
\end{verbatim}
Only \texttt{full} mode checks all computational claims used in the theorem;
\texttt{quick} mode does not check the theorem. Full mode includes P=16
stages 2/3, all W fans, certificate authentication and corruption controls.
Neither mode verifies the written reductions automatically.

\section{What is checked in Lean}\label{sec:lean}
%======================================================================

The directory \file{lean/} is a Lean project with dependencies specified in
\file{lean/lean-toolchain} and \file{lean/lake-manifest.json}. It contains
geometric lemmas and a conditional classification of genuine convex
unit-edge uniform five-dimensional polytopes. The formalization is incomplete: the external numerical data and their geometric
correspondence have not been jointly instantiated and discharged in Lean.

\paragraph{The conditional composition.}
With explicit hypotheses as parameters,
\file{lean/Uniform5/CompositionMainClassification.lean} proves
\[
  \forall P:\operatorname{Set}(\operatorname{Space}5),\quad
  \operatorname{UnitUniform}(5,P)\ \Longrightarrow\
  K_1(P)\lor K_2(P)\lor K_3(P).
\]
Here \texttt{UnitUniform} means a finite convex hull of full affine
dimension five, ambient vertex transitivity, recursively uniform facets
and unit exposed edges. The conclusions are the explicit Wythoff orbit
hulls and two product families, up to intrinsic Euclidean congruence.
The geometry-equivalence module connects the hull and vertex-set
formulations. The composition supplies the finite fan realization and
admissibility needed to apply W and combines the small-face and
large-face arguments; it does not assume a Case A or Case B conclusion
in place of that composition.

\paragraph{Assumed inputs.}
Both \texttt{mainClassification} and
\texttt{classification\_of\_core\_inputs} take twelve computational
hypotheses and the two cited classifications, besides their raw data.
The unused FILTER, RIDGES, QFOLLOW and LU hypotheses and their data
parameters have been removed. Appendix~\ref{app:lean-inputs} prints the
twelve declarations verbatim.

Every computational input remains an assumed bound parameter in Lean.
The external computations and written arguments establish the intended
numerical and geometric assertions; no full external numerical search is
discharged by the kernel. Checking report fields does not establish their geometric meaning or
construct exact placements. Their correspondence to certificates,
including coverage, canonical rigidity, refutation and forcing, remains
an external semantic obligation.

The contracts distinguish coverage from existence. S1 asserts coverage
and statuses without a merged-interval-width bound. S2 and U3 cover every
actual figure or star, without asserting that every retained encoding is
realizable. DEV is conditional on the hypothetical polytope's root,
center and entire incident-facet star; it excludes that actual star, not
every star having the same unlabeled figure. W is restricted to finite
parameters, sufficient for actual polygonal faces, with real/integer
ranges and exceptional slices explicit. The known models have separate
existence arguments; the extra candidate needs no existence assertion.

\paragraph{Build and axiom audit.}
From \file{lean/}, run \texttt{lake exe cache get}, \texttt{lake build}
and \texttt{lake env lean Audit.lean}. The audit rejects dependencies
other than \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound}.
These checks prove the stated implications, not their computational premises.
The supplied modules express the sphere and separation lemmas,
vertex-figure geometry, the centroid argument, the face lemma, rooted
rigidity, local-to-global propagation, and conditional Case B arguments.

\paragraph{What remains outside Lean.}
The following written-to-computation links are not formalized:
the numerical library/model correspondence
(Section~\ref{sec:facetdata}); stage-1 enumeration
and root coverage (Section~\ref{sec:S1}); the coverage of actual vertex
figures by stage-2 signatures (Section~\ref{sec:S2}); stage-3 encodings,
identification and neighbor forcing (Section~\ref{sec:S3} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:u3}); the exhaustive stuck-development refutation
of the extra candidate (Section~\ref{sec:extra}); and the numerical
B2, P1, partner-radius and W bounds used by the Case B structural proof
(Section~\ref{sec:caseB}). The conditional geometric lemmas do not
establish these hypotheses for the supplied certificates.
In particular, rooted determinism (Lemma~\ref{lem:rooted}), congruence
from discrete data (Lemma~\ref{lem:D}) and coset comparison
(Lemma~\ref{lem:coset}) are not formalized end to end for the concrete
program encodings and candidate lists. The finite catalog coverage itself
is derived conditionally from the cited classifications in
\texttt{unitUniform4\_smallFaces\_catalogue}.
No Lean theorem jointly instantiates the raw data and all twelve
computational hypotheses with the two cited classifications; their joint
truth rests on these written arguments and the external checkers.
A kernel proof of their implication alone proves neither their truth nor
their joint satisfiability. The classification is not a whole-theorem Lean proof.
