\begin{abstract}
Let $S$ be a compact orientable surface, $f$ a mapping class of $S$, and
$t$ a Dehn multitwist about disjoint curves $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ whose
$f$-orbits together fill $S$. Ivanov asked, as Problem~5 of his 2006 list
and of an earlier list dated 1994--1998, whether the integers $j$ for which $t^j f$ is
not pseudo-Anosov must lie among $N_S$ consecutive integers, for a constant
$N_S$ depending only on $S$. For a single Dehn twist on a closed surface this
holds with $N_S=7$, by a theorem of Fathi (1987), which he presents as
generalizing one of Long and Morton for pseudo-Anosov $f$; his theorem
assumes only that the $f$-orbit of the twist curve fills. We show that
this bound does not extend to multitwists: in Ivanov's orbit-filling form,
with reducible base maps, no such $N_S$ exists when $S$ is the sphere with
five boundary components, which answers Ivanov's Problem~5 negatively; his
weaker question, which bounds only the number of exceptions, is
untouched. For every $k\ge1$ we
give $f_k$ and a two-component multitwist $t$ satisfying the
hypothesis for which $t^0f_k$ and $t^{-k}f_k$ both fix an essential curve.
So two exceptional exponents can lie arbitrarily far apart. The base maps
$f_k$ are themselves reducible, so this gives no counterexample with a
pseudo-Anosov base map, the setting of Long and Morton. The
counterexample is on the five-holed sphere only; we claim nothing about
other surfaces. The proof is a short written argument; its braid-group
algebra is checked in Lean, and the finite intersection and fixed-curve
facts it uses are checked by two independent programs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $S$ be a compact orientable surface, possibly with boundary, and let
$\Mod_S=\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(S))$ be its mapping class group. A mapping
class is \emph{pseudo-Anosov} if it is neither periodic nor reducible
(it fixes no finite set of disjoint essential curves); see~\cite{FM,FLP}.
Many pseudo-Anosov classes come from Dehn twists. Long and
Morton~\cite{LM} and then Fathi~\cite{Fathi} studied the sequence
$T_\gamma^j f$, $j\in\Z$, where $T_\gamma$ is the Dehn twist about a curve
$\gamma$. Fathi proved, for a closed surface $M$:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Theorem 0.1 of~\cite{Fathi}.} ``Let $f$ be in $\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(M))$ and let $\gamma$ be in $\mathcal{S}(M)$. If the orbit of $\gamma$ under $f$ fills $M$, then $T_\gamma^n f$ is a pseudo-Anosov class except for at most 7 consecutive values of $n$.''
\end{quote}

\noindent Here $\mathcal{S}(M)$ is the set of isotopy classes of simple closed
curves. Long and Morton~\cite{LM} had proved, for pseudo-Anosov $f$, that
$T_\gamma^jf$ is pseudo-Anosov for all but finitely many $j$, with no bound
on the number of exceptions, as Watanabe~\cite[\S1.1]{Watanabe} states
it; we have not read their article. Fathi's summary says: ``we generalize a
theorem of Long and Morton to obtain that if $f$ is a pseudo-Anosov
diffeomorphism of an oriented surface and $T_\gamma$ is the Dehn twist
around the simple closed curve $\gamma$, then the isotopy class of
$T_\gamma^nf$ contains a pseudo-Anosov diffeomorphism except for at most 7
consecutive values of $n$''~\cite{Fathi}, and Watanabe writes that Fathi
``effectivised the above result of Long--Morton''~\cite[\S1.1]{Watanabe}.
Theorem~0.1 itself assumes only that the $f$-orbit of $\gamma$ fills, so $f$
may be reducible; Cumplido and Wiest state it in this form, for any mapping
class~\cite[Theorem~3]{CW}. Boyer, Gordon and Zhang later improved the constant $7$ to
$6$~\cite{BGZ}, as recorded by Watanabe~\cite[\S1.1]{Watanabe}.

Ivanov asked whether Fathi's theorem extends to multitwists, with a
constant depending only on the surface~\cite{Ivanov94,Ivanov06}. Let $t$ be
a Dehn multitwist $T_{\alpha_1}^{\pm1}\cdots T_{\alpha_n}^{\pm1}$ about disjoint
curves $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$, and suppose that the curves $f^m(\alpha_i)$,
$1\le i\le n$, $m\in\Z$, together fill $S$, that is, every essential curve
has nonzero geometric intersection number with one of them. His \emph{consecutive-integers}
question asks whether the $j$ with $t^j\circ f$ not pseudo-Anosov always lie
among $N_S$ consecutive integers, for a constant $N_S$ depending only on
$S$; for a single twist on a closed surface this is Fathi's theorem with
$N_S=7$. His weaker \emph{count} question asks only that at most $N_S$ of
the $t^j\circ f$ fail to be pseudo-Anosov. Both were posed as Problem~5
of his 2006 list and of an earlier list~\cite{Ivanov94}, for compact
orientable surfaces possibly with boundary; Section~\ref{sec:question}
quotes the problem in full. We answer the first question, for one surface,
and say nothing about the second. All curves below are essential in the
usual sense (Section~\ref{sec:conventions}).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $S$ be the sphere with five boundary components. There are essential
simple closed curves $a,d,c,C',C$ on $S$ such that for every $k\ge1$ and
each $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$ the mapping class $f_k=T_a^kT_d$ and the
multitwist $t=T_aT_c^{\varepsilon}$ satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a$ and $c$ are disjoint and not isotopic, so $t$ is a Dehn multitwist
  with two components and nonzero exponents (a product of left twists when
  $\varepsilon=1$);
\item the set $\{f_k^m(a),f_k^m(c):m\in\Z\}$ fills $S$; already
  $\{a,c,f_k(a)\}$ does;
\item $t^0f_k=T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and $t^{-k}f_k=T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$
  fixes $C$, so neither is pseudo-Anosov.
\end{enumerate}
Hence the set of $j$ with $t^jf_k$ not pseudo-Anosov contains $0$ and $-k$,
and no constant $N_S$ as in the consecutive-integers question exists for
this $S$.
\end{theorem}

Composition is right to left, as in Ivanov's $t^j\circ f$: $T_a^kT_d$ applies
$T_d$ first. The theorem holds verbatim for right-handed twists, and for
$f_kt^j$ in place of $t^jf_k$, since the two are conjugate by $f_k$
(Remark~\ref{rem:conventions}).

\paragraph{What is new, and the key verification.} The mechanism is
simple. Since $T_a$ and $T_c$ commute,
$t^jf_k=T_a^{k+j}T_c^{\varepsilon j}T_d$, and the two components of $t$
have exponents that vanish at different values of $j$: at $j=-k$ the twist
about $a$ disappears and at $j=0$ the twist about $c$ does. Each vanishing
leaves a product of twists about two curves of the pentagon of
Figure~\ref{fig:curves}, and such a product fixes the curve of the pentagon
disjoint from both. The key verification is the hypothesis: Ivanov's
filling condition is on the union of the orbits of all components of $t$,
and we must check that $a$, $c$ and $f_k(a)$ already fill. This is
consistent with Fathi's theorem for several curves~\cite[Theorem
0.2]{Fathi}: for curves whose $f$-orbits are distinct and fill a closed
surface, it gives a pseudo-Anosov class once \emph{every} exponent has
absolute value at least some $n$, and it gives no window in which the
exceptions must lie. We
know of no earlier written account of this example or of an answer to the
consecutive-integers question; given how short it is, a specialist may well
have known it informally.

\paragraph{What remains open.} The count question (whether at most $N_S$
members $t^jf$ fail to be pseudo-Anosov) is untouched: our family has
exceptional exponents arbitrarily far apart, and we make no claim about how
many there are, nor whether each such family has only finitely many
exceptions. Also untouched are the variant in which the $f$-orbit of
each single $\alpha_i$ fills $S$ (here the $f_k$-orbit of $a$ stays
disjoint from $C'$), closed surfaces, and other surfaces with boundary.
Finally, the base maps $f_k=T_a^kT_d$ are reducible (they fix $C'$). The
theorem therefore answers the question in the orbit-filling form Ivanov
states, where $f$ is arbitrary; it gives no counterexample in which $f$ is
pseudo-Anosov, the setting of Long and Morton, and says nothing about that
version.

\subsection{Ivanov's question in full}\label{sec:question}

Ivanov's list concerns ``a compact orientable surface, possibly with
boundary'', with $\Mod_S=\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(S))$ including
orientation-reversing classes~\cite[p.~1]{Ivanov06}. We quote Problem~5
exactly, from the arXiv version~\cite[pp.~4--5]{Ivanov06} (version~1,
posted August 14, 2006, the only version). He writes $t_\alpha$ for the
left Dehn twist along $\alpha$.

\begin{quote}
``\textbf{5.\ Dehn multitwists.} For a nontrivial circle $\alpha$ on $S$ (i.e.\ a submanifold $\alpha$ of $S$ diffeomorphic to the standard circle $S^1$) let us denote by $t_\alpha$ the (left) Dehn twist along $\alpha$; $t_\alpha\in\Mod_S$. A Dehn multi-twist is any composition of the form $t_{\alpha_1}^{\pm1}\circ t_{\alpha_2}^{\pm1}\circ\cdots\circ t_{\alpha_n}^{\pm1}$ for disjoint circles $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_n$.

\textbf{Question.} Is there a constant $N_S$, depending only on $S$, such that the following holds? Let $f\in\Mod_S$ and let $t=t_{\alpha_1}^{\pm1}\circ t_{\alpha_2}^{\pm1}\circ\cdots\circ t_{\alpha_n}^{\pm1}$ be a Dehn multi-twist. If $A=\{f^m(\alpha_i):1\le i\le n,\ m\in\Z\}$ fills $S$ (i.e.\ for any nontrivial circle $\gamma$ there exists an $\alpha\in A$ such that the geometric intersection number $i(\gamma,\alpha)\ne0$), then only a finite number of elements of the form $t^j\circ f$, $j\in\Z$, are not pseudo-Anosov, and the corresponding $j$ are among some $N_S$ consecutive integers.

By a theorem of A.~Fathi [Fath], if $t$ is a Dehn twist, then this is true for $N_S=7$. This theorem of A.~Fathi was preceded by a theorem of D.~Long and H.~Morton [LM] to the effect that for a Dehn twist $t$, only a finite number of element $t^j\circ f$ are not pseudo-Anosov under the above assumptions, without a uniform bound on the number of exceptions.

A weaker version of this question is still interesting: under the same conditions, is it true that no more than $N_S$ of $t^j\circ f$ are not pseudo-Anosov?''
\end{quote}

\noindent Long and Morton's theorem, as noted above, assumes that $f$ is
pseudo-Anosov, which is stronger than the filling hypothesis in Ivanov's
summary of it. Ivanov goes on to call Fathi's paper ``one of the deepest and the
most underappreciated works in the theory of the mapping class groups'' and
adds: ``The above question may be considered as a test of our
understanding of Fathi's ideas''~\cite[p.~5]{Ivanov06}. The question already
appears, in essentially the same words, as Problem~5 of his earlier
list~\cite{Ivanov94}, which also states the weaker version. That list is
headed ``1994--1998, 2014''; Ivanov writes there that its original version
was written in early 1994 and that updates were added until 1998, marked
as such, and Problem~5 carries no update, but we have not seen the 1994
original. Watanabe
restated it in 2016, describing it as ``a Long's and Penner's like extension on
Fathi's theorem''~\cite[\S1.1]{Watanabe}. Zhang discussed whether the twist can be replaced by a multitwist ``at a
cost of replacing the number 7 by an undetermined but universal number'',
citing Ivanov's manuscript \emph{Mapping class groups} of December 21,
1998, in a preprint first posted August 6, 2007~\cite{Zhang07}.
For a special class of pseudo-Anosov maps (those that become trivial when a
chosen puncture is filled in) and multitwists satisfying a
non-compressibility condition, it claimed that every composition is
pseudo-Anosov, so that no exceptions occur; the author withdrew the
preprint in January 2008. In a later paper~\cite{Zhang11}, Zhang showed
that for any Dehn twist $t_c$ on a punctured surface there are
pseudo-Anosov maps $f_m$ with $f_m\circ t_c^k$ pseudo-Anosov for all $k$
once $m$ is large, and deduced a corollary for multitwists on two curves.
Both works concern pseudo-Anosov base maps and give positive results; our
example is of a different kind. So there are two questions: the
\emph{consecutive-integers} question, which asks for Fathi's conclusion
with $N_S$ in place of $7$, and the weaker \emph{count} question. We answer
the first, for one surface, and say nothing about the second.


\section{Conventions and standard facts}\label{sec:conventions}

Throughout, $S$ is the compact sphere with five boundary components.
Ivanov's $\Mod_S$ is $\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(S))$, so isotopies may move the
boundary. Isotopy classes of curves, the action of mapping classes on them,
geometric intersection numbers and the Nielsen--Thurston types are therefore
those of the five-punctured sphere $S_{0,5}$, and we work there. A simple
closed curve is \emph{essential} if it bounds neither a disk nor a disk with
one puncture; on $S_{0,5}$ this means that each side contains at least two
of the five punctures. This is Ivanov's ``nontrivial'' circle, which he
describes in his Problem~6 as ``not bounding a disk and non deformable into
the boundary''~\cite{Ivanov06}. (With a weaker reading the filling
hypothesis could never hold, since an inessential curve meets nothing.)
Ivanov's $\Mod_S$ includes orientation-reversing classes; our example uses
orientation-preserving ones only.

We write $i(x,y)$ for the geometric intersection number of isotopy classes
and $T_x$ for the left Dehn twist about $x$. We use these standard facts:

\begin{itemize}
\item[(D)] If $i(x,y)=0$ then $T_x(y)=y$ and $T_xT_y=T_yT_x$, since the twists
  have disjoint supports.
\item[(I1)] $i(T_x^m(y),y)=|m|\,i(x,y)^2$ for all $m\in\Z$~\cite[Prop.~3.2]{FM}.
\item[(I2)] $\bigl|\,i(T_x^m(y),z)-|m|\,i(x,y)\,i(x,z)\bigr|\le i(y,z)$
  for all $m\in\Z$~\cite[Prop.~3.4]{FM}.
\item[(N)] $i(h(x),h(y))=i(x,y)$ for every mapping class $h$.
\item[(P)] A pseudo-Anosov class fixes no isotopy class of essential curve
  (Nielsen--Thurston classification~\cite[Thm.~13.2]{FM}; see
  also~\cite{FLP}).
\item[(Q)] A pair of pants (a sphere with three boundary components or
  punctures) contains no essential curve that is not parallel to a boundary
  component or puncture. Indeed, a simple closed curve in it separates it,
  and one of the two sides contains at most one of the three boundary
  components or punctures, so that side is a disk or an annulus.
\end{itemize}

\section{The curves}\label{sec:curves}

Model $S$ as the closed disk $D$ with four punctures $p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4$ on a
horizontal diameter, in this order; the boundary circle $\partial D$ plays
the role of the fifth puncture $p_5$ (collapsing it to a point gives
$S_{0,5}$). For a set $I$ of consecutive indices, the
\emph{round curve} about $I$ is the boundary of a convex neighborhood of
$\{p_i:i\in I\}$ that contains no other puncture. Our five curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:curves}) are
\[
a=\text{round}\{1,2\},\quad d=\text{round}\{2,3\},\quad
c=\text{round}\{3,4\},
\]
\[
C'=\text{round}\{1,2,3\},\quad C=\text{round}\{2,3,4\}.
\]
Each separates the five punctures into sets of sizes $2$ and $3$, so each is
essential; and they induce five different partitions of $\{1,\dots,5\}$, so
they are pairwise non-isotopic. Equivalently, with $p_1,\dots,p_5$ placed at
the corners of a convex pentagon in this cyclic order, $a$, $d$, $c$, $C'$,
$C$ are the boundaries of thin neighborhoods of the sides $p_1p_2$,
$p_2p_3$, $p_3p_4$, $p_4p_5$, $p_5p_1$.

\begin{lemma}[the pentagon pattern]\label{lem:pentagon}
In the cyclic order $(a,d,c,C',C)$, consecutive curves have geometric
intersection number $2$ and non-consecutive curves are disjoint:
\begin{gather*}
i(a,c)=i(a,C')=i(d,C')=i(d,C)=i(c,C)=0,\\
i(a,d)=i(d,c)=i(c,C')=i(C',C)=i(C,a)=2.
\end{gather*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Round curves about runs $I$, $J$ are disjoint when $I\subseteq J$,
$J\subseteq I$ or $I\cap J=\emptyset$: choose nested or disjoint convex
neighborhoods. This gives the five zeros. In each of the other five pairs
the runs overlap partially, and the round curves can be drawn meeting in
two points, as in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}. Each region bounded by one arc
of each curve contains a puncture, so there is no bigon, and by the bigon
criterion~\cite[Prop.~1.7]{FM} the curves are in minimal position, so the
intersection number is $2$.
\end{proof}

Section~\ref{sec:checks} describes two independent exact checks of the
pattern, in the form $i(u,v)=0\iff T_u(v)=v$ (which follows from (D) and
(I1)).

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}\label{sec:proof}

We prove the three items of the theorem in turn and then conclude. Fix
$k\ge1$ and $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$, and put $f=f_k=T_a^kT_d$ and
$t=T_aT_c^{\varepsilon}$.

\paragraph{(1) The multitwist.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}, $a$ and $c$ are
disjoint, and they are not isotopic. Both exponents in $t$ are nonzero, and
for $\varepsilon=1$ the multitwist $t$ is a product of left twists.

\paragraph{(2) The orbits fill.} We first show that an essential curve
$\gamma$ with $i(\gamma,a)=i(\gamma,c)=0$ is isotopic to $a$ or to $c$.
Suppose it is isotopic to neither. Cutting $S$ along the disjoint curves $a$
and $c$ leaves three pairs of pants: one bounded by $a$, $p_1$, $p_2$; one by
$c$, $p_3$, $p_4$; and one by $a$, $c$, $p_5$. Realize $a$, $c$, $\gamma$ by
geodesics of a complete hyperbolic metric; distinct simple closed geodesics
are in minimal position~\cite[\S1.2]{FM}, so $\gamma$ is disjoint from
$a\cup c$ and lies in one of the three pants. By (Q) it is parallel to $a$,
to $c$ or to a puncture, which contradicts our assumption or the
essentiality of $\gamma$.

Next we compute two intersection numbers of $f(a)=T_a^kT_d(a)$. Since
$i(a,a)=i(a,c)=0$, the twist $T_a^k$ fixes $a$ and $c$ by (D). By (N) and
(I1),
\[
i(a,f(a))=i\bigl(T_a^{-k}(a),T_d(a)\bigr)=i(a,T_d(a))=i(d,a)^2=4 .
\]
By (N), $i(c,f(a))=i(T_a^{-k}(c),T_d(a))=i(c,T_d(a))$, and (I2) with
$x=d$, $y=a$, $z=c$, $m=1$ gives
$\bigl|\,i(T_d(a),c)-i(d,a)\,i(d,c)\bigr|\le i(a,c)=0$, so
$i(c,f(a))=2\cdot2=4$.

Now let $\gamma$ be any essential curve. If $\gamma$ is disjoint from both
$a$ and $c$, it is $a$ or $c$, and both meet $f(a)$. So some member of
$\{a,c,f(a)\}$ meets $\gamma$, and this set, which lies in
$A=\{f^m(a),f^m(c):m\in\Z\}$, fills $S$.

\paragraph{(3) Two exceptional members, $k$ apart.} By (D), $T_a$ and $T_c$
commute, so $t^j=T_a^jT_c^{\varepsilon j}$ and
\[
t^jf=T_a^{k+j}\,T_c^{\varepsilon j}\,T_d\qquad(j\in\Z).
\]
For $j=0$ this is $T_a^kT_d$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon},
$i(d,C')=i(a,C')=0$, so $T_d$ and $T_a$ fix $C'$ by (D), and so does
$T_a^kT_d$. For $j=-k$ it is $T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}, $i(d,C)=i(c,C)=0$, so $T_d$ and $T_c$ fix $C$,
and so does $T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$. Since $C$ and $C'$ are essential,
neither member is pseudo-Anosov, by (P).

\paragraph{(4) Conclusion.} For each $k\ge1$ the hypothesis of Ivanov's
question holds for $(f_k,t)$, and the set of $j$ with $t^jf_k$ not
pseudo-Anosov contains $0$ and $-k$. Any run of consecutive integers that
contains both has at least $k+1$ elements. If $N_S$ existed, taking
$k=N_S$ would give a contradiction. So no such $N_S$ exists for the sphere
with five boundary components. \qed

\begin{remark}[conventions]\label{rem:conventions}
With right-handed twists every $T_x$ becomes $T_x^{-1}$. The proof uses
only (D), (I1), (I2) and (N), which hold for both, so it applies verbatim.
Since $f\,t^j=f\,(t^jf)\,f^{-1}$, the members $ft^j$ and $t^jf$ are
conjugate and have the same exceptional $j$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[what the example does not show]
In the family $t^jf_k$ the exceptional set contains $0$ and $-k$; we do not
determine it, so the example bears only on where exceptions lie, not on how
many there are. The base maps $f_k$ are reducible, so the example does not touch the
version of the question in which $f$ is pseudo-Anosov. It also uses a filling hypothesis on the union of the
orbits, as Ivanov states it; for the variant in which each orbit fills on
its own it says nothing, since the $f_k$-orbit of $a$ is disjoint from
$C'$ ($T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and $i(a,C')=0$).
\end{remark}

\section{What is checked by machine}\label{sec:checks}

The proof above is complete as written. Its finite combinatorial core is
also checked by a Lean proof and by two independent programs, in the
folders \file{lean/} and \file{checks/} that accompany this paper. We state what each covers.

\paragraph{The model.} The fundamental group of $S$ (the disk with four
punctures) is free on loops $x_1,\dots,x_4$ around $p_1,\dots,p_4$, with
$x_1x_2x_3x_4$ parallel to $\partial D$. The half twists act by the Artin
automorphisms
\[
\sigma_i:\ x_i\mapsto x_ix_{i+1}x_i^{-1},\quad x_{i+1}\mapsto x_i,\quad
x_m\mapsto x_m\ (m\ne i,i+1),
\]
and the twists about the round curves are the full twists of the enclosed
strands~\cite[Ch.~9]{FM}: $T_a=\sigma_1^2$, $T_d=\sigma_2^2$, $T_c=\sigma_3^2$,
$T_{C'}=(\sigma_1\sigma_2)^3$, $T_C=(\sigma_2\sigma_3)^3$, up to one global
choice of handedness. The curves are the conjugacy classes, up to inversion,
of the words $x_1x_2$, $x_2x_3$, $x_3x_4$, $x_1x_2x_3$, $x_2x_3x_4$. In this
model the theorem rests on five finite facts:
\begin{itemize}
\item[F0.] Each of $T_a,T_d,T_c,T_{C'},T_C$ fixes its own curve.
\item[F1.] The pentagon pattern of Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}: $T_u(v)=v$ for
  the ten ordered disjoint pairs and $T_u(v)\ne v$ for the ten ordered
  meeting pairs.
\item[F2.] $T_aT_c=T_cT_a$.
\item[F3.] For every $k$ and both signs, $T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and
  $t^{-k}f_k=T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$ fixes $C$.
\item[F4.] For every $k$, $T_a(f_k(a))\ne f_k(a)$ and $T_c(f_k(a))\ne f_k(a)$,
  that is, $f_k(a)$ meets $a$ and $c$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Lean.} The file \file{lean/Multitwist.lean} (Lean~4 with Mathlib)
proves F0--F4 for every $k\in\mathbb{N}$ and both signs, as statements about
the Artin automorphisms of the free group $F_4$ and conjugacy in it. For
disjoint pairs it gives explicit conjugators. For meeting pairs it gives a
homomorphism $F_4\to\mathrm{Sym}(3)$ that sends exactly one of $T_u(v)$ and
$v$ to the identity, so $T_u(v)$ is conjugate neither to $v$ nor to
$v^{-1}$. For every $k$, F3 follows by commuting and canceling twists (F2), and F4
by applying the automorphism $T_a^k$, which preserves conjugacy, to the case
$k=0$. The file
\file{lean/Check.lean} restates each fact with its exact type, checks that
exchanging $C$ and $C'$ makes the $j=0$ statement false, and checks which
axioms each statement uses: only the standard \texttt{propext},
\texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound}.

What Lean does \emph{not} check is the surface topology, which is the
written proof above: that the Artin automorphisms are the maps the twists
induce on $\pi_1$; that essential curves correspond to conjugacy classes up
to inversion, so that $T_u(v)=v$ in the model means that the curves are
equal; that $T_u(v)=v$ if and only if $i(u,v)=0$; the exact values
$i=2$ and $i=4$; the pants argument for filling; and that a class fixing an
essential curve is not pseudo-Anosov. Mathlib has no theory of mapping
class groups or isotopy classes of curves, so these steps are not
formalized.

\paragraph{Checker A.} The program \file{checks/check_free_group.py} works in
the same free-group model: it computes the Artin action on reduced words
and compares curves by a canonical form of their cyclic words up to
inversion. It verifies F0--F4 for $k=1,\dots,30$ and both signs, $327$
checks in all. It is exact and uses only the Python standard library.

\paragraph{Checker B.} The program \file{checks/check_dynnikov.py} was
written separately, from the statement of the facts alone and without
seeing checker~A or the Lean file. It uses a different model: curves in the
four-punctured disk are encoded by their Dynnikov coordinates~\cite{Dynnikov}
in $\Z^4$, a bijective encoding of multicurves, and the half twists act by
the piecewise-linear update rules of Hall and Yurttaş~\cite{HY}.
Intersection numbers with round curves are computed exactly by the formula
of Yurttaş and Hall~\cite[Lemma~9]{YH} and, independently, by the twist test
$i(u,v)=0\iff T_u(v)=v$. Before checking F0--F4 it validates the model on
known facts: inverses, the braid relations, triviality of the full twist,
and the identity (I1) on random curves. It verifies F0--F4 for
$k=1,\dots,30$ and both signs, also with every twist reversed, and confirms
the exact values $i(a,f_k(a))=i(c,f_k(a))=4$. It is exact (integers only)
and uses only the Python standard library.

\paragraph{Corruptions.} Both checkers must reject four corrupted inputs:
$C$ replaced by $T_a(C)$; $C'$ replaced by $T_c(C')$; $C$ and $C'$
exchanged; and $c$ replaced by $T_{C'}^{-1}(c)$. Each corruption breaks the
pentagon pattern and the fixed-curve facts, and both checkers reject all
four. The command \texttt{python3 checks/check.py} runs both checkers and
all corruptions in about a second.

The two programs implement one list of facts in two models, so they guard
against errors of computation, not against an error in the topology above;
like the Lean proof, they check the combinatorial core, while the step from
it to the theorem is the written proof.
