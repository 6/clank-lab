\begin{abstract}
Fathi proved that if the orbit of a simple closed curve $\gamma$ under a
mapping class $f$ fills a closed surface, then $T_\gamma^nf$, where
$T_\gamma$ is the Dehn twist about $\gamma$, is pseudo-Anosov for all but at
most seven consecutive integers $n$; for pseudo-Anosov $f$, Long and Morton
had shown that the exceptions are finite in number. In a 2006 problem
list, Ivanov asked whether this extends to a multitwist $t$, a product of twists about disjoint curves: if the
orbits under $f$ of all the curves of $t$ together fill a surface $S$, must
the integers $j$ for which $t^jf$ is not pseudo-Anosov lie among $N_S$
consecutive integers, for a constant $N_S$ depending only on $S$? We show
that no such constant exists on the sphere with five boundary components.
For each $k\ge1$ we give a pseudo-Anosov class $g_k$ such that the fixed
multitwist $t=T_aT_c$ of two left twists satisfies Ivanov's
filling hypothesis, while $tg_k$ and $t^{k+1}g_k$ each fix an essential
curve; reducible bases give the same conclusion. Penner's construction
proves that $g_k$ is pseudo-Anosov, and a pants decomposition proves
filling. The group identities are checked in Lean and the finite
curve calculations by independent programs. The examples settle neither
finiteness nor Ivanov's weaker question bounding the number of exceptions.

This work has not yet been reviewed by experts. The proofs, the Lean formalization, the checking programs and the text were produced by AI models (from OpenAI and Anthropic), working under the author's direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Let $S$ be a compact orientable surface, possibly with boundary, and let
$\Mod_S=\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(S))$ be its mapping class group. A mapping class is \emph{reducible} if it preserves a \emph{multicurve}, a
nonempty set of disjoint, pairwise non-isotopic essential curves
(Section~\ref{sec:conventions}). By the Nielsen--Thurston trichotomy an
orientation-preserving class is \emph{pseudo-Anosov} if it is neither
periodic nor reducible; see~\cite{FM,FLP}.
We write $i(x,y)$ for the geometric intersection number of isotopy classes
and $T_x$ for the left Dehn twist about $x$.

Let $f$ be a mapping class and $\gamma$ a simple closed curve.
Fathi's single-twist theorem~\cite[Theorem~0.1]{Fathi} says that,
on a closed surface, if the $f$-orbit of $\gamma$ fills, then
$T_\gamma^n f$ is pseudo-Anosov except for at most seven consecutive
integers. Ivanov~\cite[Problem~5]{Ivanov06} asked for a
surface-dependent bound for a multitwist. Its definition and question
are as follows, quoted from the arXiv version, Problem~5, pp.~4--5:
\begin{quote}
\emph{5. Dehn multitwists.} For a nontrivial circle $\alpha$ on $S$ (i.e. a submanifold $\alpha$ of $S$ diffeomorphic to the standard circle $S^1$) let us denote by $t_\alpha$ the (left) Dehn twist along $\alpha$; $t_\alpha\in\mathrm{Mod}_S$. A Dehn multi-twist is any composition of the form $t_{\alpha_1}^{\pm1}\circ t_{\alpha_2}^{\pm1}\circ\cdots\circ t_{\alpha_n}^{\pm1}$ for disjoint circles $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_n$.

\emph{Question.} Is there a constant $N_S$, depending only on $S$, such that the following holds? Let $f\in\mathrm{Mod}_S$ and let $t=t_{\alpha_1}^{\pm1}\circ t_{\alpha_2}^{\pm1}\circ\cdots\circ t_{\alpha_n}^{\pm1}$ be a Dehn multi-twist. If $A=\{f^m(\alpha_i):1\le i\le n,\ m\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ fills $S$ (i.e. for any nontrivial circle $\gamma$ there exists an $\alpha\in A$ such that the geometric intersection number $i(\gamma,\alpha)\ne0$), then only a finite number of elements of the form $t^j\circ f$, $j\in\mathbb{Z}$, are not pseudo-Anosov, and the corresponding $j$ are among some $N_S$ consecutive integers.
\end{quote}
We call $f$ the \emph{base}, the classes $t^jf$ the \emph{members} of the
family, and the $j$ with $t^jf$ not pseudo-Anosov its \emph{exceptional
exponents}. A set of curves \emph{fills} $S$ if every essential curve
meets one of them; a multitwist is \emph{positive}
if it is a product of left twists.
Problem~5 also asks for finiteness of the exceptional exponents; a
subsequent weaker question bounds only their number. The earlier list asks
the same consecutive-integers question \cite[Problem~5]{Ivanov94}.
The following examples answer the consecutive-integers question on the
five-holed sphere. The curves in both theorems are constructed in
Section~\ref{sec:curves} and shown in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $S$ be the sphere with five boundary components. There are essential
simple closed curves $a,d,c,C',C$ on $S$ such that for every $k\ge1$ and
each $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$ the mapping class $f_k=T_a^kT_d$ and the
multitwist $t=T_aT_c^{\varepsilon}$ satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $a$ and $c$ are disjoint and not isotopic, so $t$ is a Dehn multitwist
  with two components and nonzero exponents (a product of left twists when
  $\varepsilon=1$);
\item the set $\{f_k^m(a),f_k^m(c):m\in\Z\}$ fills $S$; already
  $\{a,c,f_k(a)\}$ does;
\item $t^0f_k=T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and $t^{-k}f_k=T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$
  fixes $C$, so neither is pseudo-Anosov.
\end{enumerate}
Hence the set of $j$ with $t^jf_k$ not pseudo-Anosov contains $0$ and $-k$,
and no constant $N_S$ as in the consecutive-integers question exists for
this $S$.
\end{theorem}

Composition is right to left, as in Ivanov's $t^j\circ f$: $T_a^kT_d$ applies
$T_d$ first. The theorem holds verbatim for right-handed twists, and for
$f_kt^j$ in place of $t^jf_k$, since the two are conjugate by $f_k$
(Remark~\ref{rem:conventions}).

\begin{remark}[what the example does not show]
In the family $t^jf_k$ the exceptional set contains $0$ and $-k$; we do not
determine it, so the example bears only on where exceptions lie, not on how
many there are. The original bases $f_k$ are reducible. Their $a$-orbits do not fill
individually, since $T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and $i(a,C')=0$.
The next theorem provides different, pseudo-Anosov bases; its filling
proof again uses the union of the orbits of $a$ and $c$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:pa}
On the same five-holed sphere, with the same curves and left-twist
convention, put
\[
 g_k=T_a^{-1}T_c^{-(k+1)}T_d,\qquad t=T_aT_c\qquad(k\ge1).
\]
Then $g_k$ is pseudo-Anosov, and the union
$\{g_k^m(a),g_k^m(c):m\in\Z\}$ fills $S$; already
$\{a,c,g_k(a)\}$ fills. For every $n\in\Z$,
\[
 t^ng_k=T_a^{n-1}T_c^{n-k-1}T_d.
\]
The member at $n=1$ fixes $C$, and the member at $n=k+1$ fixes $C'$.
Consequently the exceptional exponents need an interval of at least
$k+1$ consecutive integers, even with a pseudo-Anosov base and a fixed
positive multitwist. The statement also holds on reversing every twist.
\end{theorem}

The two families coincide up to a shift of exponent. With
$\varepsilon=1$, $t^{-(k+1)}f_k=T_a^{-1}T_c^{-(k+1)}T_d=g_k$, so
$t^ng_k=t^{n-k-1}f_k$, and the members at $n=1$ and $n=k+1$ are those of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} at $j=-k$ and $j=0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:pa} thus
re-bases the same family at a pseudo-Anosov member and checks the filling
hypothesis again, since filling depends on the orbits of the base.

For a single twist, Long and Morton proved finiteness of the exceptions
for a pseudo-Anosov base~\cite[Theorem~A]{LM}. Fathi's hypothesis allows
any base with a filling orbit, as also stated by Cumplido and
Wiest~\cite[Theorem~3]{CW}. On closed connected orientable surfaces of
positive genus, Boyer, Gordon and Zhang improved seven to six, for a
pseudo-Anosov base~\cite[Theorem~1.3]{BGZ} and, more generally, whenever
the orbit of the twisting curve fills~\cite[Theorem~2.6]{BGZ}; see
Watanabe~\cite[\S1.1]{Watanabe}, who restated Ivanov's question. For
several curves whose $f$-orbits are distinct and together fill a closed
surface, Fathi's several-curve theorem~\cite[Theorem~0.2]{Fathi} gives a
pseudo-Anosov class once
every twist exponent has absolute value at least some $n$ depending on
$f$; it locates no window for the exceptions.

Zhang studied pseudo-Anosov bases in a special class: those that become
trivial when a chosen puncture is filled in. A 2007
preprint~\cite[Theorem~2]{Zhang07} claimed that for these bases and
multitwists satisfying a non-compressibility condition every composition
is pseudo-Anosov, so that no exceptions occur. The author withdrew the
preprint in January 2008. A published paper returns to Ivanov's multitwist
question~\cite[pp.~415--416]{Zhang10}: for bases in that class whose associated
axis meets a certain region, every product of twist powers about suitable
disjoint curves with the base is pseudo-Anosov. On a punctured surface of
genus $p$ with $n\ge1$ punctures and $3p+n>4$, Zhang also showed that for
any Dehn twist $t_c$ there are pseudo-Anosov maps $f$ with $f\circ t_c^k$
pseudo-Anosov for all $k$~\cite[Theorem~A and the Remark following
it]{Zhang11}; Theorem~B there gives infinitely many pseudo-Anosov lifts of
multitwists on one or two curves when $3p+n>5$. Zhang's families have no
exceptions at all, so they are consistent with ours.

Watanabe's Theorem~1.3~\cite{Watanabe} gives pseudo-Anosov compositions
$\phi^jf$ for large $|j|$ under hypotheses that include a curve-graph
distance $d_S(a,f(a))>4$; our bases have distance at most $2$ at both
twisting curves (Appendix~\ref{app:watanabe}), so it does not apply.

\noindent\emph{Proof overview.} Since $T_a$ and $T_c$ commute,
$t^jg_k=T_a^{j-1}T_c^{j-k-1}T_d$, so the two twist exponents vanish at
different values of $j$, namely $1$ and $k+1$. At each of these values
only two twists remain, and both fix the curve of Figure~\ref{fig:curves}
disjoint from their curves, so that member is reducible. Because the two
exponents vanish separately, the gap between these members grows with $k$.
The step that needs work is Ivanov's filling hypothesis, which concerns
the union of the orbits of $a$ and $c$. Cutting along $a$ and $c$ leaves
three pairs of pants, so an essential curve disjoint from both is $a$ or
$c$, and filling reduces to two positive intersection numbers,
$i(a,g_k(a))$ and $i(c,g_k(a))$. Penner's
construction~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{Penner} proves that $g_k$ is
pseudo-Anosov. The reducible bases $f_k$ are handled the same way.

The mathematical data and algebraic formalization are archived at
\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23639372}; Section~\ref{sec:checks}
says what is checked by machine and where the checks are described.

\section{Conventions and standard facts}\label{sec:conventions}

Throughout, $S$ is the compact sphere with five boundary components.
Ivanov's $\Mod_S$ is $\pi_0(\mathrm{Diff}(S))$, so isotopies may move the
boundary. Isotopy classes of curves, the action of mapping classes on them,
geometric intersection numbers and the Nielsen--Thurston types are therefore
those of the five-punctured sphere $S_{0,5}$, and we work there. A simple
closed curve is \emph{essential} if it bounds neither a disk nor a disk with
one puncture; on $S_{0,5}$ this means that each side contains at least two
of the five punctures. Following Ivanov (Problem~6 of~\cite{Ivanov06}), a
nontrivial circle means an essential curve.
Ivanov's $\Mod_S$ includes orientation-reversing classes; our example uses
orientation-preserving ones only.

We use these standard facts:

\begin{itemize}
\item[(D)] If $i(x,y)=0$ then $T_x(y)=y$ and $T_xT_y=T_yT_x$, since the twists
  have disjoint supports.
\item[(I1)] $i(T_x^m(y),y)=|m|\,i(x,y)^2$ for all $m\in\Z$~\cite[Prop.~3.2]{FM}.
\item[(I2)] $\bigl|\,i(T_x^m(y),z)-|m|\,i(x,y)\,i(x,z)\bigr|\le i(y,z)$
  for all $m\in\Z$~\cite[Prop.~3.4]{FM}. This is the one-twist
  case of that proposition: its same-sign condition is automatic, and
  $m=0$ is immediate.
\item[(N)] $i(h(x),h(y))=i(x,y)$ for every mapping class $h$.
\item[(P)] A pseudo-Anosov class fixes no isotopy class of essential curve
  (Nielsen--Thurston classification~\cite[Thm.~13.2]{FM}; see
  also~\cite{FLP}).
\item[(Q)] A pair of pants (a sphere with three boundary components or
  punctures) contains no essential curve that is not parallel to a boundary
  component or puncture. Indeed, a simple closed curve in it separates it,
  and one of the two sides contains at most one of the three boundary
  components or punctures, so that side is a disk or an annulus.
\end{itemize}

\section{The curves}\label{sec:curves}

Model $S$ as the closed disk $D$ with four punctures $p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4$ on a
horizontal diameter, in this order; the boundary circle $\partial D$ plays
the role of the fifth puncture $p_5$ (collapsing it to a point gives
$S_{0,5}$). For a set $I$ of consecutive indices, the
\emph{round curve} about $I$ is the boundary of a convex neighborhood of
$\{p_i:i\in I\}$ that contains no other puncture. Our five curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:curves}) are
\[
a=\text{round}\{1,2\},\quad d=\text{round}\{2,3\},\quad
c=\text{round}\{3,4\},
\]
\[
C'=\text{round}\{1,2,3\},\quad C=\text{round}\{2,3,4\}.
\]
Each separates the five punctures into sets of sizes $2$ and $3$, so each is
essential; and they induce five different partitions of $\{1,\dots,5\}$, so
they are pairwise non-isotopic. Equivalently, with $p_1,\dots,p_5$ placed at
the corners of a convex pentagon in this cyclic order, $a$, $d$, $c$, $C'$,
$C$ are the boundaries of thin neighborhoods of the sides $p_1p_2$,
$p_2p_3$, $p_3p_4$, $p_4p_5$, $p_5p_1$.

\begin{lemma}[the pentagon pattern]\label{lem:pentagon}
In the cyclic order $(a,d,c,C',C)$, consecutive curves have geometric
intersection number $2$ and non-consecutive curves are disjoint:
\begin{gather*}
i(a,c)=i(a,C')=i(d,C')=i(d,C)=i(c,C)=0,\\
i(a,d)=i(d,c)=i(c,C')=i(C',C)=i(C,a)=2.
\end{gather*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Round curves about runs $I$, $J$ are disjoint when $I\subseteq J$,
$J\subseteq I$ or $I\cap J=\emptyset$: choose nested or disjoint convex
neighborhoods. This gives the five zeros. In each of the other five pairs
the runs overlap partially, and the round curves can be drawn meeting in
two points, as in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}. In the pentagon description
each such pair consists of thin neighborhoods of two sides sharing a vertex
$p_v$; the union of the two curves has four complementary regions,
containing $p_v$, the other endpoint of the first side, the other endpoint
of the second side, and the remaining two punctures. So no region is an
unpunctured bigon, and by the bigon
criterion~\cite[Prop.~1.7]{FM} the curves are in minimal position, so the
intersection number is $2$.
\end{proof}

\section{The reducible-base examples}\label{sec:proof}

Cancellation gives the exceptional members in Theorem~\ref{thm:main};
a pants decomposition verifies its filling hypothesis.

\noindent\emph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.} We prove its three items in turn and then the conclusion. Fix
$k\ge1$ and $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$, and put $f=f_k=T_a^kT_d$ and
$t=T_aT_c^{\varepsilon}$.

\paragraph{(1) The multitwist.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}, $a$ and $c$ are
disjoint, and they are not isotopic. Both exponents in $t$ are nonzero, and
for $\varepsilon=1$ the multitwist $t$ is a product of left twists.

\paragraph{(2) The orbits fill.} We first show that an essential curve
$\gamma$ with $i(\gamma,a)=i(\gamma,c)=0$ is isotopic to $a$ or to $c$.
Suppose it is isotopic to neither. Cutting $S$ along the disjoint curves $a$
and $c$ leaves three pairs of pants: one bounded by $a$, $p_1$, $p_2$; one by
$c$, $p_3$, $p_4$; and one by $a$, $c$, $p_5$. Realize $a$, $c$, $\gamma$ by
geodesics of a complete hyperbolic metric; distinct simple closed geodesics
are in minimal position~\cite[\S1.2]{FM}, so $\gamma$ is disjoint from
$a\cup c$ and lies in one of the three pants. By (Q) it is parallel to $a$,
to $c$ or to a puncture, which contradicts our assumption or the
essentiality of $\gamma$.

Next we compute two intersection numbers of $f(a)=T_a^kT_d(a)$. Since
$i(a,a)=i(a,c)=0$, the twist $T_a^k$ fixes $a$ and $c$ by (D). By (N) and
(I1),
\[
i(a,f(a))=i\bigl(T_a^{-k}(a),T_d(a)\bigr)=i(a,T_d(a))=i(d,a)^2=4 .
\]
By (N), $i(c,f(a))=i(T_a^{-k}(c),T_d(a))=i(c,T_d(a))$, and (I2) with
$x=d$, $y=a$, $z=c$, $m=1$ gives
$\bigl|\,i(T_d(a),c)-i(d,a)\,i(d,c)\bigr|\le i(a,c)=0$, so
$i(c,f(a))=2\cdot2=4$.

Now let $\gamma$ be any essential curve. If $\gamma$ is disjoint from both
$a$ and $c$, it is $a$ or $c$, and both meet $f(a)$. So some member of
$\{a,c,f(a)\}$ meets $\gamma$, and this set, which lies in
$A=\{f^m(a),f^m(c):m\in\Z\}$, fills $S$.

\paragraph{(3) Two exceptional members, $k$ apart.} By (D), $T_a$ and $T_c$
commute, so $t^j=T_a^jT_c^{\varepsilon j}$ and
\[
t^jf=T_a^{k+j}\,T_c^{\varepsilon j}\,T_d\qquad(j\in\Z).
\]
For $j=0$ this is $T_a^kT_d$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon},
$i(d,C')=i(a,C')=0$, so $T_d$ and $T_a$ fix $C'$ by (D), and so does
$T_a^kT_d$. For $j=-k$ it is $T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}, $i(d,C)=i(c,C)=0$, so $T_d$ and $T_c$ fix $C$,
and so does $T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$. Since $C$ and $C'$ are essential,
neither member is pseudo-Anosov, by (P).

\paragraph{(4) Conclusion.} For each $k\ge1$ the hypothesis of Ivanov's
question holds for $(f_k,t)$, and the set of $j$ with $t^jf_k$ not
pseudo-Anosov contains $0$ and $-k$. Any run of consecutive integers that
contains both has at least $k+1$ elements. If $N_S$ existed, taking
$k=N_S$ would give a contradiction. So no such $N_S$ exists for the sphere
with five boundary components. \qed

\begin{remark}[conventions]\label{rem:conventions}
With right-handed twists every $T_x$ becomes $T_x^{-1}$. The proof uses
(D), (I1), (I2), (N), (P) and Lemma~\ref{lem:pentagon}, which hold for both, so it applies verbatim.
Since $f\,t^j=f\,(t^jf)\,f^{-1}$, the members $ft^j$ and $t^jf$ are
conjugate and have the same exceptional $j$.
\end{remark}

\section{Pseudo-Anosov bases}\label{sec:pa}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pa}]
We use Penner's construction in the following form
\cite[Theorem~3.1, p.~187, and definitions on pp.~180--181]{Penner}.

\smallskip\noindent\emph{Penner's theorem.} Let $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$
be multicurves on an oriented finite-type surface of negative Euler
characteristic. Suppose that no curve of either is parallel to an end,
that $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$ intersect
efficiently (no complementary region of $\mathcal A\cup\mathcal B$ is a disk bounded by one
arc of each, an unpunctured bigon), and that $\mathcal A\cup\mathcal B$ fills: each
complementary region is a disk, or an annulus or punctured disk parallel
to an end. Then every product of positive right twists about curves of
$\mathcal A$ and negative right twists about curves of $\mathcal B$ in which
every curve occurs
is pseudo-Anosov.

\smallskip
We take $\mathcal A=\{a,c\}$ and $\mathcal B=\{d\}$ and check the hypotheses.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] The curves $a$, $c$ are disjoint and non-isotopic, and $d$ is
  isotopic to neither; all three separate the punctures two from three, so
  they are essential and not parallel to an end.
\item[(ii)] Figure~\ref{fig:penner} draws $a$, $d$, $c$ as rectangles
  enclosing the same runs of punctures as in Section~\ref{sec:curves}, so
  they are isotopic to those curves. Viewing the five punctures as marked points on the sphere, the
  complement of their union has six disk faces: four once-punctured
  bigons, a once-punctured outer quadrilateral and one unpunctured
  quadrilateral (Appendix~\ref{app:diagram}). No face is an unpunctured
  bigon, so the intersections are efficient.
\item[(iii)] On the compact surface these faces are five annuli parallel
  to the ends and one disk, so $\mathcal A\cup\mathcal B$ fills in Penner's sense
  \cite[p.~181]{Penner}.
\end{itemize}

rectangles, is the fifth puncture.}
\label{fig:penner}
\end{figure}

Penner uses right twists $\tau_x$, so our left twists satisfy
$T_x=\tau_x^{-1}$. Thus
$g_k=\tau_a\tau_c^{k+1}\tau_d^{-1}$ is in Penner's semigroup with positive
family $\{a,c\}$ and negative family $\{d\}$. Every generator is used,
with multiplicities $1,k+1,1$, and the oriented surface has Euler
characteristic $-3$. Penner's theorem makes $g_k$
pseudo-Anosov. Replacing every twist by its inverse swaps the roles of
$\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$, so the conclusion also holds for the mirror family.

For filling, write $g_k=M_kT_d$ with $M_k=T_a^{-1}T_c^{-(k+1)}$, which
fixes $a$ and $c$ by (D). By (N), $i(a,g_k(a))=i(a,T_d(a))$ and
$i(c,g_k(a))=i(c,T_d(a))$; these are $4$ as in step~(2) of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and the rest of that step applies verbatim, so
$\{a,c,g_k(a)\}$ fills $S$.

Disjoint supports give $T_aT_c=T_cT_a$, so combining integer powers
proves the displayed normal form for every $n\in\Z$. At $n=1$ it is
$T_c^{-k}T_d$, which fixes $C$ since $i(c,C)=i(d,C)=0$.
At $n=k+1$ it is $T_a^kT_d$, which fixes $C'$ since
$i(a,C')=i(d,C')=0$. Both curves are essential, since each separates
two punctures from three. By (P) both members fail to be pseudo-Anosov.
The exponents are $k$ apart, so an interval containing them has at least
$k+1$ integers.
\end{proof}

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:checks}

The programs, their test inputs and the reproduction commands are
described in the checking notes, the files \file{checks/README.md} and
\file{lean/README.md} of the \href{https://github.com/6/clank-lab/tree/multitwist-exceptions-v4/results/multitwist-exceptions}{supplementary material}. The finite checks run in about a second; the Lean
proof builds in a few minutes once its library cache is downloaded.

\paragraph{The model.} The fundamental group of $S$ (the disk with four
punctures) is free on loops $x_1,\dots,x_4$ around $p_1,\dots,p_4$, with
$x_1x_2x_3x_4$ parallel to $\partial D$. Choosing a basepoint and representatives gives the Artin
automorphisms for half twists
\[
\sigma_i:\ x_i\mapsto x_ix_{i+1}x_i^{-1},\quad x_{i+1}\mapsto x_i,\quad
x_m\mapsto x_m\ (m\ne i,i+1),
\]
and the twists about the round curves are the full twists of the enclosed
strands~\cite[Ch.~9]{FM}: $T_a=\sigma_1^2$, $T_d=\sigma_2^2$, $T_c=\sigma_3^2$,
$T_{C'}=(\sigma_1\sigma_2)^3$, $T_C=(\sigma_2\sigma_3)^3$, up to one global
choice of handedness. The canonical fundamental-group action is only defined up to inner
automorphism; this ambiguity disappears on conjugacy classes. The five
curves are represented, up to conjugacy and inversion, by the words $x_1x_2$, $x_2x_3$, $x_3x_4$, $x_1x_2x_3$, $x_2x_3x_4$.

The group identities are formalized in Lean. Their interpretation as
statements about curves uses the standard surface facts of Section~\ref{sec:conventions}. The finite checks are given in Appendix~\ref{app:algebra}.
Here is the correspondence with
Theorem~\ref{thm:pa}. In the following displays, the twist symbols denote
the corresponding Artin automorphisms of $F_4$. Multiplication denotes composition of
automorphisms, with the right factor acting first. The formal definitions
are $t=T_aT_c$ and $g_k=T_a^{-1}T_c^{-(k+1)}T_d$ for
$k\in\mathbb N$, where the inverse symbols denote the specified inverse
Artin maps. Write $w_C=x_2x_3x_4$ and $w_{C'}=x_1x_2x_3$
for the words representing $C,C'$. The formal theorems
\texttt{Multitwist.V2.fixed\_C} and \texttt{Multitwist.V2.fixed\_Cp} state
\[
 ((t^1)g_k)(w_C)=w_C,\qquad
 ((t^{k+1})g_k)(w_{C'})=w_{C'}\quad(k\in\mathbb N).
\]
For Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, the formal statements
\texttt{Multitwist.F3\_j0}, \texttt{F3\_jk\_pos}, and
\texttt{F3\_jk\_neg} are, in the same notation,
\begin{gather*}
 (T_a^kT_d)(w_{C'})=w_{C'},\\
 ((T_a^{-1}T_c^{-1})^k(T_a^kT_d))(w_C)=w_C,\\
 ((T_a^{-1}T_c)^k(T_a^kT_d))(w_C)=w_C\qquad(k\in\mathbb N).
\end{gather*}
The last two equations are the two signs of the member at exponent $-k$.
The formal theorem \texttt{Multitwist.V2.}\allowbreak\texttt{integer\_identity} states,
for every group $G$, elements $A,C,D\in G$ with $AC=CA$, and
$k,n\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
 (AC)^n(A^{-1}C^{-(k+1)}D)=A^{n-1}C^{n-k-1}D.
\]
The concrete fixed-word statements use natural $k$, including zero;
the surface theorems require $k\ge1$. The integer identity supplies
the normal form for all integers $n$. Further formal nonconjugacy
statements show that the words representing $f_k(a)$ and $g_k(a)$ are
moved by the twists about $a$ and $c$, even up to conjugacy and inversion. These are the algebraic counterparts of
the positive intersection numbers used in the filling arguments.
Both choices of $\varepsilon$ occur in the displayed identities for
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}; reversing all twists is justified in
Remark~\ref{rem:conventions} and the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pa}.
In summary, Lean checks the free-group identities above, using only the
standard axioms \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and
\texttt{Quot.sound}. The rest comes from the
literature and Sections~\ref{sec:conventions}--\ref{sec:pa}: the
correspondence between twists and Artin automorphisms, the correspondence
between essential curves and conjugacy classes up to inversion, the
intersection formulas, filling, Penner's theorem and~(P).

\section{Finite checks}\label{app:diagram}\label{app:algebra}

For the complementary regions, Figure~\ref{fig:penner} uses
punctures $p_1=(2,0)$, $p_2=(5,0)$, $p_3=(9,0)$, $p_4=(12,0)$,
$p_5=(20,0)$ and rectangles with coordinate
bounds
\[
 a=[0,6]\times[-2,2],\quad d=[4,10]\times[-4,4],\quad
 c=[8,14]\times[-2,2].
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:penner} uses the plane model of the sphere: the fifth
puncture lies in the exterior face instead of being the disk boundary. An
increasing homeomorphism of the horizontal axis takes the four interior
punctures to those of Section~\ref{sec:curves}, and each rectangle encloses
the same run of punctures as the corresponding round curve. Two
independent implementations trace the crossings and the boundary walk of
each face; neither infers the complementary regions from intersection
numbers alone. Both find four crossings, at $(4,\pm2)$ and $(10,\pm2)$,
eight edges once corners that are not crossings are ignored, and six disk
faces with the puncture contents listed in Section~\ref{sec:pa}.

The algebraic model in Section~\ref{sec:checks} has the following five finite facts:

\begin{itemize}
\item[F0.] Each of $T_a,T_d,T_c,T_{C'},T_C$ fixes its own curve.
\item[F1.] For words $w_v$ representing the five curves, $T_u(w_v)$ is conjugate to $w_v$
  for the ten ordered disjoint pairs, and $T_u(w_v)$ is not conjugate
  to $w_v$ or $w_v^{-1}$ for the ten ordered meeting pairs.
  Nonconjugacy, by the contrapositive of (D), certifies nonzero intersection.
\item[F2.] $T_aT_c=T_cT_a$.
\item[F3.] For every $k$ and both signs, $T_a^kT_d$ fixes $C'$ and
  $t^{-k}f_k=T_c^{-\varepsilon k}T_d$ fixes $C$.
\item[F4.] For every $k$, neither $T_a(f_k(w_a))$ nor $T_c(f_k(w_a))$
  is conjugate to $f_k(w_a)$ or its inverse. Hence the contrapositive of (D) implies
  that $f_k(a)$ meets both $a$ and $c$.
\end{itemize}

The free-group calculation proves these facts for every natural $k$ and
both signs. It also proves the equalities and nonconjugacy statements for
$g_k$, with the formal statements displayed in Section~\ref{sec:checks}.

The independent curve calculation uses Dynnikov coordinates on the
four-punctured disk~\cite{Dynnikov}, the update rules of Hall and
Yurttaş~\cite{HY}, and the intersection formula of Yurttaş and
Hall~\cite[Lemma~9]{YH}. For $k=1,\dots,30$ and both signs it confirms
the pentagon pattern and the exact intersection values
$i(a,f_k(a))=i(c,f_k(a))=4$, and the free-group checker confirms facts
F0--F4 over the same range; the two checkers agree. The
checking notes (Section~\ref{sec:checks}) give the programs and their
test inputs.

\section{Watanabe's distance hypothesis}\label{app:watanabe}

Theorem~1.3 of Watanabe's arXiv version~\cite{Watanabe} gives
pseudo-Anosov compositions $\phi^jf$ whenever $|j|$ exceeds an explicit
constant depending only on $S$, provided $d_S(a,f(a))>4$ and the
nonidentity pure mapping class $\phi$ fixes $a$ and has a loxodromic
domain outside a finite set depending on $f,a$; here $d_S$ is distance in
the curve graph, whose vertices are essential curves and whose edges join
disjoint curves. At our twisting curves $a,c$, both base families have
distance at most $2$. Indeed, $f_k$ fixes $C'$, so $C'$ is disjoint from
both $a$ and $f_k(a)$. For $g_k=UT_d$ with $U=T_a^{-1}T_c^{-(k+1)}$, the
curve $U(C')$ is disjoint from both $a$ and $g_k(a)$, since $U$ fixes $a$
and $C'$ is disjoint from $a,d$. Likewise $T_a^k(C)$ and $U(C)$ are
disjoint from $c,f_k(c)$ and $c,g_k(c)$, respectively, since $C$ is
disjoint from $c,d$. Thus these examples do not satisfy that distance
hypothesis at either twisting curve.
