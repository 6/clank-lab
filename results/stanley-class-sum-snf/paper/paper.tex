\begin{abstract}
We determine the Smith normal form of multiplication by the sum of all
$n$-cycles on the center of $\Z S_n$. For every $n\geq1$, it has
$p(n)-n$ zero entries, where $p(n)$ counts partitions of $n$, and
nonzero factors $d_0=1$ and $d_r=a_r r!/\gcd(n,r)$ for $1\le r<n$,
with $a_r=2$ when $r\equiv n\pmod2$ and $a_r=1$ otherwise.
In 2019 Stanley conjectured these factors when $n$ is an odd prime or
twice an odd prime; the formula proves both conjectures and covers every $n$.
Goupil and Schaeffer gave a character-sum formula for the long-cycle
connection coefficients and turned it into a positive-term formula.
Biane reproduced the character-sum formula and gave a short algebraic
proof of their positive-term formula. Following Biane, we derive the
character-sum formula from Frobenius's formula; Grinberg's exposition
supplies class-sum and representation-theory background. Through hook characters, the
character-sum formula writes the matrix as a product of three matrices. We add the
integral structure: we determine the lattices spanned by the two outer
factors and show that, in a suitable basis, row $r$ of the remaining
product is divisible by $d_r$.
The image lattice and the divisibility $d_r\mid d_{r+1}$ are checked in
Lean from the group-ring definitions, including the character formula.
The diagonal form and the prime cases follow by written arguments.

This work has not yet been reviewed by experts. The proofs, the Lean formalization and the text were produced by AI models (from OpenAI and Anthropic), working under the author's direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Here $S_n$ denotes the symmetric group on $n$ letters.
For a partition $\mu\vdash n$, write $K_\mu$ for the sum of the
permutations of cycle type $\mu$ in $\Z S_n$. The elements $K_\mu$ form
a $\Z$-basis of the center $Z(\Z S_n)$, identifying it with
$\Z^{p(n)}$, where $p(n)$ is the number of partitions of $n$.
We consider the matrix $M_n$ defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix}
 K_{(n)}K_\nu=\sum_{\mu\vdash n}M_n[\mu,\nu]K_\mu.
\end{equation}
Thus the rows record output cycle types and the columns record input cycle
types. The Smith normal form $\SNF(A)$ of an integer matrix $A$ is the
diagonal matrix obtained from $A$ by invertible integral row and column
operations whose positive entries $d_0\mid d_1\mid\cdots$, the
invariant factors, come before its zeros. Equivalently, it describes the
embedding of the column lattice of $A$ in its ambient free abelian group
up to integral changes of basis.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $n\geq1$, and let $p(n)$ denote the number of partitions of $n$.
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d}
 d_0=1,\qquad
 d_r=\frac{a_r r!}{\gcd(n,r)}\quad(1\leq r<n),\qquad
 a_r=\begin{cases}2&r\equiv n\pmod2,\\1&r\not\equiv n\pmod2.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
These are positive integers satisfying $d_r\mid d_{r+1}$ for
$0\leq r<n-1$. There is a basis
\[
 b_0,\ldots,b_{n-1},b'_1,\ldots,b'_{p(n)-n}
\]
of $\Z^{p(n)}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:image}
 M_n\Z^{p(n)}=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n-1}d_r\Z b_r.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
$\SNF(M_n)=\diag(d_0,\ldots,d_{n-1},0,\ldots,0)$,
with $p(n)-n$ zeros.
\end{theorem}

For example, the nonzero factors for $n=4$ are $(1,1,2,6)$.

Lindzey studies the adjacency matrix of the Cayley graph of $S_n$
generated by all $n$-cycles~\cite[opening paragraph]{Lindzey}. That
operator acts on the full group algebra; here multiplication is
restricted to its center. Stanley cites Lindzey's question as
motivation~\cite[opening paragraph]{StanleyQuestion} and asks for the
Smith normal form of multiplication by any class sum, and for other
finite groups~\cite[opening and final paragraphs]{StanleyQuestion}.
For the $n$-cycles, Stanley observed that the rank is
$n$~\cite[paragraph preceding the two specific conjectures]{StanleyQuestion}
and stated two conjectures~\cite[conjectures (a) and (b)]{StanleyQuestion}:
``(a) If $n$ is an odd prime, then the nonzero SNF terms are $k!$ for $k$
even and $2\cdot k!$ for $k$ odd, where as above $0\leq k\leq n-1$.
(b) If $n$ is twice an odd prime, then the nonzero SNF terms are $k!$ for
all $0\leq k\leq n-1$, except that $(n/2)!$ is omitted, and
$(\frac n2-1)!$ appears twice.'' Stanley added: ``Possibly with more data one
could make a conjecture for all $n$''~\cite[paragraph following
conjecture (b)]{StanleyQuestion}. Corollary~\ref{cor:primes} proves (a)
and (b), and Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is the formula for all $n$. One entry
of Stanley's displayed $n=12$ example differs from it;
see Appendix~\ref{sec:examples}.

Goupil and Schaeffer's character-sum formula gives the long-cycle connection
coefficients~\cite[equation (4), as identified in Biane]{GoupilSchaeffer},
and their paper turns it into an expression with only positive
terms~\cite[as described in Biane, p.~1]{GoupilSchaeffer}.
Biane reproduces the character-sum formula~\cite[equations (1)--(2)]{Biane}
and gives a short algebraic proof of Goupil and Schaeffer's positive-term
formula~\cite[pp.~1--3, final expression]{Biane}.
Following Biane, Proposition~\ref{prop:bridge} derives the character-sum
form from Frobenius's formula; the proof uses only this form.
Our contribution is the integral structure of the resulting factorization
of $M_n$, described below.
Stanley~\cite[Lemma 2.1]{StanleyFactorizations} gives the long-cycle
hook values, and Stanley~\cite[Lemma 2.2]{StanleyFactorizations} gives
the hook character values, including the dimensions. Grinberg's exposition~\cite[Definition 3.9.4(c), Theorem 3.9.5 and Corollary 5.17.41]{Grinberg}
supplies the class-sum and representation-theory background used here.
Sagan~\cite[\S4.10]{Sagan} is another reference for the Murnaghan--Nakayama rule.
Related Smith-form results are
\cite[Theorems 5.1--5.2]{StanleySurvey} and
\cite[Theorem 1.2]{CaiStanley}. Both concern a different operator on Young's lattice.

Equation~\eqref{eq:image} and the divisibility $d_r\mid d_{r+1}$ are
checked in Lean from the group-ring definitions of $K_\mu$ and $M_n$,
including Proposition~\ref{prop:bridge} (Section~\ref{sec:lean}). The
standard step from~\eqref{eq:image} to the diagonal form, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:primes}, are written proofs.

The proof is built on one factorization. For $\mu\vdash n$, let
$\eta_\mu\in\Z^n$ list the coefficients of the polynomial
$\prod_j((1+y)^{\mu_j}-1)$. Through hook characters, the character-sum
formula becomes
\[
 M_n[\mu,\nu]=\epsilon_\mu\sum_{i,s=0}^{n-1}\eta_\mu(i)\,
 \frac{Q(i,s)}{e_s}\,\alpha(\nu)_s,
\]
with a sign $\epsilon_\mu=\pm1$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:pairing} and
Section~\ref{sec:assembly}). Here $\alpha(\nu)$ is a rescaling of
$\eta_\nu$, $Q$ is the explicit anti-triangular matrix~\eqref{eq:Q},
and $e_s=s!\gcd(n,s+1)$.
There are three steps. First, the vectors $\alpha(\nu)$ are integral and
span $\Z^n$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:A}). Second, the vectors $\eta_\mu$ span a lattice with an
explicit triangular basis, cut out by a mod-$2$ symmetry
(Lemma~\ref{lem:B}). Third, on the $r$th vector of that basis the
rescaled pairing gives $d_r$ times a row of a unimodular anti-triangular
matrix (Lemma~\ref{lem:C} and Section~\ref{sec:assembly}). A splitting
argument then extends these vectors to a basis, giving the Smith form.
The main difficulty is integral: the first step needs an integrality and
spanning argument, and the second a parity invariant.

\section{The character formula and a change of basis}\label{sec:characters}
Fix $n\geq1$ and put $N=n-1$. For $\mu\vdash n$, let $\ell(\mu)$ be
its number of parts and $m_j(\mu)$ the multiplicity of $j$. Set
\[
 z_\mu=\prod_{j\geq1}j^{m_j(\mu)}m_j(\mu)!,\qquad
 \epsilon_\mu=(-1)^{\ell(\mu)-1}.
\]
The number $z_\mu$ is the order of the centralizer of a permutation of
type $\mu$. Write $\chi_k=\chi^{(n-k,1^k)}$ for the hook character,
where $0\leq k\leq N$. The hook characters are the characters of the
exterior powers $\Lambda^kS$ of the standard representation $S$
(Stanley~\cite[Lemma 2.2]{StanleyFactorizations} gives their values), so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hook}
 \sum_{k=0}^N\chi_k(\mu)q^k
 =\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\mu)}(1-(-q)^{\mu_j})}{1+q}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, on the permutation module $V=\Q^n$, a permutation $\sigma$ of
type $\mu$ has $\sum_k\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda^k\sigma)q^k=\det(1+q\sigma)
=\prod_j(1-(-q)^{\mu_j})$, and $\Lambda^kV=\Lambda^kS\oplus\Lambda^{k-1}S$
gives the factor $1+q$.

For Frobenius's formula and the hook restriction used below, see
Biane~\cite[equations (1)--(2) and the intervening hook restriction]{Biane}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bridge}
For $\mu,\nu\vdash n$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bridge}
 M_n[\mu,\nu]=\frac1{z_\nu}\sum_{k=0}^N
       (-1)^k k!(N-k)!\chi_k(\mu)\chi_k(\nu).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Frobenius's class-multiplication formula gives
\[
 M_n[\mu,\nu]=\frac{|K_{(n)}|\,|K_\nu|}{n!}
 \sum_{\lambda\vdash n}
 \frac{\chi^\lambda((n))\chi^\lambda(\nu)\chi^\lambda(\mu)}{\chi^\lambda(1)}.
\]
All symmetric-group characters here are real, and every conjugacy class
is closed under inversion. By the Murnaghan--Nakayama rule, only hooks
contribute: $\chi_k((n))=(-1)^k$. Their dimensions are
$\binom Nk$, while $|K_{(n)}|=N!$ and $|K_\nu|=n!/z_\nu$.
Substitution gives~\eqref{eq:bridge}.
\end{proof}

To relate the normalization to the factorization literature, let
$c^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}$ count the factorizations of a fixed $n$-cycle as
a product of permutations of types $\mu$ and $\nu$. Biane's formula
\cite[equation (2)]{Biane}, identified there with equation~(4) of
Goupil and Schaeffer~\cite{GoupilSchaeffer}, gives
\[
 c^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{n}{z_\mu z_\nu}
 \sum_{k=0}^N(-1)^k k!(N-k)!\chi_k(\mu)\chi_k(\nu).
\]
Thus $M_n[\mu,\nu]=(z_\mu/n)c^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}$. The entries of $M_n$
are explicit, but its Smith form depends on the integral lattices they
span, which Sections~\ref{sec:alpha}--\ref{sec:assembly} determine.

Brackets denote coefficient extraction. Introduce polynomials
\begin{align*}
 H_\mu(y)&=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\mu)}((1+y)^{\mu_j}-1),&
 \eta_\mu(i)&=[y^{i+1}]H_\mu(y),\quad 0\leq i\leq N,\\
 \Phi_\mu(y)&=\frac{H_\mu(y)}{z_\mu},&
 e_s&=s!\gcd(n,s+1),\quad 0\leq s\leq N.
\end{align*}
The order of $H_\mu$ is $\ell(\mu)$, and its lowest coefficient is
$\prod_j\mu_j$. For $0\leq i,s\leq N$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 Q(i,s)=\begin{cases}
 (-1)^{i+s}\dfrac{(N-i)!(N-s)!}{(N-i-s)!},&i+s\leq N,\\
 0,&i+s>N.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pairing}
For $0\leq i,s\leq N$,
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^N(-1)^k k!(N-k)!
       [x^k](x-1)^i\,[x^k](x-1)^s=Q(i,s).
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:changed}
 M_n[\mu,\nu]=\frac{\epsilon_\mu\epsilon_\nu}{z_\nu}
        \sum_{i,s=0}^N\eta_\mu(i)Q(i,s)\eta_\nu(s).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
The coefficient identity follows by repeated use of Pascal's identity;
the calculation is in Appendix~\ref{app:pairing}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The vectors $\alpha(\nu)$ span $\Z^n$}{The vectors alpha(nu) span Z\^n}}\label{sec:alpha}
Define the rational vector $\alpha(\nu)$ by
\[
 \alpha(\nu)_s=\epsilon_\nu e_s[y^{s+1}]\Phi_\nu
       =\frac{\epsilon_\nu e_s\eta_\nu(s)}{z_\nu}.
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:A}
The vectors $\alpha(\nu)$, for $\nu\vdash n$, belong to $\Z^n$ and
span $\Z^n$ over $\Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first prove integrality. Allow $\mu$ to be any multiset of positive
integers, including the empty multiset, and put
\[
 \phi_j(y)=\frac{(1+y)^j-1}{j},\qquad
 \Phi_\mu=\prod_{j\geq1}\frac{\phi_j^{m_j(\mu)}}{m_j(\mu)!},
 \qquad I_t(\mu)=t![y^t]\Phi_\mu.
\]
For the empty multiset, $\Phi_\varnothing=1$. Removing one part $j$
is denoted by $\mu-j$. Since $\phi_j'=(1+y)^{j-1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:derivative}
 \Phi_\mu'=\sum_{j:m_j(\mu)>0}(1+y)^{j-1}\Phi_{\mu-j}.
\end{equation}
Induction on the sum of the parts proves $I_t(\mu)\in\Z$ for every
$t\geq0$. The constant coefficient is immediate; for $t\geq1$,
\eqref{eq:derivative} expresses $I_t(\mu)$ as a sum of
\[
 \binom{j-1}{i}\frac{(t-1)!}{(t-1-i)!}I_{t-1-i}(\mu-j),
 \qquad 0\leq i\leq\min(j-1,t-1),
\]
each an integer by induction.

When the sum of the parts is $n$, the identity
$j m_j(\mu)\Phi_\mu=((1+y)^j-1)\Phi_{\mu-j}$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler}
 n\Phi_\mu=\sum_{j:m_j(\mu)>0}((1+y)^j-1)\Phi_{\mu-j}.
\end{equation}
For $t\geq1$, multiply the coefficient of $y^t$ by $(t-1)!$.
The right side is a sum of
\[
 \binom ji\frac{(t-1)!}{(t-i)!}I_{t-i}(\mu-j),
 \qquad 1\leq i\leq\min(j,t),
\]
so $nI_t(\mu)/t$ is an integer. B\'ezout's identity for $n$ and $t$
then shows that $\gcd(n,t)I_t(\mu)/t$ is an integer. With $t=s+1$,
\[
 \alpha(\nu)_s=\epsilon_\nu\frac{s!\gcd(n,t)I_t(\nu)}{t!}
 =\epsilon_\nu\frac{\gcd(n,t)I_t(\nu)}{t},
\]
which proves integrality.

For spanning, fix a length $\ell=s+1$. The vectors from partitions
of this length vanish in coordinates below $s$, and their $s$th
coordinates, up to sign, are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leading-alpha}
 \frac{(\ell-1)!\gcd(n,\ell)}{\prod_j m_j(\nu)!}.
\end{equation}
We show that the gcd of these integers is one. If $\ell=n$, the partition
$1^n$ gives one. Suppose $\ell<n$, fix a prime $p$, and write
$v=v_p(\gcd(n,\ell))$, where $v_p$ is the $p$-adic valuation. If $v=0$, the partition
$(n-\ell+1,1^{\ell-1})$ gives $\gcd(n,\ell)$, prime to $p$.
If $v>0$, put $q=p^v$ and $A=(n-\ell)/q+1\geq2$. The partition
$(A^q,1^{\ell-q})$ gives
\[
 \binom{\ell-1}{q-1}\frac{\gcd(n,\ell)}q.
\]
The second factor is prime to $p$. In
$\binom{\ell-1}{q-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{q-1}(\ell-i)/i$,
the valuations of numerator and denominator agree term by term, because
$v_p(i)<v\leq v_p(\ell)$. Thus the first factor is also prime to $p$.
No prime divides every number in~\eqref{eq:leading-alpha}.
B\'ezout therefore gives a combination $f_s$ of the length-$s+1$
vectors with coordinate one at $s$ and zero below it. The vectors
$f_0,\ldots,f_N$ form a unitriangular integral matrix, hence a basis
of $\Z^n$.
\end{proof}

\section{\texorpdfstring{The lattice spanned by the $\eta_\mu$}{The lattice spanned by the eta\_mu}}
Let $U$ be the $\Z$-span of the vectors $\eta_\mu$ in $\Z^n$.
Extend the notation~\eqref{eq:d} by setting $a_0=n$, so that
$d_r=a_rr!/\gcd(n,r)$ also holds at $r=0$, using $\gcd(n,0)=n$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:B}
The lattice $U$ has a basis $u_0,\ldots,u_N$ such that $u_r(i)=0$
for $i<r$ and $u_r(r)=a_r$. Each $u_r$ is an integral combination of
the $\eta_\mu$ with $\ell(\mu)=r+1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take $u_0=\eta_{(n)}$. When $N>0$, take $u_N=\eta_{1^n}$.
For $1\leq r<N$, the two partitions
\[
 (n-r,1^r),\qquad(n-r-1,2,1^{r-1})
\]
have length $r+1$ and leading coefficients $n-r$ and $2(n-r-1)$.
Their gcd is $\gcd(n-r,2)=a_r$, so B\'ezout gives the desired $u_r$.
This constructs independent vectors with the asserted leading terms.

We prove that they span. Identify $v\in\Z^n$ with
$\sum_{i=0}^Nv(i)y^i$. Every $v\in U$ satisfies $n\mid v(0)$.
It also satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parity}
 T_N(v)\equiv v\pmod2,\qquad
 T_m(f)=(1+y)^m f\bigl(y/(1+y)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Indeed, $T_{a+b}(fg)=T_a(f)T_b(g)$ and
\[
 T_m((1+y)^m-1)=(1+2y)^m-(1+y)^m
                  \equiv(1+y)^m-1\pmod2.
\]
Consequently $T_n(H_\mu)\equiv H_\mu\pmod2$.
Since $T_n(H_\mu)=yT_N(H_\mu/y)$, cancellation of $y$ proves
\eqref{eq:parity} on the generators and hence on $U$.

Suppose $v(i)=0$ for $i<r$. At $r=0$ we have $a_0\mid v(0)$.
For $1\leq r<N$, comparing coefficients of $y^{r+1}$ in
\eqref{eq:parity} gives $(N-r)v(r)\equiv0\pmod2$.
When $r\equiv n\pmod2$, the factor $N-r$ is odd, so $a_r=2$
divides $v(r)$. In all other cases, including $r=N$, $a_r=1$.
Subtracting $(v(r)/a_r)u_r$, successively for $r=0,\ldots,N$,
therefore expresses every element of $U$ in the constructed basis.
\end{proof}

\section{Divisibility of the pairing}
For a partition $\mu$ and $0\leq s\leq N$, put
\[
 \Gamma(\mu,s)=\frac1{e_s}\sum_{i=0}^N\eta_\mu(i)Q(i,s).
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:C}
If $\ell(\mu)=r+1$, then $\Gamma(\mu,s)\in d_r\Z$ for every $s$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Rewrite~\eqref{eq:Q} as
\[
 Q(i,s)=(-1)^{i+s}s!(N-s)!\binom{N-i}s.
\]
Thus, for the integer
$X_s=\sum_i(-1)^i\binom{N-i}s\eta_\mu(i)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
 \Gamma(\mu,s)=(-1)^s\frac{(N-s)!}{\gcd(n,s+1)}X_s.
\end{equation}
Terms vanish unless $r\leq i\leq N-s$. If $s>N-r$ the assertion
follows at once. If $s=N-r$, then $X_s=(-1)^r\prod_j\mu_j$ and
\[
 \Gamma(\mu,N-r)=\pm\frac{r!}{\gcd(n,r)}\prod_j\mu_j.
\]
The product is divisible by $a_r$: for $r=0$ it equals $n$;
for $a_r=2$ and $r\geq1$, $r+1$ odd parts would have sum of
parity $r+1$, contrary to $n\equiv r\pmod2$.

It remains to treat $s<N-r$. Put $t=N-s>r$ and $b=\gcd(n,s+1)$.
As $b\mid n-(s+1)=t$, we have
\[
 \frac{t!}{b}=r!R,\qquad
 R=(r+1)(r+2)\cdots(t-1)\frac tb\in\Z.
\]
It suffices to show $a_r\mid\gcd(n,r)R$. This is immediate if
$r=0$, or $a_r=1$, or $n,r$ are even. The remaining case has
$a_r=2$ and $n,r$ odd. Then $b$ is odd. If $t>r+1$, $R$ contains
the even factor $r+1$; if $t=r+1$, its factor $t/b$ is even.
This proves the required divisibility, including integrality.
\end{proof}

\section{The Smith normal form}\label{sec:assembly}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
We apply the three lattice lemmas to compute the image in~\eqref{eq:image}.
Write $\boldsymbol\delta_r$ for the $r$th standard basis vector.
For $0\leq r,s\leq N$, define
\[
 G(r,s)=\frac1{e_s}\sum_i u_r(i)Q(i,s).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:C} and the length restriction in Lemma~\ref{lem:B}
show that $d_r$ divides every entry of row $r$. Moreover $G(r,s)=0$
if $r+s>N$, and
\[
 G(r,N-r)=\frac{a_rQ(r,N-r)}{e_{N-r}}
         =(-1)^N\frac{a_rr!}{\gcd(n,n-r)}=(-1)^Nd_r.
\]
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factorG}
 G=\diag(d_0,\ldots,d_N)V,
\end{equation}
where $V$ is an integral anti-triangular matrix with entries $\pm1$
on the anti-diagonal. Its determinant is $\pm1$, so $V$ is unimodular.

Write $\eta_\mu=\sum_r Y(\mu,r)u_r$ using Lemma~\ref{lem:B}, and
write $u_r=\sum_\mu W(r,\mu)\eta_\mu$ using the construction there.
The matrices $Y$ and $W$ are integral, and independence of the $u_r$
gives $WY=I_n$. Let $E=\diag(\epsilon_\mu)$, let $\Gamma$ be the
$p(n)\times n$ matrix with entries $\Gamma(\mu,s)$, and let $\alpha$ be
the $n\times p(n)$ matrix whose columns are $\alpha(\nu)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:changed}
and the definitions give
\[
 M_n=E\Gamma\alpha=EYG\alpha.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:A}, $\alpha:\Z^{p(n)}\to\Z^n$ is surjective.
Since $V$ is unimodular,~\eqref{eq:factorG} now yields
\[
 M_n\Z^{p(n)}=EY\diag(d_0,\ldots,d_N)\Z^n
             =\bigoplus_{r=0}^N d_r\Z b_r,
 \qquad b_r=EY\boldsymbol\delta_r.
\]
The map $WE$ is a left inverse of $EY$, because $E^2=I$.
Therefore
\[
 \Z^{p(n)}=EY\Z^n\oplus\ker(WE).
\]
The kernel is a free abelian group, being a subgroup of a finitely
generated free abelian group. Choosing its basis extends the $b_r$
to a basis of $\Z^{p(n)}$, proving~\eqref{eq:image}.

For completeness, all $d_r$ are positive integers: $d_0=1$, and for
$r\geq1$ the divisor $\gcd(n,r)$ divides $r!$. We also have
$d_0\mid d_1$ whenever the latter occurs. For $1\leq r<N$,
\[
 \frac{d_{r+1}}{d_r}
 =\frac{a_{r+1}}{a_r}\frac{(r+1)\gcd(n,r)}{\gcd(n,r+1)}.
\]
Since $\gcd(n,r+1)\mid r+1$, only $a_r=2$ could cause a
denominator. In that case $a_{r+1}=1$. If $n,r$ are even,
$\gcd(n,r)$ is even and $\gcd(n,r+1)$ is odd. If $n,r$ are odd,
$r+1$ is even and $\gcd(n,r+1)$ is odd. The ratio is integral
in both cases, establishing the divisor chain.

Finally, choose an integral change of basis $P$ carrying $b_r$ to
the first $n$ standard basis vectors. Then $PM_n$ maps
$\Z^{p(n)}$ onto $\bigoplus_r d_r\Z\boldsymbol\delta_r$. Lift the basis
vectors $d_r\boldsymbol\delta_r$ of this image to the domain. These lifts,
together with a basis of the kernel, form a basis of the domain:
the map onto its free image splits. The corresponding unimodular
column change $C$ satisfies
\[
 PM_nC=\diag(d_0,\ldots,d_{n-1},0,\ldots,0).
\]
The image has rank $n$, so precisely $p(n)-n$ entries vanish.
This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
\end{proof}

\section{The prime cases}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:primes}
If $n=p$ is an odd prime, the nonzero invariant factor at index $r$
is $r!$ for even $r$ and $2r!$ for odd $r$, for $0\leq r<p$.
If $n=2p$ for an odd prime $p$, the list is $0!,1!,\ldots,(2p-1)!$
with $p!$ replaced by $(p-1)!$.
\end{corollary}
Here $2r!$ means $2\cdot r!$. The two assertions are Stanley's
conjectures (a) and (b) quoted in the introduction.
\begin{proof}
For $1\leq r<p$, $\gcd(p,r)=1$, and $r\equiv p\pmod2$
means $r$ is odd. This proves the first assertion, including $r=0$
separately. When $n=2p$, even $r$ with $0<r<2p$ have gcd two
and $a_r=2$. Odd $r\ne p$ have gcd one and $a_r=1$. At $r=p$
the factor is $p!/p=(p-1)!$. Again $d_0=1$.
\end{proof}

The theorem also gives
\[
 Z(\Z S_n)/K_{(n)}Z(\Z S_n)
 \cong\Z^{p(n)-n}\oplus\bigoplus_{r=0}^{n-1}\Z/d_r\Z.
\]

\section{Formal verification}\label{sec:lean}
The theorem \texttt{StanleySnf.stanley\_class\_sum\_snf} has the statement
\[
 \forall n\in\mathbb N,\quad 0<n\Longrightarrow
 \left(\operatorname{HasStanleySNF}(n,M_n)\ \land\
 \forall r\in\mathbb N,\ r+1<n\Longrightarrow d_r\mid d_{r+1}\right).
\]
Here $\operatorname{HasStanleySNF}(n,A)$ means that there is a finite
index set $\kappa$ and an integral basis of the integer-valued functions
on partitions of $n$, indexed by $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\sqcup\kappa$,
for which the range of multiplication of column vectors by $A$ is the
span of $d_r b_r$. Independence makes this the direct sum in
\eqref{eq:image}. The factors are exactly~\eqref{eq:d}: in Lean, $d_r$ is
defined by integer division, and the proof shows that the division is
exact and that $d_r>0$.

The formal permutations act on $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ and include fixed
points in their cycle types. Class sums contain each permutation of the
prescribed type once. The formal coefficient definition uses a chosen
representative of the output type; proved conjugacy and class-sum
identities give exactly~\eqref{eq:matrix}, with the same row and column
conventions. In Lean, $\chi_k(\mu)$ is defined as the coefficient of
$q^k$ on the right side of~\eqref{eq:hook}, so the formal statement
of~\eqref{eq:bridge} is an identity about these coefficients and needs
neither the Murnaghan--Nakayama rule nor the identification of hooks with
irreducible characters. The formal proof of~\eqref{eq:bridge} takes a different route from the
proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:bridge}, which uses Frobenius's formula.
It works in the exterior algebra of $\Q^n$ and shows that the
$k$th coefficient is the trace of a permutation on the degree-$k$ part
with the trivial summand removed. A commutant argument then gives
$\binom{n-1}{k}\sum_{g\in S_n}\chi_k(agbg^{-1})=n!\,\chi_k(a)\chi_k(b)$,
the indicator function of the $n$-cycles is
$\frac1n\sum_k(-1)^k\chi_k$, and an orbit--stabilizer count
gives~\eqref{eq:bridge}. The lattice argument then uses that proved
identity. The theorem's only hypothesis is $n>0$.
The diagonal equivalence, the $p(n)-n$ zero entries, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:primes} are the written deductions above.

The Lean project accompanying this paper uses Lean 4.32.2 and Mathlib
v4.32.2; its README gives the build commands. The default build checks
the exact type of the theorem above and that it depends only on the
axioms \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and
\texttt{Quot.sound}, so no \texttt{sorry} remains. After Mathlib's
cache is downloaded, the build takes about a minute and about 7~GB of
memory. The checking notes there give the logical dependencies.
The Lean files are unchanged since
\href{https://github.com/6/clank-lab/tree/stanley-class-sum-snf-v4/results/stanley-class-sum-snf}{version 4}
of this paper.

\section{The coefficient identity}\label{app:pairing}
We give the routine coefficient calculation for Lemma~\ref{lem:pairing}.
\begin{proof}
The coefficient $[x^k](x-1)^i$ is $(-1)^{i-k}\binom ik$.
Using
$k!\binom ik(N-k)!=i!(N-i)!\binom{N-k}{N-i}$,
the sum in the lemma becomes
\[
 (-1)^{i+s}i!(N-i)!
 \sum_{k=0}^s(-1)^k\binom sk\binom{N-k}{N-i}.
\]
Pascal's identity, applied successively $s$ times, gives
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^s(-1)^k\binom sk\binom{N-k}{m}
       =\binom{N-s}{m-s},
\]
where an out-of-range lower index means zero. Take $m=N-i$ and
simplify factorials to obtain~\eqref{eq:Q}.
Now set $g_\mu(x)=\prod_j(1-x^{\mu_j})/(1-x)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:hook} implies $\chi_k(\mu)=(-1)^k[x^k]g_\mu(x)$,
and substitution gives
\[
 g_\mu(1+y)=\epsilon_\mu H_\mu(y)/y
            =\epsilon_\mu\sum_i\eta_\mu(i)y^i.
\]
Apply the first assertion to~\eqref{eq:bridge}.
\end{proof}

\section{Examples}\label{sec:examples}
For $n=1$, the matrix is $[1]$. The nonzero factors for $n=2,3,4,5$
are respectively
\[
 (1,1),\quad(1,2,2),\quad(1,1,2,6),\quad(1,2,2,12,24).
\]
The formula at $n=9$ gives
\[
 0!,\ 2\cdot1!,\ 2!,\ \tfrac23\,3!,\ 4!,\ 2\cdot5!,\
 \tfrac13\,6!,\ 2\cdot7!,\ 8!,
\]
written, as in Stanley's question, as $r!$ times a rational number and
agreeing term by term with Stanley's example~\cite{StanleyQuestion}.
At $n=12$, it gives
\[
 0!,\ 1!,\ 2!,\ \tfrac13\,3!,\ \tfrac12\,4!,\ 5!,\
 \tfrac13\,6!,\ 7!,\ \tfrac12\,8!,\ \tfrac13\,9!,\ 10!,\ 11!.
\]
Stanley's displayed $n=12$ list~\cite{StanleyQuestion}, revision 2, has
$2\cdot6!$ at index six, which does not divide the next entry $7!$; the
formula gives $6!/3$, and all other entries agree. These examples illustrate
the formula and are not inputs to its proof.
