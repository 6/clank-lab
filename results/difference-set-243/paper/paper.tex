\begin{abstract}
A $(243,121,60)$ difference set in $C_3\times C_9\times C_9$ would be a
121-element subset whose ordered differences represent every nonzero
element exactly 60 times; we prove that none exists. Vera L\'opez and
Garc\'ia S\'anchez recorded this case as unresolved, and Gordon singled
it out in a presentation on the La Jolla Difference Set Repository.
Keita Baba proved this first, by a SAT computation with a certificate
accepted by a formally verified checker; we give an independent proof
by a different method. It builds on the fixed-translate theorem of
McFarland and Rice, the multiplier and character methods of Hall, Menon
and Turyn, and the multiplier searches of Vera L\'opez and Garc\'ia
S\'anchez. Using the multiplier 61, we reduce any such set, up to
symmetry, to eight explicit patterns of coset sizes; an exhaustive
search shows that none extends to a difference set. Lean proves the
whole reduction; the formal nonexistence theorem assumes only the
search results, which independent programs establish outside Lean.

This work has not yet been reviewed by experts. The proofs, the Lean formalization, the search code, the certificate checkers and the text were produced by AI models (from OpenAI and Anthropic), working under the author's direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Write $C_m=\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$ additively. For a subset $P$ of an
abelian group, put
\[
 d_P(g)=\bigl|\{(a,b)\in P\times P:a-b=g\}\bigr|.
\]
A $(v,k,\lambda)$ difference set in a group of order $v$ is a subset
$D$ with $|D|=k$ and $d_D(g)=\lambda$ for every $g\ne0$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There is no subset $D\subseteq C_3\times C_9\times C_9$ such that
$|D|=121$ and
\[
 \bigl|\{(a,b)\in D\times D:a-b=g\}\bigr|=60
 \qquad\text{for every }g\ne0.
\]
\end{theorem}

Vera L\'opez and Garc\'ia S\'anchez record these parameters in this
group as unresolved in their Table~1, p.~105 of~\cite{VeraGarcia1997}, with
the question-mark convention of p.~101. Gordon singles out this case
in~\cite[slide~38]{Gordon2019}; the repository's archived version~1.3
also lists $\operatorname{DS}(243,121,60,[3,9,9])$ as
Open~\cite{GordonData}. The neighboring abelian groups and the relevant
published restrictions are reviewed in Appendix~\ref{sec:context}.

Keita Baba proved Theorem~\ref{thm:main} first, on October~4, 2026, by
encoding the orbit structure of multiplier~61 as a SAT instance whose
refutation was accepted by a formally verified proof
checker~\cite[Steps~1--5 and~8(a)]{Baba2026}; Appendix~\ref{sec:context}
describes that proof in more detail. The present paper gives a second,
independent proof. It shares no code or encoding with Baba's. Its
reduction of the problem to eight explicit cases is a short written
argument, and every step of that reduction, including the multiplier
theorem, is proved in Lean from the definitions. Only the final
exhaustive search lies outside Lean, so the two proofs rest on different
computations and different software.

A translate fixed by the multiplier still has many possible unions of
orbits; the difficult step is to reduce these possibilities far enough
that all lifts can be tested. Section~\ref{sec:multiplier} obtains a
fixed translate and the coset moment identities, and
Section~\ref{sec:symmetry} gives the affine maps that preserve
differences and fixedness. Section~\ref{sec:singletons} shows that the
fixed translate meets the subgroup of elements of order dividing three in
thirteen points, and a finite classification shows that all such
thirteen-point sets lie in one orbit under the affine maps.
Section~\ref{sec:quotient} recovers the intersection numbers with the
cosets of $3G$ from sums over hyperplanes and reduces them to eight
cases. Section~\ref{sec:lifting} lifts each case by choosing orbits of
length three in its cosets, pruning a partial choice whenever an ordered
difference count exceeds 60. The finite classifications are computations
carried out inside Lean; the lifting search is an external computation.
Section~\ref{sec:formal} states exactly what each part establishes.

The work uses the fixed-translate theorem of McFarland and
Rice~\cite{McFarlandRice1978}, the classical multiplier and character
methods developed by Hall~\cite{Hall1956}, Menon
(see~\cite[Theorem~5]{Miller2025}), Turyn~\cite{Turyn1965}, and others,
and the systematic multiplier searches of Vera L\'opez and Garc\'ia
S\'anchez. It also benefits from the later nonexistence and
field-descent theory of Arasu, Ma, Leung, Schmidt, and
Miller~\cite{ArasuMa2001,LeungMaSchmidt2004,LeungSchmidt2005,Miller2025}.
These contributions supply both the historical question and the
algebraic framework for reducing it to a finite computation.

\section{Multiplier fixedness and quotient moments}\label{sec:multiplier}

The multiplier theorem supplies the symmetry used throughout the proof;
the moment identities then restrict the intersection numbers with cosets.

Use coordinates $G=C_3\times C_9\times C_9$ and put
\[
\begin{split}
 H&=G[3]=\{(x,3u,3w):x,u,w\in C_3\},\\
 K&=3G=\{(0,3u,3w):u,w\in C_3\}.
\end{split}
\]
Thus $|H|=27$, $|K|=9$, and
$Q=G/K\cong C_3^3$.

Set
\[
 v=243=3^5,\qquad k=121,\qquad\lambda=60,\qquad
 n=k-\lambda=61,\qquad\exp(G)=9.
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fixed}
If $D$ is a difference set as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, then a
translate $D_0$ satisfies $61D_0=D_0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
This is the theorem of McFarland and Rice~\cite{McFarlandRice1978} that
every abelian difference set has a translate fixed by all numerical
multipliers, specialized to this group; for completeness we give the
short argument here.
The first multiplier theorem applies because $G$ is abelian,
$n=61$ is prime, $61>\lambda=60$, and $\gcd(61,243)=1$.
It gives $61D=D+g$ for some $g\in G$. This is also the
$n_1=61$ specialization of Menon's multiplier theorem in
Miller~\cite[Theorem~5]{Miller2025}; see the classical character
and multiplier theory in Turyn~\cite{Turyn1965}.
Put $s=\sum_{d\in D}d$. Summing the set identity gives
$60s=121g$. Multiplication by $121$ has inverse multiplication
by~7 on $G$, since $121\cdot7\equiv1\pmod9$.
For $u=7s$ we therefore have $60u=g$, whence
\[
 61(D-u)=D+g-61u=D-u.
\]
Take $D_0=D-u$.
\end{proof}

In what follows, a subset $P\subseteq G$ is fixed, or scalar-fixed, if
$61P=P$. The scalar orbits are the orbits of $p\mapsto61p$; a singleton
is a scalar orbit with one point and a triple is one with three points.
Multiplication by $61$, equivalently by $7$ modulo $9$, has order
three and fixed subgroup exactly $H$, so there are 27 singletons and 72
triples, and every fixed subset is a union of scalar orbits. Since
$61\equiv1\pmod3$, multiplication by 61 fixes the image of every point
in $G/K=Q$, so every $K$-coset is a union of scalar orbits.
The initial translating element $u$ can be anywhere in $G$.
Subsequent normalizing translations will lie in $H$.
A group automorphism commutes with scalar
multiplication, and translation by $t\in H$ preserves fixedness
because $60t=0$.

A difference set has no nonzero translation period: if $D+h=D$,
then $d_D(h)=121$, contrary to $60$ when $h\ne0$.
It follows that the fixed translates of a fixed difference set
$D_0$ are exactly $D_0+H$: fixedness of $D_0+t$ implies that
$60t$ is a period, hence $60t=0$, whose solution set in $G$ is $H$.

For later use, let $U\leq G$ and
$r_U(a+U)=|D\cap(a+U)|$. For $b\in G$, the number of ordered pairs in
$D\times D$ whose difference lies in $b+U$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:autocorr}
 \sum_{a+U}r_U(a+U)\,r_U(a+b+U)=
 \begin{cases}121+60(|U|-1),&b\in U,\\60|U|,&b\notin U,\end{cases}
\end{equation}
since 121 pairs have difference zero and every nonzero element is the
difference of 60 pairs. Together with $|D|=121$, the case $b\in U$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment}
 \sum_{a+U}r_U(a+U)=121,\qquad
 \sum_{a+U}r_U(a+U)^2=121+60(|U|-1).
\end{equation}
For an index-three subgroup, the three coset counts consequently
satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triples}
 \nu_0+\nu_1+\nu_2=121,\qquad
 \nu_0^2+\nu_1^2+\nu_2^2=4921,\qquad0\leq \nu_i\leq81.
\end{equation}
The complete integer solutions are the six permutations of
$(36,40,45)$.

\section{Affine normalization}\label{sec:symmetry}

The normalizations must preserve both differences and fixedness under
the multiplier. We give the coordinate maps and show why they do so.

Use $F=A+t$ with $t=(t_0,3t_1,3t_2)\in H$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine}
\begin{split}
 F(x,y,z)=\bigl(&\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z+t_0\pmod3,\\
              &3\delta_1x+b_{11}y+b_{12}z+3t_1\pmod9,\\
              &3\delta_2x+b_{21}y+b_{22}z+3t_2\pmod9\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha\ne0\pmod3$ and the matrix $B=(b_{ij})$ is invertible
modulo three. The parameters $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta_j,t_j$ are
taken modulo three; the entries of $B$ have chosen lifts modulo nine.
We call the maps \eqref{eq:affine} legal: their linear parts are
automorphisms, as shown next, and their translations lie in $H$.

The coordinate orders make $A$ additive. Its induced map on
$G/3G$ has block upper-triangular matrix with diagonal blocks
$\alpha$ and $B\pmod3$, so is invertible. If $A(p)=0$, it follows
that $p\in3G=K$. On $K$, the map is again given by
$B\pmod3$, which is invertible; hence $p=0$.
Thus $A$ is an automorphism. Translation preserves differences,
and $60t=0$ gives $F(61p)=61F(p)$. Consequently $F$ preserves
the difference-set property and scalar fixedness.

The restriction of a legal map to $H$ does not depend on $\beta$ and
$\gamma$, since $\beta y$ and $\gamma z$ vanish modulo three on $H$.
Writing the last two coordinates of a point of $H$ as a vector
$\boldsymbol u\in C_3^2$, these restrictions are
\[
 (x,\boldsymbol u)\longmapsto
 (\alpha x+t_0,B\boldsymbol u+\boldsymbol\delta x+\boldsymbol t),
\]
with $B$ reduced modulo three, $\boldsymbol\delta=(\delta_1,\delta_2)$
and $\boldsymbol t=(t_1,t_2)$.
They form a group of order
$2\cdot3\cdot48\cdot9\cdot9=23{,}328$; the factors count
$\alpha,t_0,B,\boldsymbol\delta,\boldsymbol t$, respectively.
Each of these maps on $H$ extends to a legal map on $G$.

\section{The thirteen singleton points}\label{sec:singletons}

The fixed points of the multiplier form $H$. We determine how a fixed
difference set can meet $H$, then classify the resulting subsets under
the legal maps.

Assume from now on that $D_0$ is fixed, and put $S=D_0\cap H$.
Let $\bar y,\bar z$ denote $y,z$ modulo three, so that
$(x,y,z)\mapsto(\bar y,\bar z)$ identifies $G/H$ with $C_3^2$.
Each of the four nonzero linear forms $\ell$ on $C_3^2$, taken up to
sign, gives a homomorphism $p\mapsto\ell(\bar y,\bar z)$ from $G$ onto
$C_3$, whose kernel $U_\ell\supseteq H$ has index three. By
\eqref{eq:triples}, the three cosets of $U_\ell$ meet $D_0$ in 36, 40
and 45 points in some order. The two cosets outside $U_\ell$ contain no
point of $H$ and are unions of $K$-cosets, so they are unions of
triples and their counts are divisible by three. Of $36,40,45$, only
$40$ is not divisible by three; thus $|D_0\cap U_\ell|=40$.
Every point of $H$ lies in all four kernels, and every point
outside $H$ lies in exactly one. Counting incidences gives
\[
 4\cdot40=4|S|+(121-|S|),\qquad\text{hence}\quad|S|=13.
\]

Let $\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2$ be the sizes of $S$ in the three $x$-layers of $H$.
They satisfy $\mu_0+\mu_1+\mu_2=13$ and $0\leq\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2\leq9$.
Call a $K$-coset a cell, indexed by its image in $Q$; the three
special cells, indexed by $(x,0,0)$, make up $H$. They contain only
singleton points and meet $D_0$ in $\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2$ points. Every other
cell contains three triples, so its multiplicity lies
in $\{0,3,6,9\}$. The $U=K$ instance of
\eqref{eq:moment} gives
\[
 \mu_0^2+\mu_1^2+\mu_2^2\equiv601\equiv7\pmod9.
\]
Applied to the subgroup $\{x=0\}$, \eqref{eq:triples} forces the
residues of $(\mu_0,\mu_1,\mu_2)$ modulo three to be a permutation of $(1,0,0)$.
The complete list of layer triples satisfying these conditions
consists of the permutations of $(4,0,9)$ and $(4,3,6)$.

For any $h\in H\setminus\{0\}$, the scalar action permutes the
60 ordered pairs in $D_0^2$ of difference $h$. Its fixed pairs
have both endpoints in $S$; all remaining pair orbits have
length three. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:congruence}
 |S\cap(S+h)|\equiv0\pmod3
 \qquad(h\in H\setminus\{0\}).
\end{equation}
A $(4,0,9)$ layer pattern has intersection four with its
translate by $(1,0,0)$, so cannot satisfy \eqref{eq:congruence}.

The layer conditions above describe exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maskcount}
 6\left[\binom94+\binom94\binom93\binom96\right]
 =5{,}335{,}092
\end{equation}
distinct subsets of $H$: the factor six orders the three distinct layer
sizes, and the binomial factors choose the points in each layer of nine
points. Exhaustive enumeration shows that exactly 7,776 of these
subsets satisfy all 26 congruences \eqref{eq:congruence}, and that they
form exactly the orbit of the set $S_0$ of Appendix~\ref{app:finite}
under the group of order 23,328 of Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}. The layer
conditions are a hand-checkable restriction that the classification
does not need: among all $\binom{27}{13}=20{,}058{,}300$ thirteen-point
subsets of $H$, the congruences alone leave the same 7,776, and this is
the form of the classification proved in Lean. Thus every hypothetical
difference set has a fixed legal image with $H$-part exactly $S_0$.

The orbit of $S_0$ has 7,776 elements, so its stabilizer in the group
of order 23,328 has order three. Since the three layer sizes of $S_0$
are distinct, each stabilizing map has $\alpha=1$ and $t_0=0$. Its
three elements have
$B\equiv I$, $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
and $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&2\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
modulo three, the last two with $\boldsymbol\delta=(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$,
respectively, and $\boldsymbol t=0$. A legal map extending one of them
acts on $Q$ by
\[
 (x,\bar y,\bar z)\longmapsto\bigl(x+\beta\bar y+\gamma\bar z,\,
 B(\bar y,\bar z)\bigr),
\]
which depends only on $\beta,\gamma$ and $B$ modulo three. Since the
three matrices are distinct, these maps induce 27 distinct permutations
of $Q$, forming a group of order~27.

\section{Quotient multiplicities}\label{sec:quotient}

For the normalized fixed image $D_0$, with $H$-part $S_0$, define
$r(q)=|D_0\cap(q+K)|$ for $q\in Q$. Its special entries
$r(x,0,0)$ are $(4,3,6)$; each of the other 24
entries is one of $0,3,6,9$.

The key point is that sums of $r$ over hyperplanes determine $r$.
Choose one representative of each of the 13 nonzero linear forms
$L:Q\to C_3$ up to sign, by requiring its first nonzero
coefficient to be~1, and put
\[
 \nu_L(j)=\sum_{L(q)=j}r(q).
\]
If $L$ ignores $x$, its zero fiber contains all thirteen
singleton points; otherwise it contains the four points of
the $x=0$ singleton layer. Thus $\nu_L(0)\equiv1\pmod3$ in
both cases. Equation~\eqref{eq:triples} forces
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lines}
 \nu_L(0)=40,\qquad \{\nu_L(1),\nu_L(2)\}=\{36,45\}.
\end{equation}

For $p,q\in Q$, exactly thirteen of the forms have
$L(p)=L(q)$ when $p=q$, and exactly four when $p\ne q$.
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inversion}
 \sum_L\nu_L(L(q))=13r(q)+4(121-r(q))=484+9r(q).
\end{equation}
The coefficient of $r(q)$ is nine, so the hyperplane sums $\nu_L$
determine $r$, and every possible array solves \eqref{eq:inversion}
for one of the $2^{13}=8192$ orderings of $(36,45)$ in
\eqref{eq:lines}. Enumerating these orderings and keeping the solutions with integer entries between zero and nine,
special entries $(4,3,6)$ and nonspecial entries divisible by three,
gives exactly 144 arrays. Each has twelve nonspecial entries
equal to three and twelve equal to six, and each satisfies
\eqref{eq:autocorr} for $U=K$, with value 601 at $b\in K$ and 540
elsewhere.

The group of order 27 from Section~\ref{sec:singletons} partitions
these arrays into eight orbits, of sizes 27, 27, 9, 9, 27, 27, 9, 9.
Their representatives are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cases}
(Appendix~\ref{app:finite}). For each array an explicit legal map
fixing $S_0$ sends it to its representative.

\section{Lifting and the proof of nonexistence}\label{sec:lifting}

For each quotient representative, selecting multiplier orbits in the
$K$-cosets gives a ternary tree. We first prove that these choices cover
all possible sets and then give the rejection rule for its search.

Fix a case in Table~\ref{tab:cases}. Each of its 24
nonspecial $K$-cosets has nine points, partitioned into
three scalar triples. A multiplicity-three cell must
select exactly one triple, and a multiplicity-six cell
exactly two. In either case there are exactly three
choices, namely all members of the appropriate
$\binom31$ or $\binom32$ family.

Number the triples of each cell $0,1,2$ and order the 24 nonspecial
cells as in Appendix~\ref{app:computation}. A digit $j$ in a
multiplicity-three cell selects triple $j$; in a multiplicity-six cell
it omits triple $j$. Denote the resulting set for a case $c$ and digit
word $\boldsymbol d\in\{0,1,2\}^{24}$ by $\mathcal L(c,\boldsymbol d)$,
and call it a leaf of case $c$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lifting}
For a fixed case, the map $\boldsymbol d\mapsto
\mathcal L(c,\boldsymbol d)$ is a bijection onto all
scalar-fixed subsets with singleton part $S_0$ and that
quotient multiplicity array. Every such set has 121 points.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The union of $S_0$ and the chosen cell options has size
$13+12\cdot6+12\cdot3=121$, is scalar-fixed, and has
the prescribed multiplicities. Conversely, any fixed
set with those multiplicities intersects each cell
in an invariant subset of the required size, hence
in one of the three stated options. This proves
surjectivity. Intersecting the union with each of
the disjoint cells recovers its option and unique
digit, proving injectivity.
\end{proof}

For each of the eight representatives, a full choice is a word of
length~24 in three digits. Its first twelve digits determine a subtree,
whose completions are all possible twelve-digit tails. Every full word
has exactly one such prefix, so the subtrees partition the eight
lifting families. Appendix~\ref{app:computation} records their numerical
indexing and the intervals $I_\rho$ used to collect consecutive prefixes.

If $P\subseteq P'$, then $d_P(g)\leq d_{P'}(g)$
for every $g$. Thus a partial set for which some
nonzero difference count exceeds 60 has no
difference-set extension. At a leaf,
\[
 \sum_{g\ne0}d_P(g)=121\cdot120=242\cdot60.
\]
Consequently all counts being at most 60 is equivalent
to every count being exactly 60.

For every prefix, a depth-first search tries all three digits at each
remaining position, unless this rejection rule applies. In each of the
eight cases it finds no leaf that is a difference set.
Section~\ref{sec:formal} describes how this exhaustion is checked.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Suppose $D$ were such a difference set.
Lemma~\ref{lem:fixed} gives a fixed translate $D_0$.
By the classification of Section~\ref{sec:singletons}, a legal map
sends $D_0$ to a fixed difference set with $H$-part $S_0$.
By \eqref{eq:inversion}, its quotient array is one of the 144 arrays of
Section~\ref{sec:quotient}, and a legal map fixing $S_0$ sends that
array to a representative in Table~\ref{tab:cases}. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:lifting}, the resulting difference set is a leaf of that
case. Its prefix lies in exactly one interval \eqref{eq:ranges}. The
exhaustion of that interval rejects every leaf below it by tests that
reject no difference set, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:formal}

The computation excludes every leaf, while the formal proof shows that
every hypothetical difference set, after a translation and a legal map,
is one of these leaves. The steps divide as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Proved in Lean from the definitions: the multiplier theorem and
Lemma~\ref{lem:fixed}; the instances of \eqref{eq:moment} used here and
the solutions of \eqref{eq:triples}; $|S|=13$ and the congruences
\eqref{eq:congruence}; the classification of
Section~\ref{sec:singletons}; the reduction to the eight arrays of
Table~\ref{tab:cases} through \eqref{eq:inversion};
the part of Lemma~\ref{lem:lifting} used in the proof, that every such
set is a leaf, and the coverage statement \eqref{eq:coverage};
the partition into the intervals \eqref{eq:ranges}; and soundness of the
rejection rule. The finite classifications are computations checked
inside Lean.
\item Assumed by the Lean theorem as hypotheses: the 1,038 statements
$\operatorname{RangeClear}(\rho)$, $\rho<1038$, each saying that no leaf whose
prefix lies in $I_\rho$ is a difference set (precise form
\eqref{eq:clear} in Appendix~\ref{app:computation}).
\item Established only by programs outside Lean: these 1,038
statements, by the searches of Section~\ref{sec:lifting}, and the
agreement of the cell options used by the search programs with the
leaves defined in Lean, checked outside Lean by comparing every option
with independently written definitions. Independent programs also recompute the finite
classifications of the first item.
\end{enumerate}

The Lean group is the product of the residue groups of moduli $3,9,9$,
and its predicate $\texttt{IsDiffSet}(D)$ says exactly that $|D|=121$
and that the ordered-difference count is 60 at every nonzero element.
The theorem \texttt{DS243.no\_difference\_set} states
\[
 \left(\forall \rho<1038,\
   \operatorname{RangeClear}(\rho)\right)
 \ \Longrightarrow\
 \neg\exists D\subseteq G,\ \operatorname{IsDiffSet}(D),
\]
whose conclusion is exactly Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. In the Lean source
each hypothesis is written as a value of a structure whose only field is
$\operatorname{RangeClear}(\rho)$, labeled by the digest of range~$\rho$; the
label has no logical content, so the two forms are equivalent. The
formal proofs use only the axioms \texttt{propext},
\texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound}.

The lifting exhaustion is an uncertified computation: it produces no
certificate that a proof checker or Lean verifies, and its correctness
rests on the agreement of independent programs. Two search programs,
which store the difference counts in different ways, and a separately
written third program each exhaust all 1,038 intervals. For every
interval they agree on the number of prefixes, the number of search
nodes (partial choices of the tail that pass the rejection rule,
counting the empty tail), the number of difference-set leaves, which is zero, and the
SHA-256 digest of the list of these per-prefix counts. Each complete
pass visits 108,733,222,341 nodes; a replay of all three passes takes
hundreds of CPU-hours.

The README and checks folder of the
\href{https://github.com/6/clank-lab/tree/difference-set-243-v5/results/difference-set-243}{source bundle}
give the commands that build the Lean development and replay the
searches, and its checking notes, \texttt{checks/edition-notes.md},
identify the exact formal statement types and axioms. The contributors
to Lean, mathlib, and the declared library dependencies are acknowledged
for the formal-development infrastructure.

\section{Prior proof and known nonexistence criteria}\label{sec:context}

Baba's proof uses multiplier~61, its 99 orbits, and a SAT encoding over
all 49 cyclic quotients with 64,940 variables~\cite[Steps~1--5 and~8(a); VERIFY, Section~5]{Baba2026}.
The CaDiCaL refutation was accepted by cake\_lpr, a formally verified
checker, and by lrat-check. A second route uses the 864 possible images
in $G/3G$: one representative has a checked certificate, and all 864
instances were also solved separately, without individual
certificates~\cite[Steps~6--8 and VERIFY, Sections~3--4]{Baba2026}.
Baba describes the work as written with AI assistance from
Anthropic~\cite[repository README, Caveats]{Baba2026}.

The 1997 table credits the negative entry for $C_{243}$ to

Arasu and
Ma~\cite{ArasuMa2001} subsequently resolved $C_9\times C_{27}$.
For $C_3^5$, Paley's quadratic-residue construction~\cite{Paley1933}
in the additive group of $\mathbb F_{243}$ gives the stated parameters.
The 1997 table cites Emma Lehmer~\cite{Lehmer1953} for its positive entry.
The nonexistence at issue here concerns
the group $C_3\times C_9\times C_9$, settled first by Baba~\cite{Baba2026}.

The parameter identity $k(k-1)=\lambda(v-1)$ holds. The odd-order
Bruck--Ryser--Chowla equation $61x^2-60y^2=z^2$ has the nonzero
solution $x=y=z=1$. These elementary necessary conditions do not
exclude the parameters.

Turyn's self-conjugacy criterion~\cite{Turyn1965} does not exclude
these parameters: at the nontrivial character orders $3$ and $9$, the
prime $61$ is not self-conjugate, since its powers are $1$ modulo $3$
and cycle through $7,4,1$ modulo $9$, never $-1$. Obstructions requiring
a common prime divisor of $n$ and
$v$ have no such prime here; in particular that ingredient of the
Lander-type restrictions in Leung--Ma--Schmidt~\cite{LeungMaSchmidt2004}
is absent.

Write $F_{\mathrm{LS}}$ for the field-descent function $F$ of Leung and
Schmidt~\cite[Definition~2.6]{LeungSchmidt2005}. One obtains
$F_{\mathrm{LS}}(9,61)=3$. Here $\operatorname{ord}_3(61)=1$ and
$61\not\equiv1\pmod9$, so the exponent $b_3=1$ is permitted in
that definition. Taking their factor $A$ to be the entire group and
the complementary factor trivial, Theorem~4.3 gives
\[
 \exp(G)\leq243\sqrt{\frac{3^2}{4\cdot61\cdot\varphi(3)}}
 =\frac{729}{\sqrt{488}}\approx33.0003,
\]
which permits exponent~9. Their Theorem~A~\cite[Theorem~A]{LeungSchmidt2005} cannot instead split off a
$C_9$ factor: its coprimality condition would require
$\gcd(9,\exp(C_3\times C_9))=1$, whereas this gcd is~9.
Theorem~4.1~\cite[Theorem~4.1]{LeungSchmidt2005} requires a cyclic Sylow subgroup, also absent here.

Arasu and Ma's 2001 nonexistence result~\cite{ArasuMa2001} resolves
$C_9\times C_{27}$. The relevant theorem, as explicitly restated and
extended by Miller~\cite[Theorems~1 and~3]{Miller2025}, assumes
\[
 G=C_{p^e}\times J,\qquad e\geq2,\qquad
 \exp(\operatorname{Syl}_p J)<p^e.
\]
The displayed group hypothesis is from Miller's Theorem~1; Miller's
Theorem~3 gives the subsequent multiplier bound. Splitting off $C_9$ in the present group leaves another $C_9$, so
the strict exponent inequality fails. The forbidden-multiplier
congruences would demand both $\sigma\equiv4\pmod9$ and
$\sigma\equiv1\pmod9$. Although the older numerical condition
$4(p-1)n=488>243$ holds, it cannot repair the failed group
hypothesis.

Chen, Xiang, and Sehgal~\cite{ChenXiangSehgal1994} study skew Hadamard
abelian difference sets, which satisfy the additional partition
$G=\{0\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}D\mathbin{\dot\cup}(-D)$.
The present parameters do not supply this hypothesis. Consequently
a skew-only exponent bound cannot be applied to the ordinary
difference-set question.

Recent work on signed difference sets by He and
Wu~\cite{HeWu2026} includes ordinary difference sets but does not
yield this exclusion by the criteria considered here. Their
Theorem~3.2 has the same failed self-conjugacy test for~61;
Theorem~4.2 requires even $v$; Theorem~4.4 requires $k=v-1$;
and Theorem~4.7 requires an elementary abelian group and a bound on
the defect $v-k$, whereas this group is not elementary abelian and the
defect is $v-k=122>5$.

\subsection{A coset invariant}\label{sec:invariant}

The following invariant is not used in the proof; it gives a hand check
on the 144 arrays. For a fixed translate $D_0$, let
\[
 m(v)=|D_0\cap(v+H)|\qquad(v\in G/H\cong C_3^2).
\]
We have $m(0)=13$. The zero fiber for each of the four
directions on $G/H$ has count~40, so
\[
 m(v)+m(-v)=27\qquad(v\ne0).
\]
After singleton normalization, $m(v)=\sum_xr(x,v)$.
The complete quotient classification gives
$m(v)\in\{9,12,15,18\}$ for $v\ne0$.
Legal normalizations induce an invertible linear map on
$G/H$, so these statements transfer back to the original
fixed translate. Each opposite pair is consequently
$\{9,18\}$ or $\{12,15\}$.

The $U=H$ moment identity is
\[
 \sum_vm(v)^2=121+60\cdot26=1681.
\]
If $j$ of the four opposite pairs are $\{9,18\}$, then
\[
 1681-13^2=j(9^2+18^2)+(4-j)(12^2+15^2)
 =1476+36j.
\]
Thus $j=1$: the eight off-$H$ cosets have multiplicities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hcosets}
 9,18,12,12,12,15,15,15,
\end{equation}
with one opposite pair $\{9,18\}$ and three
$\{12,15\}$. All 144 arrays satisfy this invariant.

\section{Search data and indexing}\label{app:computation}\label{sec:search}

Points and quotient cells are ordered lexicographically by their
least nonnegative coordinate representatives. The triples of a cell are
numbered $0,1,2$ by their least encoded point. The 24 nonspecial cells
are ordered with the twelve multiplicity-six cells first, in increasing
$Q$-index, followed by the twelve multiplicity-three cells in increasing
$Q$-index.

Split each 24-digit word after twelve digits. With
leading zeroes retained, give its first half the
base-three rank, most significant digit first,
\[
 p=\sum_{j=0}^{11}d_j3^{11-j},\qquad
 \operatorname{id}=c\,3^{12}+p.
\]
The complete ID domain has size
\[
 N=8\cdot3^{12}=4{,}251{,}528,
\]
and is partitioned into 1,038 intervals
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ranges}
 I_\rho=[4096\rho,\min(4096(\rho+1),N)),\qquad0\leq \rho<1038.
\end{equation}
Each ID specifies the full cylinder of all $3^{12}$
possible tails. Different prefixes define disjoint
cylinders and every full digit word has exactly one
prefix, so these cylinders partition all eight trees.

In $\operatorname{digits}(\operatorname{id},\boldsymbol\tau)$, the
first twelve entries and the tail are concatenated.
Here the first twelve digits
are the leading-zero-preserving
base-three expansion of
$\operatorname{id}\bmod531441$
and the last twelve are
$\boldsymbol\tau$.

The formal coverage implication says: if $D$ is a
difference set, then there
exist $u\in G$, a word $w$
of legal affine maps, an
integer $\rho<1038$, an
$\operatorname{id}\in I_\rho$,
and a twelve-digit tail
$\boldsymbol\tau$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coverage}
 F_w(D-u)=\mathcal L\left(
 \left\lfloor\frac{\operatorname{id}}{531441}\right\rfloor,
 \operatorname{digits}(\operatorname{id},\boldsymbol\tau)
 \right).
\end{equation}
The map
$F_w$ is an additive
automorphism followed by a
translation in $H$.
For an interval $I_\rho$,
define the statement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clear}
\begin{split}
 \operatorname{RangeClear}(\rho):\quad
 &\text{for every }\operatorname{id}\in I_\rho
 \text{ and every twelve-digit tail }\boldsymbol\tau,\\
 &\mathcal L\left(
 \left\lfloor\frac{\operatorname{id}}{531441}\right\rfloor,
 \operatorname{digits}(\operatorname{id},\boldsymbol\tau)
 \right)\text{ is not a difference set.}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The formal nonexistence result
is conditional on the family
\[
 \forall \rho<1038,\qquad
 \operatorname{RangeClear}(\rho).
\]

The recorded exhaustive searches have the following totals.
\[
\begin{array}{c|r}
\text{depth-twelve prefixes}&4{,}251{,}528\\
\text{search nodes}&108{,}733{,}222{,}341\\
\text{difference-set leaves}&0.
\end{array}
\]
